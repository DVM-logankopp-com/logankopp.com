Care for pet rats and mice

Rats and mice are small, and they decline quickly once they stop eating or start working to breathe. Dr. Kopp is an exotic companion mammal specialist. He sees pet, fancy, and hairless rats, and pet and fancy mice, at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch. Visits include primary care and cases sent by other veterinarians.

Hamsters and gerbils are seen too. Their care is on the other companion mammals page. The plan for any of these species is individual. Evidence for many treatments is limited, so the options are discussed with you first. A broader home guide is at health problems in pet rats, mice, hamsters, and gerbils.

Respiratory disease

Sneezing, noisy breathing, porphyrin staining around the nose, or a rat that breathes harder after exercise is a reason to call. Chronic respiratory disease in rats is often tied to Mycoplasma, and it can flare into pneumonia. Open-mouth breathing, blue or pale gums, or a rat that is too tired to eat needs a call the same day, while the hospital is open. Mice get respiratory infections and chronic respiratory disease as well. Care can include oxygen and other respiratory support when the hospital can provide it that day.

  • Chronic respiratory disease in rats, including Mycoplasma-associated disease
  • Pneumonia and respiratory distress
  • Respiratory infections and chronic respiratory disease in mice
  • Respiratory supportive care when indicated

Cancer care and masses

A lump on a rat is often a mammary tumor. It may sit far from a nipple. Mammary fibroadenomas and mammary carcinomas are both seen. Many are removed surgically, and the surgery is easier while the lump is small. Pituitary tumors can show up as a head tilt, weakness, or trouble holding food. Mice are seen for mammary tumors and other masses. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating these tumors. Selected chemotherapy is considered when it is appropriate. Not every chemotherapy drug is used at the hospital. Radiation therapy is arranged through a referral facility when it is indicated. Read mammary tumors in pet rats.

  • Mammary fibroadenoma and mammary carcinoma in rats
  • Pituitary tumors and other cancer in rats
  • Mammary tumors and other masses in mice
  • Cytology or biopsy when it changes the plan
  • Surgical mass removal, including rat mammary tumor removal
  • Selected chemotherapy when appropriate, palliative care, and cancer pain control

Neurologic and musculoskeletal disease

A head tilt, a seizure, or hind limb weakness in a rat can be a pituitary tumor, an inner ear problem, trauma, or degenerative disease. Call if the rat cannot eat, falls, or seems painful. Obesity and osteoarthritis are common in older rats and are part of geriatric visits. Mice are seen for neurologic disease as well. Pain control is planned for what the animal can still do at home.

  • Head tilt, seizures, and other neurologic disease
  • Hind limb weakness and pituitary tumors in rats
  • Degenerative musculoskeletal disease and osteoarthritis
  • Neurologic disease in mice
  • Obesity

Skin, dental, gut, urinary, and reproductive disease

Abscesses, mites, lice, barbering, and other skin disease are common. In mice, ulcerative dermatitis and fight wounds are common too. Overgrown incisors need trimming or another dental plan under anesthesia when the teeth are diseased. Gastrointestinal and urinary problems are worked up when the signs fit. Spay and neuter are available. An early spay is one option families discuss when they want to lower the chance of mammary tumors in rats. Lumps can still appear later, so keep checking.

  • Skin abscesses, mites, lice, barbering, and other skin disease
  • Ulcerative dermatitis, ectoparasites, and fight wounds in mice
  • Pododermatitis
  • Dental disease and malocclusion
  • Gastrointestinal disease and urinary disease
  • Reproductive disease, spay, and neuter
  • Nutrition counseling

Surgery, anesthesia, and pain management

Rats are commonly seen for mammary tumor removal. Selected mass removal and reproductive surgery are also performed in mice, with anesthesia planned for a very small patient. Monitoring, warmth, and pain control are part of every procedure. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. Chronic pain and palliative care are part of geriatric medicine. Complex bone surgery is arranged with a referral facility when it is indicated. See chronic pain in older small pets and preparing for surgery.

  • Surgical mass removal and mammary tumor surgery
  • Spay, neuter, and other reproductive surgery
  • Selected mass removal in mice
  • Anesthesia for rats and for small mouse patients
  • Pain control, chronic pain management, and palliative care
  • Geriatric medicine

Emergency and critical care

A rat or mouse that is open-mouth breathing, collapsed, bleeding, or unable to eat needs a call before you drive. Emergency care depends on hours and staffing. The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Stabilization can include oxygen, warmth, fluids, and pain control when the team can take the case that day.

  • Respiratory distress and respiratory supportive care
  • Collapse, trauma, and bleeding
  • Anorexia and dehydration
  • Emergency stabilization when hours and staffing allow it

What a visit is like

Bring the rat or mouse in a secure carrier with a familiar hide. A recent weight, a photo of the lump if there is one, and a note about breathing at home are useful. Handling stays quiet. A patient that is working to breathe is seen as soon as the day allows.

When to call

Call while the hospital is open if your rat or mouse:

  • Is breathing harder, sneezing, or breathing with the mouth open
  • Has a new lump, or a lump that is growing
  • Has a head tilt, seizures, or hind limb weakness
  • Has stopped eating, or is bleeding or badly injured
  • Has sores, fight wounds, or incisors that are overgrown

The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Saturday and Sunday are closed. Whether an urgent patient can be seen the same day depends on hours and staffing. After hours, exotic companion mammal consults go through Veterinary Emergency Group in Franklin, Murfreesboro, and West Nashville. Do not give human pain medicine.

Questions people ask

Is there a rat veterinarian in Nashville?

Yes. Dr. Logan Kopp sees pet rats and mice at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital, 2445 Morris Gentry Blvd, Nashville, TN 37013, in Antioch. Call (615) 361-4646. He is Nashville's rat and mouse specialist.

What if my rat is breathing hard?

Call the same day while the hospital is open. Noisy breathing, sneezing, or open-mouth breathing can be chronic respiratory disease, pneumonia, or another emergency. Whether the hospital can see the rat that day depends on hours and staffing.

What if my rat has a lump?

Mammary fibroadenomas and mammary carcinomas are common in rats. Many lumps can be removed. Bring the rat in while the lump is still small. Call Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital at (615) 361-4646.

Do you see mice as well as rats?

Yes. Pet and fancy mice are seen for respiratory disease, skin disease, mammary tumors, dental malocclusion, and selected surgery. Hamsters and gerbils are described on the other companion mammals page.
