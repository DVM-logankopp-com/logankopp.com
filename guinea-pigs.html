Care for pet guinea pigs

Guinea pigs hide illness until they are quite sick. A pig that is eating less, losing weight, drooling, or making a wet chin needs to be seen while the hospital is open. Dr. Kopp sees guinea pigs of all ages, including hairless and long-haired breeds, at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch. He is an exotic companion mammal specialist, and he sees both primary care and referral cases.

The plan is chosen for the individual guinea pig. Evidence for many treatments in this species is limited, so the options are discussed with you before treatment starts. Do not give a leftover antibiotic from another pet. Some of those drugs are unsafe in guinea pigs.

Dental disease and gastrointestinal disease

Cheek teeth are hard to judge in an awake guinea pig. Drooling, a wet chin, dropping food, or a pig that still takes pellets and leaves the hay is a reason to call. Weight loss can be dental disease, gut slowdown, or not enough vitamin C, and those problems often travel together. An anesthetized oral exam is often required. Dental radiographs are obtained when they are available for the case, and CT is arranged through a referral facility when the roots need more detail. Read dental disease and vitamin C.

  • Acquired dental disease, cheek tooth elongation, and malocclusion
  • Dental abscesses and dental-related anorexia
  • Hypomotility, gastrointestinal stasis, and cecal disease
  • Diarrhea, bloat, and gastrointestinal obstruction
  • Chronic weight loss
  • Vitamin C deficiency and other nutritional disease
  • Assisted feeding and nutrition plans
  • Dental procedures, including treatment of diseased cheek teeth when indicated

Urinary disease

Squeaking while urinating, blood in the urine, straining, or a guinea pig that stops eating can mean stones or infection. A pig that cannot pass urine needs a call the same day. Imaging is used to look for bladder and urethral stones. Surgery is considered when a stone needs to come out. There is a family guide to bladder stones in guinea pigs.

  • Bladder stones and urethral stones
  • Cystitis and urinary tract infection
  • Kidney disease
  • Urinalysis, culture, and imaging
  • Surgery for stones when indicated

Reproductive disease and spay and neuter

Ovarian cysts are common. Families often notice flank hair loss, crusty nipples, or a change in behavior. Uterine disease, including tumors, and reproductive infection are also seen. A sow in labor that is not progressing needs a call while the hospital is open. Spay and neuter are planned as guinea pig procedures, with pain control and support for eating afterward. Surgery for ovarian or uterine disease is considered for selected patients. See ovarian cysts and spay.

  • Ovarian cysts and cyst-associated hair loss
  • Uterine disease and uterine neoplasia
  • Reproductive infection and dystocia
  • Spay and neuter
  • Surgery for selected reproductive disease

Skin, respiratory disease, and infection

Itch, hair loss, scabs, or lumps under the jaw can be mites, lice, ringworm, or an abscess, including cervical lymphadenitis. Sneezing, noisy breathing, or labored breathing needs a call, especially if the guinea pig has also stopped eating. Pneumonia can move quickly in this species.

  • Respiratory infection and pneumonia
  • Dermatophytosis, mites, and lice
  • Skin abscesses and cervical lymphadenitis
  • Pododermatitis
  • Otitis and eye disease

Emergency and critical care

A guinea pig that is collapsed, breathing hard, bloated, or unable to urinate needs a call before you drive. Emergency care depends on hours and staffing. The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Stabilization can include fluids, warmth, oxygen, pain control, and assisted feeding, and surgery when the case needs an operation and the team can do it that day.

  • Triage and stabilization
  • Bloat, anorexia, and gastrointestinal emergencies
  • Urinary obstruction
  • Respiratory distress and oxygen when indicated
  • Fluid support, temperature support, and pain control
  • Emergency surgery when hours and staffing allow it

Soft-tissue surgery and cancer care

Surgery includes spay and neuter, bladder stones, selected reproductive disease, abscesses, and mass removal. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating guinea pig tumors, including mammary tumors, lymphoma, and other soft-tissue masses. A lump is sampled or removed when that is the right next step. Selected chemotherapy is considered when it is appropriate. Not every chemotherapy drug is used at the hospital. Radiation therapy is arranged through a referral facility when it is indicated. Complex bone surgery is referred when the guinea pig needs it.

  • Spay, neuter, and surgery for selected reproductive disease
  • Cystotomy and surgery for urinary stones when indicated
  • Abscess surgery, mass removal, and biopsy
  • Mammary tumors, lymphoma, and other soft-tissue masses
  • Wound closure and selected reconstructive closure
  • Selected chemotherapy when appropriate, and palliative care

Anesthesia and pain management

Guinea pigs are prey animals. Pain often looks like sitting still and eating less. Anesthesia for dental work and surgery is planned around warmth, monitoring, and a return to food as soon as it is safe. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. Osteoarthritis and other chronic pain in older guinea pigs are part of geriatric visits. Recognizing pain and preparing for surgery are written for families.

  • Anesthetic planning for dental procedures and surgery
  • Sedation for imaging and oral exams
  • Monitoring during anesthesia, including ECG, oxygen saturation, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature
  • Multimodal pain control and local blocks when they fit the procedure
  • Osteoarthritis, postoperative pain, and chronic pain
  • Geriatric care and palliative pain care

What a visit is like

Bring the guinea pig in a secure carrier with a familiar hide and a little of the hay they already eat. At the hospital, handling stays quiet. A pig that has not eaten since yesterday should be mentioned first when you call, so the team can plan the day around how sick the animal is.

When to call

Call while the hospital is open if your guinea pig:

  • Has eaten less hay, lost weight, or has a wet chin or drool
  • Is squeaking while urinating, straining, or has blood in the urine
  • Is bloated, breathing hard, or collapsed
  • Has flank hair loss, a mammary lump, or swellings under the jaw
  • Is in labor and not producing pups

The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Saturday and Sunday are closed. Whether an urgent guinea pig can be seen the same day depends on hours and staffing. After hours, exotic companion mammal consults go through Veterinary Emergency Group in Franklin, Murfreesboro, and West Nashville. Do not give human pain medicine.
