We explain the choices before we start, including when the science is still catching up. Do not give a leftover antibiotic from another pet. Some of those drugs are unsafe in guinea pigs.

Guinea pig dental disease and GI stasis

Cheek teeth are hard to judge in an awake guinea pig. Drooling, a wet chin, dropping food, or a pig that still takes pellets and leaves the hay is a reason to call. Weight loss can be dental disease, gut slowdown, or not enough vitamin C, and those problems often travel together. An anesthetized oral exam is often required. Skull and dental radiographs are taken in the hospital for dental evaluation. CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists when the roots need more detail, and MRI is arranged through a referral facility. Read guinea pig dental disease and vitamin C.

  • Acquired dental disease, cheek tooth elongation, and malocclusion
  • Dental abscesses and dental-related anorexia
  • Hypomotility, gastrointestinal stasis, and cecal disease
  • Diarrhea, bloat, and gastrointestinal obstruction
  • Chronic weight loss
  • Vitamin C deficiency and other nutritional disease
  • Assisted feeding and nutrition plans
  • Dental procedures, including treatment of diseased cheek teeth when indicated

Guinea pig bladder stones and urinary disease

Squeaking while urinating, blood in the urine, straining, or a guinea pig that stops eating can mean stones or infection. A pig that cannot pass urine needs a call while the hospital is open. Always call first at (615) 361-4646. Imaging is used to look for bladder and urethral stones. Surgery is considered when a stone needs to come out. There is a family guide to bladder stones in guinea pigs.

  • Bladder stones and urethral stones
  • Cystitis and urinary tract infection
  • Kidney disease
  • Urinalysis, culture, and imaging
  • Surgery for stones when indicated

Guinea pig ovarian cysts, spay, and neuter

Ovarian cysts are common. Families often notice flank hair loss, crusty nipples, or a change in behavior. Uterine disease, including tumors, and reproductive infection are also seen. A sow in labor that is not progressing needs a call while the hospital is open. Spay and neuter are planned as guinea pig procedures, with pain control and support for eating afterward. Surgery for ovarian or uterine disease is considered for selected patients. See guinea pig ovarian cysts and spay.

  • Ovarian cysts and cyst-associated hair loss
  • Uterine disease and uterine neoplasia
  • Reproductive infection and dystocia
  • Spay and neuter
  • Surgery for selected reproductive disease

Guinea pig skin, respiratory disease, and infection

Itch, hair loss, scabs, or lumps under the jaw can be mites, lice, ringworm, or an abscess, including cervical lymphadenitis. Sneezing, noisy breathing, or labored breathing needs a call, especially if the guinea pig has also stopped eating. Pneumonia can move quickly in this species.

  • Respiratory infection and pneumonia
  • Dermatophytosis, mites, and lice
  • Skin abscesses and cervical lymphadenitis
  • Pododermatitis
  • Otitis and eye disease

Guinea pig surgery and cancer care

Surgery includes spay and neuter, bladder stones, selected reproductive disease, abscesses, and mass removal. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating guinea pig tumors, including mammary tumors, lymphoma, and other soft-tissue masses. A lump is sampled or removed when that is the right next step. When cancer is diagnosed, we discuss all options, including surgery, chemotherapy, and comfort care, and build a plan around your pet and your family's goals. Radiation therapy is arranged through a referral facility when it is indicated. Some fractures can be repaired in partnership with board-certified veterinary surgeons (DACVS). Others are referred.

  • Spay, neuter, and surgery for selected reproductive disease
  • Cystotomy and surgery for urinary stones when indicated
  • Abscess surgery, mass removal, and biopsy
  • Mammary tumors, lymphoma, and other soft-tissue masses
  • Wound closure and selected reconstructive closure
  • Comfort care

Guinea pig anesthesia and pain management

Guinea pigs are prey animals. Pain often looks like sitting still and eating less. The hospital provides high-quality anesthesia, with dedicated anesthesia technicians and full monitoring (ECG, SpO2, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature), plus warmth and a return to food as soon as it is safe. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. Osteoarthritis and other chronic pain in older guinea pigs are part of geriatric visits. Recognizing pain and preparing for surgery are written for families.

  • Anesthetic planning for dental procedures and surgery
  • Sedation for imaging and oral exams
  • Full monitoring: ECG, SpO2, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature, with dedicated anesthesia technicians
  • Multimodal pain control and local blocks when they fit the procedure
  • Osteoarthritis, postoperative pain, and chronic pain
  • Geriatric care and palliative pain care

What a visit is like

Bring the guinea pig in a secure carrier with a familiar hide and a little of the hay they already eat. At the hospital, handling stays quiet. A pig that has not eaten since yesterday should be mentioned first when you call, so the team can plan the day around how sick the animal is.

When to call

Call while the hospital is open if your guinea pig:

  • Has eaten less hay, lost weight, or has a wet chin or drool
  • Is squeaking while urinating, straining, or has blood in the urine
  • Is bloated, breathing hard, or collapsed
  • Has flank hair loss, a mammary lump, or swellings under the jaw
  • Is in labor and not producing pups
