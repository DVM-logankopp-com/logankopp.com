Analgesia in Rats, Mice, and Prairie Dogs: Lessons From the Lab for the Exam Room

November 10, 2026

Rats and mice are among the most studied animals in analgesia research, yet Oates and Tarbert cite reviews showing that they still receive perioperative analgesics less consistently than larger animals. The authors suggest part of this is bias. Their reminder is simple: all mammals share nearly identical nociceptive pathways, and rodents are not "less sentient" than dogs or primates.

Their review also makes a practical point. Rodent dose requirements are often higher, and needed more often, than the historical numbers, because of their high metabolic rate.

The article

Oates R, Tarbert DK. Treatment of pain in rats, mice, and prairie dogs. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2023;26:151-174. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2022.07.005

This is a narrative review of mostly laboratory studies in young, healthy rats and mice, with a smaller set of prairie dog data. Each dose in its tables is labeled as empirical use, a pharmacodynamic study, or a pharmacokinetic study. Doses below are as the authors state them. Check them against a current formulary.

Reading lab data for a pet

The authors list the traps:

  • Pharmacokinetic studies often target "therapeutic levels" borrowed from other species.
  • Thermal and mechanical tests don't fully represent clinical pain.
  • Study animals are young and healthy, so geriatric or sick pets may need adjustments.
  • ED50 studies find the dose that works in half the animals, which is too low for many individuals.
  • Most drugs aren't FDA approved for rodents, so families need to know about off-label use.

Some clinicians start at half the low end of the dose range the first time they use a lab protocol. The authors see multimodal analgesia as the way to work around these limits, since it improves efficacy while lowering each dose.

Local anesthetics

In small rodents, topical, splash, and infiltration routes are the easiest. Lidocaine works within 5 minutes and lasts up to 2 hours, and bupivacaine takes 10 to 15 minutes and lasts up to 8 hours. The table lists empirical total doses of 7 or 10 mg/kg for lidocaine and 4 or 8 mg/kg for bupivacaine, with dilution to 0.5% and 0.25%. Consider buffering if the rodent is awake.

  • Lidocaine-prilocaine cream was ineffective for tail injections and tail biopsies in mice.
  • Liposomal bupivacaine at 1 mg/kg SC provided analgesia for 4 days in a rat incisional model.

NSAIDs: higher doses, narrower margins

The newer lab doses are higher than the historical ones, and some are close to toxic.

  • Ketoprofen. Historical doses in mice and rats were 2 to 5 mg/kg SC. Newer sources recommend 20 mg/kg in mice, which may exceed the threshold for GI ulceration. In rats, 5 to 10 mg/kg caused GI damage after a single dose, and the only effective surgical dose (25 mg/kg) was toxic. The authors recommend careful consideration before using ketoprofen in rats.
  • Carprofen. Historical doses were 2 to 5 mg/kg SC. Newer sources recommend 20 mg/kg in mice, again possibly above the ulcerogenic threshold. In rats, 5 to 25 mg/kg SC was ineffective for laparotomy 45 minutes after surgery, and carprofen gel at about 5 mg/kg daily was ineffective in a paw incision model.
  • Meloxicam. In mice, 20 mg/kg SC once daily (at 1 mg/mL) was safe for up to 6 days. SR meloxicam can cause injection site reactions in mice and rats, and a single 4 mg/kg dose of SR meloxicam was ineffective in a rat paw incision model.
  • Acetaminophen. In mice, 65.8 to 450 mg/kg SC was ineffective for laparotomy and vasectomy, and 450 mg/kg approached toxicity. Acetaminophen in drinking water gave adequate analgesia for 24 hours after laparotomy, but mice given it alone had higher grimace scores than mice given local anesthetics. The authors don't recommend acetaminophen alone for major surgery.

NSAIDs can affect platelets, the GI tract, the kidneys, and, in research settings, bone healing. The small muscle mass of rodents makes IM dosing hard.

Opioids

Butorphanol suits mild, short-lived pain. Mice need higher doses than rats, and SC injection may sting. For some pain types, maximum analgesia isn't reachable without approaching a lethal dose.

Buprenorphine binds the mu receptor tightly and dissociates slowly. The authors note that its analgesic efficacy has been similar to full mu agonists, with a ceiling on respiratory depression. Practical points from the review:

  • Diluted buprenorphine HCl, prepared aseptically and kept in glass vials protected from light, can be stored for 180 days, refrigerated or at room temperature.
  • High-concentration buprenorphine at 1 mg/kg didn't outperform the standard formulation in mice, because concentrations dropped by 16 hours.
  • Oral buprenorphine works in rats but needs about 10 times the SC dose.

Buprenorphine side effects in rats deserve attention:

  • Pica, especially in rats, can lead to poor weight gain, GI obstruction, or self-injury. Removing indigestible bedding helps, but some rats switch to overgrooming or self-mutilation, which can target the incision. The authors recommend cautious postoperative use, alternative analgesics, and treating nausea. Rodents can't vomit, so nausea may show as weight loss or pica.
  • SR buprenorphine can cause inflammatory and necrotic skin lesions lasting up to 3 weeks. In rats, withdrawing the needle slowly and pinching the site for 15 seconds resolved this. SR buprenorphine has caused hypoxemia in healthy animals, and it isn't recommended in rats with respiratory compromise.
  • Naloxone lasts 3 hours or less, so a reversal may need repeating.
  • Through enterohepatic circulation and coprophagy, cagemates can be exposed.

Two SR buprenorphine products are FDA indexed for postprocedural pain in rats and mice.

Full mu agonists. In mice, IV morphine caused apnea at all doses tested. Fentanyl patches carry a risk of fatal overdose if eaten.

Tramadol results are mixed. At 10 mg/kg IP alone, it gave insufficient analgesia in a rat incisional model. With gabapentin at 80 mg/kg SC every 24 hours, tramadol at 10 mg/kg IP every 12 hours provided analgesia. Oral doses of 20 to 40 mg/kg worked in female rats, but males needed 40 mg/kg.

Opioid tolerance and hyperalgesia can develop with high or chronic doses. In a rat morphine model, tolerance developed within hours, and tolerance to analgesia can occur without tolerance to side effects.

Adjuncts

  • Gabapentin has a wide safety margin and works synergistically with opioids. Some effective oral doses in mice and rats exceed 100 mg/kg. It's renally excreted, so adjust for kidney disease.
  • Ketamine at low doses fits a multimodal plan, including CRIs. In a mouse orthopedic model, preemptive ketamine improved morphine's efficacy and reduced short- and long-term hyperalgesia.
  • Dexmedetomidine produces dose-dependent antinociception, and midazolam or butorphanol enhanced its effect. In rats, atipamezole reduced the analgesic effect of butorphanol, so consider extra analgesia if you reverse an alpha-2 agonist.
  • Maropitant may help visceral and inflammatory pain and was synergistic with morphine in rats. Refrigerating it reduces injection pain. One rodent died of noncardiogenic edema after IV maropitant, so the authors recommend more toxicity data before IV use.
  • Duloxetine and amitriptyline showed efficacy in rodent models of persistent, inflammatory, or neuropathic pain.

Prairie dogs

The prairie dog data are thinner and mostly empirical:

  • Empirical NSAID doses include carprofen, ketoprofen, and meloxicam. The authors note that meloxicam at 0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg may be inadequate.
  • SR buprenorphine at 0.9 and 1.2 mg/kg SC has pharmacokinetic data and caused mild skin reactions.
  • Gabapentin at 30 and 80 mg/kg was studied PO and SC. Oral gabapentin had poor bioavailability but kept presumed effective concentrations, extrapolated from rats, longer than SC dosing. No adverse effects were seen at doses up to 80 mg/kg.

Limitations

This is a narrative review, and most of the data come from young, healthy laboratory rats and mice in experimental pain models. Many table doses are empirical. Prairie dog data are limited to empirical doses and a small number of pharmacokinetic studies. Hamsters and gerbils have to be extrapolated from rat and mouse data.

How I'd use this

  1. Use multimodal analgesia for any surgery in a rodent. That includes local anesthetic at the surgical site.
  2. Expect higher and more frequent doses than older references give, but watch the GI margin with NSAIDs, especially ketoprofen in rats.
  3. Watch rats on buprenorphine for pica and incision chewing, and remove bedding they might eat.
  4. Don't send a rodent home on tramadol or acetaminophen alone after a major procedure.
  5. Plan for families. Use oral or long-acting options they can actually give, and teach them what pain looks like. A companion post covers pain recognition in rodents.
