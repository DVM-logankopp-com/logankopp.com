Rabbit GI Stasis: Warning Signs Every Bunny Owner Should Know

October 2026

A rabbit that stops eating is never a "wait until Monday" problem. Gastrointestinal (GI) stasis, when the normal movement of the gut slows down or stops, is one of the most common reasons rabbits need urgent care. Knowing the early signs can make a real difference.

How a rabbit's gut works

Rabbits are built to eat high-fiber food almost constantly. Their gut depends on a steady flow of hay and grass to keep moving. Rabbits also have a large fermentation chamber called the cecum, where helpful bacteria break down fiber. Rabbits re-eat some of the softer droppings this produces, called cecotropes, to get their nutrients.

When food intake drops, or something interferes with gut movement, gas and harmful bacteria can build up, the gut slows further, and a rabbit can become very sick quite quickly.

Early warning signs

Watch for:

  • Smaller, fewer or no fecal pellets, often the very first sign
  • Eating less, picking only at treats, or refusing hay
  • Being quieter than usual, hiding or not coming to greet you
  • Hunched posture, sitting pressed into a corner
  • Teeth grinding, which can be a sign of pain
  • A bloated or tight-feeling belly
  • Uneaten cecotropes left in the cage

You know your rabbit best. If something seems "off," trust that feeling.

Stasis is a sign, not a diagnosis

GI stasis usually has an underlying cause, and finding it is just as important as treating the slowdown. Common triggers include:

  • Pain, from dental disease, arthritis, bladder problems or other illness
  • Diet, especially too little hay and too many pellets, treats or sugary foods
  • Stress, from a move, a new pet, loud noise or losing a companion
  • Dehydration
  • A blockage in the intestines, which needs to be ruled out because it is treated differently

What your vet will do

Your veterinarian will examine your rabbit and check hydration, gut sounds, temperature and the belly. X-rays or other imaging may be recommended to check for gas patterns or a blockage. Bloodwork can help look for underlying disease.

Treatment depends on the cause and how sick your rabbit is. It may include:

  • Fluids to rehydrate
  • Pain relief
  • Assisted (syringe) feeding with a high-fiber recovery food
  • Medications to support gut movement when appropriate
  • Treating the underlying problem, such as dental disease
  • Hospitalization for more serious cases
  • Surgery, in the less common case of a true blockage

Many rabbits recover well when treatment starts early.

Preventing GI stasis

  • Unlimited fresh grass hay, such as timothy, which should make up most of the diet
  • Fresh leafy greens every day
  • Limited pellets, and very few sugary treats or fruit
  • Plenty of fresh water
  • Daily exercise outside the enclosure
  • A calm, predictable routine with places to hide
  • Regular wellness exams that include a careful dental check

When to call your vet

Call your rabbit's veterinarian the same day if your rabbit:

  • Hasn't eaten, or has eaten very little, for several hours
  • Has produced few or no droppings
  • Has diarrhea (true diarrhea is an emergency in rabbits)
  • Seems painful, bloated or hunched
  • Is weak, cold, or not responding normally

Don't give human medications or wait to see if it passes. After hours, seek emergency care.
