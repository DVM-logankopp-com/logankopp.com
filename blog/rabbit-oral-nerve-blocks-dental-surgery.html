Oral Nerve Blocks for Rabbit Dental Surgery: Landmarks, Doses, and Pitfalls

November 10, 2026

Incisor extractions, cheek tooth extractions, and mandibular abscess debridement are among the most painful procedures we do in rabbits, and these patients need to start eating again soon after surgery. Local anesthesia fits these cases well. It costs little and needs only an insulin syringe and the right landmarks.

Jekl and Hauptman wrote a step-by-step guide to the oral blocks they use. I've added notes from a 2023 rabbit analgesia review that flags where the evidence is thinner.

The articles

Jekl V, Hauptman K. Regional analgesia for oral surgery in rabbits. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2025;28:597-608. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2025.05.003

Ozawa S, Cenani A, Sanchez-Migallon Guzman D. Treatment of pain in rabbits. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2023;26:201-227. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2022.09.001

Jekl and Hauptman's paper is a technique review based on published anatomy and the authors' clinical experience. Ozawa and colleagues wrote a narrative review of rabbit analgesia. Doses below are as the authors state them. Check them against a current formulary.

How often blocks are used

In a 2022 survey the authors cite, 59.0% of respondents (246 of 417) performed locoregional anesthesia in rabbits. The most common techniques were intratesticular injection and local infiltration, not dental blocks.

The drugs

The authors list three local anesthetics for dental work in rabbits:

  • Lidocaine: 1 to 2 mg/kg total. Onset 5 to 10 minutes, lasting 60 to 120 minutes.
  • Articaine: 2 mg/kg total. Onset 10 to 30 minutes, lasting 30 to 90 minutes.
  • Bupivacaine: 2 mg/kg total. Onset 20 to 30 minutes, lasting 180 to 240 minutes.

These are total doses for the patient, not per site. Ozawa and colleagues state that infiltration or regional block doses of 4 mg/kg for lidocaine and 2 mg/kg for bupivacaine should avoid toxicity in rabbits, so a bupivacaine block at 2 mg/kg leaves no room for a second bupivacaine injection elsewhere. They also cite transient arrhythmias after 2.2 to 2.8 mg/kg of IV bupivacaine in rabbits.

Do the math first

This is the step people skip. The authors give the example of a 1 kg rabbit. At 1 to 2 mg/kg, the total lidocaine dose is 0.05 to 0.1 mL of a 2% solution.

They also recommend injecting about 0.15 to 0.3 mL per site, depending on the rabbit's size. If you plan a mandibular block plus intraligamentary injections on a small rabbit, the volume you need can be several times the volume of drug you're allowed. The authors' answer is to dilute the calculated dose with sterile water or saline, not to draw up more drug. I calculate the total local anesthetic dose for the whole procedure before drawing anything up, then split it between sites.

Their other principles:

  • Use an insulin syringe with a 27- to 31-gauge, 12 mm needle.
  • Disinfect the skin or gingiva with chlorhexidine first.
  • Warm the solution to room temperature or 37 to 38 °C.
  • Aspirate before injecting, and reposition if you see blood.
  • Inject slowly.

Which block for which procedure

The authors' combinations for common procedures:

  • Incisor extraction: mandibular nerve block, infraorbital or maxillary nerve block, and intraligamentary anesthesia.
  • Mandibular premolar abscess: mandibular nerve block.
  • Maxillary incisor abscess by a lateral transgingival approach: infraorbital or maxillary nerve block, intraligamentary anesthesia, and palatine nerve block.

Mandibular (inferior alveolar) nerve block

Covers: the ipsilateral mandible, premolars, molars, incisors, and rostral soft tissues.

Percutaneous approach (the authors' choice): Palpate the facial vascular notch (incisura vasorum facialium) on the ventral mandible. Insert the needle from the medial surface of the mandible at about 120° in a caudodorsal direction to a depth of about 10 to 12 mm, staying as close to the mandibular ramus as you can. Avoid the facial vein, which runs through the notch.

In rabbits with elongated cheek tooth apices, the space between apices can be mistaken for the notch.

Intraoral approach: Insert the needle at 45° from inside the mouth, lingual to about the second molar, roughly 1 cm deep and close to the medial mandible. The authors find it harder to get a reliable block this way.

Ozawa and colleagues add an important caveat. The efficacy study for this block in New Zealand White rabbits used 5 mL of 2% lidocaine in rabbits of about 2 kg, which is about 50 mg/kg. They note that dose exceeds what is considered safe in any species, and that the efficacy of the block at an appropriate volume is unknown.

Mental nerve block

Covers: the ipsilateral lower lip, soft tissues rostral to the mental foramen, and the gingiva around the incisors.

Percutaneous approach: Palpate the mesial edge of the first mandibular cheek tooth (P3). On a line perpendicular to the long axis of the mandible at that point, about halfway down the mandible, insert the needle perpendicular to the bone until it touches.

Intraoral approach: Insert the needle in the middle of the diastema between the incisor and premolar, and advance it ventrocaudally at 45° to a depth of 0.3 to 0.5 cm.

Maxillary nerve block

Covers: the ipsilateral lip and soft tissues, the gingiva around the maxillary incisors, and the maxillary premolars, molars, and incisors.

Percutaneous approach: Insert the needle below the zygomatic arch, 2 to 3 mm caudal to the lateral canthus. Guide it dorsomedially and caudally until it touches the alveolar bulla, walk it along the bulla's caudal border, and advance 1 to 2 mm deeper before injecting. A 12 mm insulin needle is long enough only in smaller rabbits, and the authors recommend a 25-gauge, 16 mm needle for others.

Ozawa and colleagues describe the same approach toward the pterygopalatine fossa with 0.25 to 0.5 mL of local anesthetic. In the New Zealand White rabbit study they cite, dye stained the maxillary nerve in 13 of 14 rabbits. Ultrasound of the globe and pterygopalatine fossa through the upper lid can confirm spread.

Jekl and Hauptman list puncture of the globe or a large vessel as a risk of this block.

Infraorbital nerve block

Covers: the ipsilateral lip and soft tissues rostral to P4, including the gingiva around the maxillary incisors.

Percutaneous approach: Insert the needle about 2 to 3 mm dorsorostral to the facial tuber, vertically or slightly caudomedially. When you hit bone, withdraw about 1 mm and inject. The goal is to deposit the drug above the bone, not in the spongy maxilla.

Intraoral approach: From the middle of the diastema, insert at 45° caudodorsally, buccal, about 1 cm into the gingiva.

The infraorbital nerve is thick and fills most of the infraorbital canal, so injecting into the canal or foramen risks direct nerve injury. Neuropraxia of the upper lip was reported in a pet rabbit after a deep infraorbital block, and Ozawa and colleagues note that lip function returned fully within 10 days. Based on their experience, Jekl and Hauptman are not in favor of deep infraorbital or deep mental blocks, because the needle enters a bony canal and the chance of hitting the nerve is high.

Palatine nerve block

Covers: the ipsilateral palatal mucosa and submucosa.

Intraoral approach: Inject near the major palatine foramen, 2 to 4 mm medial to the last maxillary premolar (P4). If the fourth premolars nearly meet at the midline, insert 1 to 2 mm from the midline instead, to avoid the palatine artery that exits with the nerve.

Intraligamentary anesthesia

Covers: a single tooth.

Insert the needle into the periodontal space between the tooth and the alveolus, and inject under pressure from as many sides of the tooth as possible. Use at least 0.1 to 0.2 mL per site. The drug can also go into the periodontal space after partial luxation. Onset is almost immediate. The authors pair it with other blocks and systemic analgesia for extractions rather than using it alone.

Complications

In more than 200 cases, the authors did not see systemic adverse effects from oral blocks. The complications they list:

  • Systemic toxicity from intravascular injection or overdose: seizures, hypotension, and arrhythmias. Bupivacaine carries the most cardiovascular risk. The authors note that IV lidocaine at 5 to 6 mg/kg can cause seizures.
  • Bleeding
  • Neuropraxia
  • Pain at the injection site
  • Infection
  • Failed blocks from a short needle or the wrong site

Infection near the nerve also lowers the efficacy of the block, because tissue pH changes. Our abscess patients are the cases where we most want a block, so this matters.

Limitations

This is a technique review based on anatomy and the authors' experience. For several of these blocks, controlled studies of efficacy and safety in pet rabbits haven't been published, and the published efficacy study for the mandibular block used a lidocaine dose far above safe limits. The landmark descriptions come largely from New Zealand White rabbits, so they may need adjusting for dwarf and giant breeds.

How I'd use this

  1. Calculate the total dose for every site first, then dilute to volume.
  2. Use the percutaneous mandibular approach for mandibular extractions and abscesses, and find the notch before you prep.
  3. Stay out of the canals. Deposit infraorbital blocks at the foramen, not inside it.
  4. Add intraligamentary injections for individual extractions. They're fast, and they help when an abscess makes a nerve block less reliable.
  5. Keep systemic analgesia going. Blocks are part of a multimodal plan, not a replacement. In my rabbits, that usually means ketamine with the premedication, buprenorphine, and an NSAID after surgery once the rabbit is warm and blood pressure is stable.
