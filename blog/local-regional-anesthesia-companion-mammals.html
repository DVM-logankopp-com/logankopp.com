Local and Regional Blocks in Companion Mammals: Doses, Techniques, and What Carries Over

November 10, 2026

When we have little species-specific data for a systemic analgesic, a local anesthetic is often the safest drug to reach for. DiGeronimo and da Cunha point out why. Voltage-gated sodium channels are widely conserved across vertebrates, so local anesthetics are likely to work even in species where they haven't been studied. Opioid receptor types, distribution, and affinity, by contrast, vary widely between species.

This post pulls together their principles with the mammal-specific block data from four other reviews in the same journal.

The articles

DiGeronimo PM, da Cunha AF. Local and regional anesthesia in zoological companion animal practice. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2022;25:321-336. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2021.08.015

Ozawa S, Cenani A, Sanchez-Migallon Guzman D. Treatment of pain in rabbits. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2023;26:201-227. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2022.09.001

Kapaldo N, Eshar D. Ferret sedation and anesthesia. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2022;25:273-296. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2021.08.006

Oates R, Tarbert DK. Treatment of pain in rats, mice, and prairie dogs. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2023;26:151-174. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2022.07.005

Bennett K, Lewis K. Sedation and anesthesia in rodents. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2022;25:211-255. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2021.08.013

All five are narrative reviews. Doses below are as the authors state them. Check them against a current formulary.

Principles that apply to every species

Equal doses are a warning sign. DiGeronimo and da Cunha note that when a formulary lists the same dose for lidocaine and bupivacaine in a species, you should suspect the research is thin. In well-studied species, the lidocaine dose is usually 4 to 5 times the bupivacaine dose.

Longer-acting means more toxic. Agents with a longer duration are more lipophilic and protein bound, and more toxic. Systemic toxicity shows up mainly as cardiotoxicity, along with seizures and, less often, nerve injury, allergic reactions, and methemoglobinemia. Ropivacaine is less cardiotoxic than bupivacaine.

Mixing lidocaine and bupivacaine doesn't help. The idea is to get lidocaine's fast onset with bupivacaine's long duration. The authors cite studies showing a mixture works no faster than either drug alone, and in people a 1:1 mixture lasted no longer than lidocaine alone. If you want duration, bupivacaine alone lasts longer.

Buffering is unsettled. Adding sodium bicarbonate reduces the sting of injection. Reports conflict on whether it also speeds onset and extends duration, and it may only matter for formulations with epinephrine.

Formulation matters. Use preservative-free products for epidural or intrathecal injection. Use only preservative-free lidocaine without epinephrine IV.

Avoid intra-articular bupivacaine. Chondrotoxicity has been shown in canine chondrocytes, horses, and humans, and the authors suggest it may be chondrotoxic in all species.

Be cautious with benzocaine. It caused methemoglobinemia in a cat and in a meerkat after topical application to the glottis. The authors recommend avoiding it in cats and using it with caution in other species.

Dose ceilings by species

These ceilings differ between sources and are mostly extrapolated:

  • Rabbits: Ozawa and colleagues state that lidocaine at 4 mg/kg and bupivacaine at 2 mg/kg for infiltration or regional blocks should avoid toxicity. They also cite transient arrhythmias after 2.2 to 2.8 mg/kg of IV bupivacaine in rabbits.
  • Rodents: Bennett and Lewis, in their rodent anesthesia review, give 7 mg/kg for lidocaine and 8 mg/kg for bupivacaine. Oates and Tarbert's table lists lidocaine at 7 or 10 mg/kg and bupivacaine at 4 or 8 mg/kg total dose, based on expert use, with dilution to 0.5% lidocaine or 0.25% bupivacaine.

Ozawa and colleagues note that block volume matters as much as dose, and that the calculated dose can be diluted with saline to reach the volume a block needs.

Rabbits: the best-described exotic mammal for blocks

Epidural. DiGeronimo and da Cunha call the rabbit a good model for epidural research, so the technique is well described. Ozawa and colleagues give the details. Place the rabbit in sternal recumbency with the hind limbs pulled forward, find the lumbosacral depression at the level of the iliac wings, and use a 22- to 25-gauge spinal or Tuohy needle. Confirm placement by loss of resistance or the hanging drop technique, and aspirate for CSF and blood before injecting.

Two rabbit-specific cautions:

  • The spinal cord usually ends in the second sacral vertebra, and the dural sac extends into the sacrum. Accidental subdural or spinal injection at the lumbosacral space is likely.
  • Rabbits have little CSF, and its absence in the needle hub doesn't confirm epidural placement.

Epidural lidocaine 2% at 0.2 mL/kg gave a sensory and motor block of the hindquarters within 1 to 3 minutes that lasted 20 to 40 minutes. Bupivacaine at 0.125% to 0.5%, 0.1 to 0.4 mL/kg, has also been used. A higher bupivacaine dose lasted longer but caused more hypotension. Morphine at 0.1 mg/kg is commonly added. All drugs should be preservative free.

Sciatic and femoral blocks. In the one clinical case series in pet rabbits, 0.05 mL/kg of 2% lidocaine around each nerve with a nerve stimulator gave effective blockade for orthopedic surgery distal to the stifle, with no side effects reported. The duration wasn't measured.

Brachial plexus. With ultrasound and a nerve stimulator, 0.8 mL/kg of 0.4% ropivacaine gave a sensory block lasting 319 ± 51 minutes.

Greater auricular nerve. In a clinical study, 0.3 mL/kg of 0.5% bupivacaine gave a reliable block of the pinna lasting 30 to 184 minutes. Watch for an accidental auriculopalpebral block, which in that study removed the palpebral reflex for up to 240 minutes. Protect the eye.

Intratesticular. Inject 0.05 to 1 mL per testicle, depending on the rabbit's size, through the caudal pole toward the spermatic cord until the testicle is turgid. The rest of the calculated dose can go into an incisional block.

Retrobulbar. Ozawa and colleagues describe ultrasound-guided and landmark techniques in New Zealand White rabbits. They caution about the large retrobulbar venous plexus.

For oral surgery, see our post on oral nerve blocks for rabbit dental surgery. For a thoracic plane block, see our post on the transversus thoracis plane block for rabbit sternotomy.

Ferrets: epidurals for the caudal half

Kapaldo and Eshar recommend considering a lumbosacral epidural for any invasive procedure involving the tail, perineum (for example, rectal prolapse), pelvic limbs, or abdomen. For abdominal surgery, the volume has to increase so the drug spreads further cranially. Their durations:

  • Lidocaine: 1 to 2 hours
  • Ropivacaine or bupivacaine: 4 to 6 hours
  • Morphine: 12 to 18 hours of segmental analgesia

Expect self-limiting motor paralysis from the local anesthetic, monitor for urination after epidural opioids, and watch for hypotension from sympathetic blockade when the block spreads cranially. Crystalloid boluses or vasopressors can treat it.

Rodents: mostly infiltration and splash blocks

In small rodents, topical, splash, and infiltration routes are the most practical, according to Oates and Tarbert. Epidural studies in rodents are limited to experimental models.

  • Lidocaine-prilocaine cream worked for ear tattooing in rabbits under an occlusive bandage, but was ineffective for tail vein injection and tail biopsy pain in mice. The authors suggest the animals may remove it, or that tail scales may resist absorption.
  • Liposomal bupivacaine at 1 mg/kg SC gave analgesia for 4 days in a rat incisional pain model.
  • Buffering is worth considering if lidocaine or bupivacaine is given to an awake rodent.

TAP block. DiGeronimo and da Cunha describe an ultrasound-guided transversus abdominis plane block, injecting 0.2 mL of local anesthetic through a 26-gauge needle and an extension set. It has been reported in chinchillas with good success. In the authors' experience, it's easier to learn than an epidural or brachial plexus block.

Line blocks

An incisional line block needs no detailed anatomy and works in any species. The authors describe injecting into the muscle about 1 cm from the incision line. Line blocks may prolong incisional healing, though a study of preoperative bupivacaine line blocks in spayed dogs found no clearly relevant effect on healing.

Lidocaine CRIs

In rabbits after ovariohysterectomy, a lidocaine CRI (2 mg/kg bolus, then 100 µg/kg/min for 2 days) led to better fecal output, food intake, and glucose concentrations than buprenorphine. Lidocaine CRIs are used in dogs and rabbits to reduce inhalant MAC. In cats, however, they are contraindicated because they significantly lower cardiac output. DiGeronimo and da Cunha's point is that cardiovascular effects can be species-specific, so be cautious with lidocaine CRIs in species without data.

Limitations

These are narrative reviews. Most rabbit block techniques come from New Zealand White rabbits, cadaver work, or small case series, and many have no published efficacy or complication data in pet rabbits. Rodent doses are largely based on expert use and laboratory models. Ferret epidural recommendations come from the authors' experience and other species.

How I'd use this

  1. Calculate the total local anesthetic dose before drawing anything up, and dilute to the volume you need.
  2. Pick one drug. If you need duration, use bupivacaine or ropivacaine alone rather than a mixture.
  3. Add an epidural for caudal procedures in rabbits and ferrets, and assume a rabbit's lumbosacral needle could be subdural.
  4. Consider a line block or intratesticular block for castrations and laparotomies when a more targeted block isn't practical.
  5. Use a lidocaine CRI for longer rabbit cases, but keep it out of cats and be careful in species with no data.

Questions for rounds

  1. Which of your formulary's exotic mammal local anesthetic doses list lidocaine and bupivacaine at the same mg/kg?
  2. How would you adjust a lumbosacral epidural in a rabbit if you saw CSF in the hub?
  3. Would you put a lidocaine CRI on a hedgehog or a sugar glider? What would you monitor?
