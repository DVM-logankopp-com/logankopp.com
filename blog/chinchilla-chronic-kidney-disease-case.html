Chronic Kidney Disease in a Geriatric Chinchilla: Case Rounds

November 9, 2026

Chinchillas are good at looking fine. The dense coat hides a lot of lost muscle, and a slow decline can go on for months before anyone puts a hand on the spine and notices. This case report is a quiet example of that.

The paper

Niculae M, Crăciun S, Marica R, et al. Severe chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy in a geriatric pet chinchilla (Chinchilla lanigera): a case report and literature review. Animals (Basel). 2026;16(21):3451. doi:10.3390/ani16213451

The case

The patient was an 11-year-old neutered female chinchilla. She had six months of progressive weight loss and hyporexia. There was no workup before death, so kidney disease and heat stress were not documented in life. The family estimated about eight hours in a warm room, with a peak of 29 °C (about 84 °F). She died with anorexia, no fecal output, and dyspnea.

At necropsy, both kidneys showed severe chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy with interstitial fibrosis and mineralization. There was also gas distension of the GI tract. The lungs showed congestion only. Any link to the earlier weight loss is unproven. The authors concluded the death was multifactorial.

Multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae grew from six sites. No inflammatory lesions went with it, so the Klebsiella may have spread after death.

Three points worth taking back to the clinic

Weight loss in an old chinchilla is a workup, not an age

Six months is a long runway. A geriatric chinchilla losing weight deserves a minimum database (CBC, biochemistry, urinalysis, radiographs), and the kidneys belong on the differential list next to dental disease, which is the usual first suspect. The two often travel together in older animals, so finding bad cheek teeth doesn't end the search.

A gram scale earns its keep in chinchilla geriatrics. Weekly home weigh-ins, written down and brought to each visit, show a trend long before the coat does. Body condition scoring through that coat is unreliable without feeling for the spine and pelvis.

Heat as a possible contributor

The heat exposure was the family's estimate, and heat stress was not documented in life. Chinchillas tolerate heat poorly. For older chinchillas, I talk about room temperature at every visit from late spring through early fall, and about what happens when the air conditioning fails.

Read post-mortem cultures with the histopathology

A multidrug-resistant Klebsiella from six sites looks dramatic on paper. Without inflammation in the tissues, it may say more about the interval between death and necropsy than about the cause of death. It's a fair reminder to read culture results alongside the histopathology rather than on their own.

Limitations

This is one animal, and the timeline of decline was reconstructed from the family's history. There was no workup before death, so kidney disease and heat stress were not documented in life. The heat exposure was the family's estimate. Any link to the earlier weight loss is unproven. A single case can't tell us how common severe chronic kidney disease is in older pet chinchillas.
