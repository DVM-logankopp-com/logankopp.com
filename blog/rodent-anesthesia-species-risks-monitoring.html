Rodent Anesthesia: Species Risks, Preparation, and Monitoring

November 10, 2026

A 30 g mouse, a 1 kg guinea pig, and a prairie dog have very little in common except their incisors, and anesthetizing them as if they were the same patient is how things go wrong.

Bennett and Lewis put a number on the stakes early. In the prospective study they cite, perianesthetic mortality in guinea pigs was 3.8%, compared with 0.17% in dogs and 0.24% in cats.

The article

Bennett K, Lewis K. Sedation and anesthesia in rodents. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2022;25:211-255. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2021.08.013

This is a narrative review by an exotic companion mammal clinician and a veterinary anesthesiologist. It includes extensive dose tables, most of which come from laboratory rodent references. I haven't reproduced those tables here. Check any dose against a current formulary.

Why rodents are higher risk

The authors list several reasons:

  • High metabolic rate. Rodents need more oxygen and glucose, and they tolerate even brief hypoxemia poorly. The authors state that irreversible CNS injury occurs within 30 seconds of respiratory arrest.
  • Low glycogen reserves, which predispose them to hypoglycemia.
  • A large surface area to volume ratio, so they lose heat quickly.
  • Fast drug elimination, so rodents often need higher doses given more often.
  • Small, hard-to-reach veins and airways.
  • High circulating catecholamines, with the tachycardia, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and arrhythmia risk that goes with them.

All rodents are obligate nasal breathers, so nasal disease matters for anesthesia in every species.

Species notes

Guinea pigs, chinchillas, and degus (caviomorphs). The soft palate extends to the base of the tongue, leaving a single opening to the pharynx, the palatal ostium. It is typically 2 to 3 mm wide, and the tissue around it is highly vascular and bleeds easily. Healthy guinea pigs and chinchillas normally keep food in the oropharynx. Passive regurgitation has been noted anecdotally in caviomorphs under sedation and anesthesia. Degus are prone to elodontomas that can partly obstruct the nasal passages.

The authors cite a 23% prevalence of heart murmurs in chinchillas. Low-grade murmurs are often benign, but chinchillas with a murmur of grade 3/6 or higher had a 29 times higher rate of echocardiographic abnormalities, and the authors recommend an echocardiogram before anesthesia in those chinchillas. Normal bony spicules in guinea pig lungs can be mistaken for a foreign body or osseous metaplasia on radiographs.

Rats, mice, hamsters, and gerbils (myomorphs). Hamsters store food in their cheek pouches, and it can be aspirated if the pouches aren't emptied before anesthesia. Some gerbil strains have idiopathic epilepsy, and the authors advise avoiding sedatives that lower the seizure threshold. They list acepromazine as one. Gerbils also lack a complete circle of Willis. An adult rat's trachea is only about 1.6 to 1.7 mm in diameter.

Prairie dogs and squirrels (sciuromorphs). Prairie dogs have a large, fleshy tongue and an oropharyngeal opening like that of caviomorphs, so intubation is difficult. Odontomas of the maxillary incisors are common between 2 and 6 years of age and can obstruct the nasal passages. Heart disease is diagnosed in a high percentage of pet prairie dogs older than 2 to 3 years, so auscultate carefully. Illness or cold can push a prairie dog into dormancy, and the authors suggest reassessing after 24 hours of warmth and rehydration.

Preparation

Acclimation. The authors recommend letting rodents spend an hour in a dark, quiet area of the hospital before anesthesia, and laying out every drug and piece of equipment before the animal is handled. Their example checklist is a good model.

Bloodwork. A CBC and chemistry panel is ideal. Blood volume in rodents is about 6% to 8% of body weight, and the authors recommend collecting no more than 10% of it. When that isn't enough for a full panel, a smear, PCV, total solids, glucose, BUN, and sometimes lactate still help. They suggest delaying anesthesia to correct dehydration, and correcting severe anemia (hematocrit below 20%) when possible.

Fasting. The authors don't recommend fasting small rodents, because they can't vomit and hypoglycemia is a real risk. Published fasting times for guinea pigs and chinchillas range from 0 to 8 hours. Fasting probably does little to reduce GI volume in these species given the size of the cecum, and it risks GI stasis and hypoglycemia. Instead, the authors rinse the mouths of guinea pigs, chinchillas, degus, and prairie dogs with 2 to 5 mL of water and clear food with cotton-tipped applicators 30 to 60 minutes before anesthesia, removing food at the same time. They describe this as their own clinical practice rather than a studied protocol.

Sedation and premedication

Most rodent dosing data come from laboratory studies, and lab doses often run higher than what is needed clinically. When the authors use a protocol for the first time, they start at half the low end of the laboratory dose range. Adding a local anesthetic can make sedation enough for moderately invasive procedures such as lancing an abscess.

Induction and maintenance

Inhalant induction is common in rodents to limit handling. Moving quickly through the excitement phases limits catecholamine surges. The authors note that isoflurane irritates mucous membranes, so sevoflurane may be preferred. Ketamine causes the eyes to stay open during sedation, so lubricate them.

The authors recommend constant rate infusions over intermittent boluses for injectable maintenance, stating that intermittent boluses increase morbidity and mortality in rodents. Lengthy infusions can accumulate and prolong recovery. The authors' CRI table includes butorphanol, fentanyl, hydromorphone, ketamine, lidocaine, propofol, and others, mostly from rat, mouse, and guinea pig reports. Fentanyl and remifentanil infusions reduced MAC by 10% to 60% in rats, with respiratory depression.

I published a case report this year on lidocaine-ketamine-propofol TIVA in two chinchillas (doi:10.1016/j.vaa.2026.101371), and I'm glad to talk through how I set those infusions up.

Equipment. Non-rebreathing circuits are preferred for their low resistance. As a rule, the authors set fresh gas flow at twice the minute volume with a minimum of 0.5 L/min, but note that higher flows cool the patient. Peak inspiratory pressure should stay below 20 to 25 cm H2O. Minimize dead space with low dead space connectors.

Intubation is difficult, especially through a palatal ostium. Video endoscopy, rigid endoscopes, otoscopes, and stylets all help. Have swabs ready to clear the mouth, because aspirated food can kill a rodent quickly. The smallest cuffed and uncuffed tubes are 2.5 and 2.0 mm, and IV catheters can be used as tubes in smaller species.

Monitoring when the equipment struggles

Every standard monitor has a weakness in rodents. Knowing what each one misses is half the job.

  • Doppler and stethoscope. Simple and informative. A Doppler probe on the thorax lets you hear heart rate and respiration.
  • ECG. Use needles through the skin rather than alligator clips. Some machines won't read rates above 350 bpm.
  • Pulse oximetry. Heart rates above 400 bpm can defeat it, and spring clips can occlude flow. Reflectance probes work on furred skin.
  • Capnography. Most sidestream units draw 150 to 200 mL/min, which gives falsely low readings with tiny tidal volumes and fast respiratory rates. Even low-flow units share that error, so follow trends rather than absolute values. Values up to 55 mm Hg are acceptable under anesthesia.
  • Blood pressure. The authors give 95/60 (75) mm Hg as acceptable under anesthesia. A palpable femoral pulse suggests systolic pressure above 60 mm Hg, and palpable distal pulses (tail, tibial, pedal, lingual) suggest above 80 mm Hg. Oscillometric units struggle with high heart rates. Doppler has the same cuff size limits, but reading the rate is up to the user.
  • Depth. Corneal and palpebral reflexes are unreliable. Pedal withdrawal and the ear reflex are better.
  • Everything else. Elevate the thorax in dorsal recumbency so abdominal organs don't press on the diaphragm. Lubricate the eyes, which often stay open. Check glucose in at-risk patients. Score pain after surgery. Grimace scales are reliable in rats and mice.

Temperature and recovery

Anesthetic drugs widen the thermoregulatory range from about 0.4 °C to about 4 °C. The authors' approach:

  • Use warmed scrub, fluids, and lavage.
  • Keep forced air or circulating water warming in place from sedation through recovery.
  • Use the lowest practical fresh gas flow.
  • Prewarming rats before anesthesia delayed hypothermia in the studies they cite.

The authors attribute prolonged recovery mostly to hypothermia, hypotension, hypoglycemia, or impaired drug elimination. Keep ventilating until the rodent breathes on its own, and keep the Doppler and pulse oximeter on until it can walk.

Two cautions deserve attention:

  1. Rewarming can unmask hypovolemia and hypoglycemia. Warming raises glucose demand and dilates constricted peripheral vessels. The authors suggest this may explain some sudden postoperative deaths in rodents.
  2. Chinchillas overheat. The authors recommend an 80 to 85 °F recovery cage for most rodents, but chinchillas with an intact coat need cooler surroundings and can become hyperthermic above 75 °F. Small rodents swing quickly with external heat, so check temperature every 15 to 20 minutes and reduce warming once the rodent is within a degree of normal.

Pad the recovery cage, skip particulate bedding until the rodent is fully recovered, and use a camera if no one can watch directly. Reversal agents help appetite and body temperature return sooner.

Limitations

This is a narrative review. Much of the dosing and physiology comes from laboratory rats and mice, which differ from pet rodents in age, health, and strain. The oral rinse protocol and several recovery recommendations come from the authors' experience rather than published studies.

How I'd use this

  1. Know the species before the patient arrives. Look up the airway, vital parameters, and known breed or strain issues before it's in your hands.
  2. Empty the mouth or pouches, and don't starve the patient.
  3. Start low with laboratory doses, and give reversible drugs priority.
  4. Use monitors to follow trends. In rodents, absolute numbers from small-animal equipment are often wrong, but the direction of change still tells you something.
  5. Use heat carefully. Watch for overheating, especially in chinchillas, and remember that warming can unmask a volume or glucose problem.
