Triage and Decontamination for the Poisoned Rabbit, Rodent, or Ferret

November 23, 2026

With small mammals, the usual dog-and-cat toxicology playbook doesn't transfer well. Ferrets are the only companion exotic species in which emesis can be attempted. Many patients need sedation for anything more than a quick look, and bathing a small mammal carries a real risk of hypothermia.

Latney and Hayes wrote the triage and decontamination article in the new Veterinary Clinics of North America: Exotic Animal Practice toxicology issue. These are my notes on the parts that apply to rabbits, rodents, and ferrets, along with relevant pieces from the airborne toxicants and diagnostic testing articles in the same issue.

Stabilize first, then take the history

Breathing, circulation, and seizures get addressed before anything else, regardless of the exposure. Once the patient is stable, the toxicologic history drives everything that follows: what the substance was, its concentration, the amount, the route, the time of exposure, and when signs started.

Telephone triage has real limits. Families can't assess many signs, and some will understate an exposure out of guilt or worry about cost. It still has value. The authors suggest asking callers to photograph the product label and the surrounding environment and giving them guidance on safe transport. The article includes a sample exotic animal toxin triage sheet that clients can complete at check-in, and it's worth adapting for your front desk.

Ask about the environment, not just the event. Plants or pesticide-treated greens offered as food or enrichment, galvanized cages, heat lamps, and diet formulation errors (excess vitamin D, mycotoxins) can all be sources the family hasn't connected to the illness.

Decontamination is not automatic

Decontamination depends on the route and the toxicokinetics of the substance, and it isn't always necessary. Many of these patients need sedation or anesthesia to be decontaminated safely, and that carries its own risk. The authors are direct about it: weigh the risk of sedation and the procedure against the risk of the exposure before you start.

Remove contaminated bedding, food, and water, and keep other animals away from the area.

Gastrointestinal decontamination

Emesis is an option only in ferrets. It should only be considered in mentally appropriate ferrets, and it's contraindicated for corrosives, hydrocarbons, and fast-acting toxins, and in ferrets with respiratory, neurologic, or cardiovascular disease or debilitation. The options listed are:

  • 3% hydrogen peroxide, 2.2 mL/kg PO, repeated once after 15 minutes if there's no emesis
  • Apomorphine, 0.04 mg/kg SC, IM, or IV, or 0.25 mg/kg subconjunctivally

No studies have evaluated hydrogen peroxide safety or recovery of gastric contents in ferrets. In dogs, one study found 3% hydrogen peroxide comparable to apomorphine in safety and efficacy, but case reports describe gastric necrosis in a cat and subclinical gastric ulcers in dogs.

Gastric lavage requires general anesthesia and intubation. It's contraindicated with corrosives, hydrocarbons, and fast-acting substances, and in rabbits the thin stomach wall raises the risk of perforation. Material larger than the tube won't come back, and chocolate or iron pills can form concretions that block it.

Activated charcoal is often the practical option. The recommended dose in companion exotic animals is 1 g/kg. It binds large nonpolar molecules well and binds alcohols, metals, and hydrocarbons poorly. In human data it works best within 1 to 2 hours of exposure. Contraindications include significant oral or dental disease (a real consideration in rabbits and guinea pigs), GI obstruction, dehydration, and patients that can't hold a normal head position. Use caution giving it by feeding tube in rabbits for the same thin-wall reason.

Cathartics such as sorbitol should be avoided in dehydrated patients or those with diarrhea. Cholestyramine has a low adverse effect risk because it's minimally absorbed; suggested dosing is 50 to 300 mg/kg PO three times daily. Psyllium can help move small objects such as lead paint chips through the GI tract.

Two species-specific steps are easy to forget:

  • Cecotrophy. In species that eat cecotrophs, prevent re-ingestion by collecting them or using an Elizabethan collar. Make sure the patient can still eat and drink, and keep the hindquarters clean.
  • Cheek pouches. Hamsters may store a toxin in their cheek pouches. Gently evert the pouches and empty them with a cotton swab.

When animals are group-housed, separate affected animals so cagemates aren't exposed through urine and feces.

Dermal and ocular exposures

Thin, well-vascularized skin and grooming turn many dermal exposures into oral ones. Wear impermeable gloves, stabilize first, and sedate if needed. Warm the rinse water to body temperature, monitor temperature throughout, dry the patient promptly, and provide heat without letting damp skin contact the heat source.

  • Water-soluble substances: gentle warm water flushing for 15 to 20 minutes.
  • Oily substances: dilute hand dishwashing detergent, rinsed until clear.
  • Glue traps: work corn or mineral oil into the glue until it releases, then bathe. Small areas of fur can be clipped.
  • Powders: brush out first, then bathe. A soft toothbrush works for small patients.

Monitor the skin for 1 to 2 days afterward. For ocular exposures, flush with saline, water, or artificial tears for 15 to 20 minutes, then stain with fluorescein and repeat the stain in 12 to 24 hours.

Intravenous lipid emulsion

ILE has been suggested for lipophilic toxicants, including ivermectin, local anesthetics, NSAIDs, permethrin, calcium channel blockers, beta blockers, baclofen, barbiturates, cardiac glycoside plants, and marijuana. Doses for zoological companion species have not been established. Published mammal protocols using 20% ILE include:

  • Ferret, ibuprofen: 1.5 mL/kg IV over 15 minutes, then 0.25 mL/kg/min for 30 minutes, repeated 2 additional times
  • Guinea pig, ivermectin: 1.5 mL/kg IV over 15 minutes, then 0.25 mL/kg/min for 60 minutes
  • Rabbit, ivermectin: 2 mL/kg IV bolus, then 0.25 mL/kg/min for 60 minutes

Stop other IV fluids during ILE to reduce the risk of fluid overload. Adverse effects include hyperlipidemia, volume overload, hemolysis, hypersensitivity, and interference with laboratory measurements. ILE can also be given IO.

Smoke inhalation

McCready, Butcher, and Brandão's airborne toxicants article is mostly about birds, but the mammal section has some practical points:

  • Pulse oximetry can mislead. Carbon monoxide and other toxicants causing carboxyhemoglobinemia or methemoglobinemia can produce an SpO2 above 90% in a cyanotic patient.
  • Lactate is less useful in rabbits. Rabbits normally have higher plasma lactate than many mammals, which limits its value as a marker of tissue hypoxia.
  • Repeat radiographs. Early films may be normal. Repeat in 24 to 36 hours, and hospitalize smoke-exposed animals for at least 48 hours.
  • Oxygen cages over masks to limit handling. For prolonged therapy, keep FiO2 under 50%.
  • Avoid cough suppressants, including opioids, and be cautious with corticosteroids.
  • Plan for the airway. The glottis is hard to see in rabbits without endoscopy, and the palatal ostium in guinea pigs and chinchillas makes it harder still, especially with swollen upper airway tissue.
  • Underlying infection. Rabbits with pasteurellosis and rats with mycoplasmosis may flare after the stress of exposure. Oral antibiotics targeting mainly gram-positive organisms remain contraindicated in rabbits and guinea pigs.

Save the samples

Niedringhaus and Murphy make the case that toxicology testing works best when it's targeted to a suspected compound rather than run as a fishing expedition. Turnaround can take days to weeks, so results usually help rule differentials in or out rather than guide initial treatment.

That makes the early samples matter. Freeze separated serum, and if the patient dies, freeze liver, kidney, fat, and GI contents in plastic bags at -20 °C. Keep hay and feed cool and dry rather than frozen. Save some formalin-fixed tissue as well, and call the lab when in doubt. Trace results don't automatically mean a finding is insignificant, and reference values for most exotic species are limited, so interpretation needs the clinical picture alongside it.
