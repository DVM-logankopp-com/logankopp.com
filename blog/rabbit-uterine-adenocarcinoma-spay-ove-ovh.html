Uterine Adenocarcinoma and the Rabbit Spay: OVE, OVH, and Why I Usually Take Part of the Vagina

November 23, 2026

Uterine adenocarcinoma is the tumor I expect to find in an older intact doe, and preventing it is the main medical reason I recommend spaying female rabbits. The question I get most from colleagues is no longer whether to spay a doe. It is which procedure: ovariectomy (OVE), ovariohysterectomy (OVH), or OVH with removal of part of the vagina. This post goes through what the larger case series show about uterine disease in rabbits, what is and is not known about OVE in this species, the anatomy that makes the rabbit spay different from a cat or dog spay, and how I approach the surgery.

Throughout, I try to keep three things separate: what the studies measured, what the authors concluded, and what I do in practice and why. Several of the points below rest on my own clinical reasoning rather than published data, and I say so where that applies.

How common is uterine disease in intact does?

The answer depends heavily on which population you look at, and the studies below look at very different populations.

Settai and colleagues (2020): 1,928 rabbits that had an OVH

Settai, Kondo, and Shibuya sent a questionnaire to 441 Japanese veterinary hospitals and received data from 43 of them (a 9.8% response rate). Those hospitals reported 1,928 rabbits that had an OVH between 2009 and 2018, either for suspected uterine disease or for prevention, all with histologic diagnoses. Median age at surgery was 59 months (range, 6 to 144 months).

  • Endometrial adenocarcinoma was reported in 1,035 of 1,928 rabbits (53.7%), and endometrial hyperplasia in 842 (43.7%).
  • Hematuria or a serosanguineous vaginal discharge was reported in 1,020 rabbits (52.9%).
  • Of 372 rabbits with no clinical signs at the time of OVH, 182 (48.9%) had adenocarcinoma.
  • Mean age at OVH was 67.2 months for rabbits with adenocarcinoma alone and 47.2 months for rabbits with hyperplasia alone.
  • The youngest rabbit with adenocarcinoma was a 6-month-old Netherland Dwarf.
  • The odds of adenocarcinoma increased with age at OVH (odds ratio 1.826; 95% CI, 1.640 to 2.033). Netherland Dwarfs had lower odds than Holland Lops (odds ratio 0.380).
  • Concurrent uterine leiomyoma was reported in 165 rabbits and leiomyosarcoma in 75. Anemia was reported in 26 rabbits, all of which had adenocarcinoma.

The authors recommended OVH before 2 years of age, preferably at 6 to 9 months, careful examination of the removed uterus, and histology.

The 53.7% figure should not be quoted as the prevalence of adenocarcinoma in pet rabbits. This was a population of rabbits that went to surgery, many of them because a uterine problem was already suspected, reported by fewer than 1 in 10 of the hospitals asked. The authors also note a bias toward dwarf breeds. The more useful numbers for practice are the asymptomatic subgroup and the age relationship: almost half of the does without clinical signs already had adenocarcinoma when the uterus was examined.

Walter and colleagues (2010): 59 rabbits with uterine disorders in Munich

Walter, Poth, Böhmer, Braun, and Matis reviewed 59 rabbits with uterine disorders seen between 2000 and 2006. Forty-five had an OVH and 14 were euthanized, 3 of them because of pulmonary metastases.

  • Endometrial hyperplasia was diagnosed in 24 rabbits, adenocarcinoma in 18, and both in 11.
  • Mean age was 4.5 years for hyperplasia and 6.1 years for adenocarcinoma. Every rabbit with adenocarcinoma was at least 2 years old. The youngest rabbit in the series, at 1 year, had hyperplasia.
  • Only 10 of 59 rabbits had vaginal discharge or hematuria, and the authors wrote that these signs cannot be considered hallmark signs. The other rabbits were presented for mammary masses, skin tumors, anorexia, or poor general health.
  • Ultrasonography identified abnormalities in all 35 rabbits examined. Radiographs showed an enlarged uterus in 32 of 39.
  • Four rabbits had ovarian tumors (3 granulosa cell tumors). Four had mammary adenocarcinoma, and all 4 also had uterine adenocarcinoma.
  • Eight of 29 rabbits with adenocarcinoma were alive 22 months or more after surgery. In other rabbits, pulmonary metastases appeared 2, 3, 5, and 8 months after OVH.

The authors recommended neutering before 1 year of age and regular ultrasonographic examination of intact does.

Saito and colleagues (2002): 47 rabbits diagnosed at ventrotomy

Saito, Nakanishi, and Hasegawa described 47 rabbits with uterine disorders diagnosed at ventrotomy. Endometrial hyperplasia was found in 29.8% and adenocarcinoma in 21.3%, with tumorous lesions overall in 46.8%. Ages ranged from 2 years 2 months to 7 years 6 months, with a peak at 4 to 5 years. Bleeding was the most common complaint (the abstract gives 62.2% and the results give 68.1%), and more than 90% of the rabbits that bled did so only at urination. Mammary disorders such as cysts were found on examination in 31.9%.

Two numbers from this paper are worth keeping in mind before surgery. Palpation detected the uterine disorder in 15 of 32 rabbits presented for bleeding, while ultrasonography detected it in 21 of 24. And 10 of 47 rabbits (21.3%) died within 3 months of surgery; the authors related outcome to the rabbit's physical status at presentation (anorexia, emaciation, anemia, and dehydration).

Mäkitaipale and colleagues (2022): a prospective series that includes routine spays

This Finnish study is the one I find most useful for the prevention question, because most of the rabbits were not suspected of having uterine disease. Mäkitaipale, Airas, Engblom, and Linden prospectively collected the reproductive tracts of 116 rabbits and examined them histologically.

  • In the 94 rabbits without suspected uterine disease (mean age 2.6 years), 41 (44%) had histologic findings and 12 (13%) had neoplasms.
  • Thirteen of those 94 rabbits (14%) had a uterus that looked normal at surgery but was abnormal on histology. The findings included adenoma and carcinoma in situ.
  • Only 1 of 23 rabbits younger than 1 year had a histologic finding.
  • In the 22 rabbits with suspected uterine disease, 21 had abnormal findings.
  • Across all rabbits of known age (n = 110), malignant uterine neoplasia was found in 40% of those older than 3 years, compared with 3.3% of those younger than 3 years (odds ratio 19.3; 95% CI, 4.2 to 88.3). The confidence interval is wide, which fits the sample size.

The authors concluded that OVH is preferred over OVE, at least in older rabbits, and that the gross appearance of the uterus does not exclude disease. The authors list their limitations plainly: the sample was smaller than planned despite active recruitment, and routine neutering of female rabbits is uncommon in Finland, which shaped who was enrolled.

Bertram and colleagues (2021): necropsy and biopsy data from Berlin

Bertram and colleagues re-evaluated pet rabbit tumors from necropsies, postmortem tissue samples, and surgical biopsies submitted between 1995 and 2019. In necropsies, uterine tumors were found in 117 of 894 intact females (13.1%; 95% CI, 10.9% to 15.6%), and adenocarcinoma made up 75.6% of uterine tumors across sample types. Prevalence of uterine neoplasia increased steadily with age, and the authors describe uterine adenocarcinoma as very rare in rabbits younger than 2.5 years. Metastasis was found in 46 of 98 necropsy cases of uterine adenocarcinoma (47%).

Bertram's group adds a note of caution about neutering that I think is fair to repeat. Neutered females had the highest overall tumor prevalence in their data, but they were also older, and the authors state that this should not be read as evidence that neutering lengthens life. They call for studies of how neutering affects life expectancy and overall welfare in female rabbits.

What these numbers mean together

Each of these studies has a different denominator: hospital questionnaires about rabbits that had surgery, referral case series, a prospective series of routine and diagnostic spays, and a pathology archive. None of them tells you the lifetime risk for a pet doe in Middle Tennessee. What they agree on is that uterine disease becomes more common with age, that it is uncommon in the first year and rare in the first 2 years, that a large share of affected does have no outward signs, that hematuria is a common but not universal presentation, and that adenocarcinoma metastasizes, most often to the lungs.

I have written separately about pulmonary masses in rabbits and the uterus as the primary.

Hormones and uterine adenocarcinoma

Uterine adenocarcinoma occurs in intact does, its prevalence rises with age, and it travels with endometrial hyperplasia and with mammary changes in many of these series. That pattern is the reason I think of it as a disease driven by female reproductive hormones, and the reason removing the ovaries is the core of prevention.

The receptor data are more complicated than that summary. Walter and colleagues cite Asakawa and colleagues, who found estrogen and progesterone receptors in 59.3% of normal uteri and 68.4% of hyperplastic uteri. In that work, 80.8% of papillary adenocarcinomas were negative for both receptors, while 93.8% of tubular or solid adenocarcinomas were positive. Mäkitaipale and colleagues make the same point: papillary adenocarcinomas appear to lose hormone receptors early, so hormones may not be needed for the growth of every adenocarcinoma type once it is established, while more than 80% of cystic hyperplasia in the cited work was estrogen receptor positive. Bertram and colleagues also note that uterine adenocarcinomas often lack receptor immunoreactivity, depending on growth pattern.

My reading is that hormonal exposure over time sets the stage for hyperplasia and neoplasia, which is why early gonadectomy is protective, but that receptor status in an established tumor does not tell you much about how to prevent one. I would not claim that every uterine adenocarcinoma is hormone dependent once it exists.

Anatomy that changes the surgery

The rabbit reproductive tract is not a small cat uterus, and several features matter at surgery.

  • Two cervices. The rabbit uterus is completely paired. Each horn ends in its own cervix, and the two cervices open separately into the vagina (Capello 2005; Olson and Bruce 1986). There is no uterine body in the sense that a dog or cat has one.
  • A long, flaccid vagina. The vagina is a single, long, wide, thin-walled structure. The urethra opens into its caudoventral part, caudal to where a ligature for a vaginectomy is placed, and urine can reflux into the vaginal body (Capello 2005).
  • A fat-filled broad ligament with large vessels. The mesometrium carries a lot of fat, and the uterine vessels run within it. Capello describes visualizing these vessels through the fat and ligating them individually.
  • Ovaries without a true bursa, and a mesovarium that can be fatty in older or overweight does, which makes hemoclip placement harder (Capello 2005).
  • A thin-walled bladder. Olson and Bruce warn that the bladder is thin walled and usually distended, so the linea alba has to be incised with care.

Capello also points out that terms like "pyometra" or "uterine stump disease" carry over poorly, because what is left after an OVH in a rabbit is a vaginal body, not a uterine stump. In his example, a 7-year-old doe with purulent vaginitis had a vaginal body so distended it resembled the bladder.

OVE in rabbits

Ovariectomy has real advantages. It is shorter and less invasive than OVH, with a smaller incision and less tissue handling, and in my observation Europe is moving toward OVE in veterinary patients more broadly. For a young doe, those advantages are worth taking seriously.

The evidence on OVE in rabbits, though, is thin, and here is how I would summarize it.

  • Efficacy for preventing uterine disease is not established. Capello described the efficacy of OVE in preventing uterine disease in rabbits as controversial and supported by anecdotal reports, called for clinical trials, and wrote that in his experience OVE works best before 9 to 12 months of age. I did not find a controlled study of long-term uterine outcomes after OVE in rabbits in the literature I reviewed for this post.
  • There are anecdotal failures. Van Zeeland's 2017 review notes that ovariectomy before 6 months of age, or breeding at a young age, have been suggested as ways to reduce risk, but that adenocarcinoma has been reported anecdotally in rabbits despite these measures (cited as personal communication). The same review recommends OVH, preferably in the first year of life.
  • Subclinical disease is already there in older does. Mäkitaipale and colleagues found histologic abnormalities in 44% of rabbits without suspected disease, and 14% had a grossly normal uterus with abnormal histology. An OVE in those rabbits leaves that tissue in place. The authors raise the concern that subclinical disease could progress after OVE and note that follow-up research is lacking. They cite Divers as limiting OVE to very young rabbits, under 6 months.

In practice, the age at surgery matters more than anything else in this decision. An OVE in a 4-month-old doe and an OVE in a 4-year-old doe are different operations from a disease standpoint, because the older uterus may already be abnormal.

My concern about adrenal disease

The reason I generally recommend a full OVH with partial vaginectomy, rather than OVE, is a concern about adrenal disease. I want to be clear that this concern is my own reasoning. It has not been proven, and I am not aware of any study that tests it.

The reasoning runs like this. Adrenal tissue in rabbits can produce sex steroids. Fecteau and colleagues measured progesterone, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, androstenedione, testosterone, and cortisol in 29 healthy rabbits neutered before 1 year of age and found no significant differences between neutered males and females; progestins and androgens were measurable in both. Baine and colleagues described a 10-year-old doe, spayed in her first year, with an adrenal cortical adenoma, an enlarged clitoris, behavior changes, and elevated progesterone, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, and testosterone. Lennox and Chitty described two neutered males, 7 and 8 years old and both castrated in their first year, with adrenal hyperplasia or neoplasia and high testosterone. Baine and colleagues and van Zeeland both describe the hypothesis, borrowed from ferrets, that gonadectomy removes negative feedback and leaves chronically elevated luteinizing hormone acting on the adrenal cortex. Baine's group also cites earlier work in which juvenile does continued sexual behavior after ovariectomy, and the behavior decreased after adrenalectomy.

If a doe has had an OVE and later develops an adrenal tumor that secretes progestins or other sex steroids, her uterus is still there to respond. That is the scenario I worry about. What the literature does not show is any case of uterine disease caused this way, or any data on how often it would happen. Adrenal disease in rabbits is reported infrequently, males are overrepresented about 5 to 1 among published cases, and Baine's group gives an average age at onset of 7.6 years. Most cases are in older rabbits. The youngest adrenal case I have consulted on was about 4 years old.

So I weigh a theoretical and unquantified risk against the extra time and tissue handling of an OVH. For most does, I come down on the side of OVH with partial vaginectomy. A colleague who prefers OVE in a young doe is making a defensible choice with the same evidence, and the data that would settle it do not exist yet.

Why partial vaginectomy

Because each horn has its own cervix and the vagina is long and flaccid, a standard OVH that ligates across the cervices leaves the vaginal body behind. Capello recommends a true ovariohysterovaginectomy in rabbits for this reason, removing the cervices with part of the vaginal body, and describes a reduced risk of disease in the vaginal body with that technique. Van Zeeland notes that the cervix should be resected when operating on a rabbit with adenocarcinoma, because tumors have been reported to recur when uterine tissue is left behind.

Taking the cervices and a portion of the vagina means that no uterine tissue remains for an adrenal or other hormone source to act on, and no cervical tissue remains to recur from.

How I do the surgery

Technique varies among surgeons, and what follows is how I approach it, with the published technique noted where it applies.

  • Ovarian pedicles. I typically use ligatures because they are easy and reliable in my hands. Many surgeons use hemoclips. Capello lists cautery, hemoclips, or a 3-0 absorbable ligature as options for the ovarian artery, and notes that hemoclips are harder to apply when the mesovarium is fatty.
  • The broad ligament. Never tear the broad ligament in a rabbit. There is typically a large vessel running in it, so I ligate it rather than breaking it down by hand. This is a bit different from other species. Capello likewise describes visualizing the uterine vessels within the fat of the broad ligament and ligating them with 3-0 absorbable suture.
  • Cervices and vagina. The vaginal body is ligated or clamped caudal to both cervices and cranial to the urethral opening, and the tract is removed with the cervices and part of the vagina. The urethra enters the vagina caudal to that point and is not usually a surgical concern, but the bladder sits close by and should be kept in view.
  • The vaginal stump. Because the vagina is wide and flaccid, the stump has to be closed carefully. Capello describes a continuous pattern with 3-0 or 4-0 absorbable suture to prevent urine reflux into the abdomen, or two transfixing sutures in small rabbits.

Before and after surgery in older does

In an older intact doe, the spay may be a cancer surgery as much as a preventive one, and the published series support planning for that.

  • Imaging before surgery. Walter and colleagues found uterine abnormalities on ultrasound in all 35 rabbits examined, and Saito's group found ultrasound more sensitive than palpation. Thoracic imaging is worth discussing before surgery, because finding pulmonary metastases changes the prognosis conversation and sometimes the plan.
  • Histology. Settai, Walter, and Mäkitaipale all submitted the tract for histology, and Mäkitaipale's data show why: 14% of rabbits without suspected disease had a grossly normal uterus with abnormal histology. A grossly normal uterus in an older doe is not proof of a normal uterus.
  • Physical status matters. Saito's group linked deaths within 3 months of surgery to anorexia, emaciation, anemia, and dehydration at presentation. Stabilizing a sick doe before surgery is part of the plan.
  • Follow-up. Pulmonary metastases appeared 2 to 8 months after OVH in Walter's series. Van Zeeland's review cites a report of more than 80% of rabbits alive 6 months after OVH when metastases were not present, and periodic rechecks for metastases are recommended.

Timing

Published recommendations range widely. Mäkitaipale and colleagues cite the Rabbit Welfare Association and Fund and the British Veterinary Zoological Society as recommending neutering at 3 to 6 months. Settai's group recommends OVH before 2 years, preferably at 6 to 9 months. Walter's group and van Zeeland recommend surgery in the first year. Capello's experience is that OVE works best before 9 to 12 months, and he prefers OVH with vaginectomy after that age.

The disease data line up with those recommendations: uterine disease was rare in rabbits under 1 year in the Finnish series, very rare under 2.5 years in Bertram's data, and became common after 3 years. Settai's 6-month-old Netherland Dwarf with adenocarcinoma is a reminder that rare is not never. The right age for an individual doe depends on her size, breed, and health, and an older doe can still benefit from surgery after a careful workup.

What we still do not know

  • Long-term uterine outcomes after OVE compared with OVH in pet rabbits, followed for years rather than months.
  • Whether adrenal disease after gonadectomy ever drives disease in a uterus left in place after OVE, and if so, how often.
  • The true lifetime risk of uterine adenocarcinoma in pet does, from a population that is not selected by surgery, referral, or necropsy.
  • How neutering affects life expectancy and overall welfare in female rabbits, which Bertram's group identifies as an open question.
