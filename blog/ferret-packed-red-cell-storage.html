Banking Ferret Packed Red Cells: What a 28-Day Storage Study Shows

October 28, 2026

In most practices, a ferret transfusion happens the same way. An anemic ferret is on the table, someone starts calling around for a healthy donor, and the blood goes in within hours of collection. That works, but it depends on luck and on how many ferret families you know.

A new paper in Veterinary Record asks the obvious next question. If we collected ferret blood ahead of time, how long would the red cells stay usable in the fridge?

What they did

Alsac and colleagues collected 15 mL of blood monthly from four healthy adult male ferrets over six months, for 24 samples in total. Packed red cells were separated by centrifugation and stored at 4 °C in vacuum tubes containing CPDA (citrate phosphate dextrose adenine).

Each week for 28 days they ran bacterial cultures, pH, glucose, lactate, electrolytes, a CBC, and red cell morphology. They used equivalence testing to judge change over time. Plasma was also stored at -18 °C and checked for stability.

What they found

The storage lesion looked a lot like what we know from dogs and cats. Over the 28 days, glucose and pH fell while lactate and potassium rose. MCV went up and MCHC went down. By day 28 there was marked echinocytosis.

Most cultures grew nothing. Serratia marcescens and Microbacterium spp. turned up in some samples, and the authors judged them to be contaminants introduced after collection. The frozen plasma stayed stable.

Based on the biochemical, hematologic, and morphologic indices, the authors concluded that ferret packed red cells in CPDA can be stored for up to 21 days at 4 °C.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first real attempt to put numbers on ferret red cell storage, and the monthly collection design is useful in its own right. It shows that repeated donation from the same healthy ferrets was practical over half a year.

The limits are worth spelling out before anyone builds a blood bank on it:

  • Four donors, all male. That is a small and narrow group.
  • Vacuum tubes, not purpose-made blood bags, and samples were diluted for some biochemistry. The authors flag both.
  • Hemolysis was not measured, and neither was in vivo red cell survival or transfusion efficacy. Those are the outcomes we actually care about at the cage side.
  • The culture results are a reminder that contamination happens. A 21-day window only helps if the collection was clean.

The authors say plainly that more work is needed before clinical blood-banking recommendations can be made. I agree with them.

What it means in practice

We perform ferret blood transfusions at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital, but for most of us, this paper will not change what happens tomorrow. It does change the conversation about what is reasonable. If you have a ferret-heavy practice and a pool of healthy donors, short-term refrigerated storage of packed red cells looks biochemically defensible for up to three weeks, with the caveat that we don't yet know how those cells perform once they are inside the patient.

A few things I would keep in mind:

  • Older units carry more potassium and lactate. That matters more in a small, unstable patient. If you have a choice, use the freshest unit.
  • Aseptic collection is not optional. The contaminants in this study are a fair warning.
  • Blood typing and crossmatch questions do not go away. Ferrets are often described as lacking clinically significant blood groups. Manning and Bell found no blood group antibodies in domestic ferrets, but a stored unit is still a foreign product. Monitor the first transfusion closely regardless.
  • Frozen plasma looked stable. That is a reasonable thing to explore further for ferrets with coagulopathy or protein loss.
  • Find the cause of the anemia. Transfusion buys time. Hyperestrogenism in intact jills, GI bleeding, lymphoma, and chronic disease still need a workup.

How I would use this

If a practice asked me whether to start storing ferret packed red cells, I would say it is a reasonable pilot project with clear rules. Collect aseptically into a closed system, label the date, discard at 21 days, and record what happens to each patient that receives stored blood. Those records are exactly the clinical data this field is missing.

For the occasional anemic ferret, fresh blood from a screened donor is still the simplest path.

Discussion questions

  1. Does your practice keep a donor list for ferrets, and how do you screen donors?
  2. Would you transfuse a 21-day-old unit into a critically ill ferret if it were the only blood available?
  3. What would you want measured in the next study before you trusted stored ferret red cells?
