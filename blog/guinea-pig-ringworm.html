Ringworm in Guinea Pigs: What It Looks Like and What to Do

November 9, 2026

You pick up your guinea pig for evening snuggles and notice a bald, flaky patch on the bridge of the nose. A few days later there's another one near an ear. Then someone in the house has an itchy red circle on their arm.

That pattern is a common way ringworm announces itself in guinea pigs. The good news is that it's treatable. It just takes some patience, and a little housework.

It isn't a worm

Ringworm is a fungal infection of the skin and hair. The name comes from the ring-shaped rash it often causes in people. In guinea pigs, the most common causes are fungi in the Trichophyton group, and some of these are well known for passing from guinea pigs to people.

What to look for

Ringworm in guinea pigs usually starts on the head. Watch for:

  • Bald patches, often on the nose, around the eyes, or on the ears
  • Dry, flaky skin or crusts in those areas
  • Broken hairs at the edge of a patch
  • Patches that slowly spread to the back or legs

Some guinea pigs scratch at the spots and some don't seem bothered at all. An itch-free guinea pig can still be contagious.

Other problems can look similar, including mites, barbering (when cage mates chew each other's fur), and hair loss from hormone changes in females. That's one reason testing matters.

Can I catch ringworm from my guinea pig?

Yes, you can. Children are often the first in the house to show it, because they tend to hold guinea pigs close to their faces. On people it usually looks like an itchy, scaly, red ring.

If anyone in your home has a rash like that, please have them see their own doctor. Let your doctor know you have a guinea pig with skin problems. It helps them choose the right treatment.

Other pets can get it too, so let us know about every animal in your household.

How we diagnose it

The black light test you may have heard about isn't very helpful in guinea pigs, because the fungi that usually infect them don't glow. Instead, I usually brush the coat with a clean toothbrush and send it to the lab for a fungal culture. It takes a while for the fungus to grow, so expect to wait for results. Sometimes a skin scraping or a small biopsy is needed too.

Please check with your vet before using any cream or spray at home. Some products aren't safe for small pets, and starting treatment before testing can make results harder to read.

How it's treated

Treatment depends on how much skin is involved. A single small patch may only need a medicated cream. Guinea pigs with several patches usually need an oral antifungal medicine, often for several weeks. We recheck with another culture, and we want to see clean results more than once before we call it cured.

Cleaning the environment is just as important as the medicine. Fungal spores live in shed hair and skin flakes, so:

  • Wash fleece liners and fabric hides often, in hot water
  • Vacuum around the cage and wipe hard surfaces regularly
  • Replace items that are hard to clean, like wooden hides or chewed toys
  • Wash your hands after handling your guinea pig or cleaning the cage

It's tedious. It also makes the difference between a case that clears and one that keeps coming back.

Preventing it next time

Ringworm often arrives with a new guinea pig. If you're adding to your herd, keep the newcomer separate for a few weeks and have them examined before introductions. Healthy skin also depends on good care overall, so a proper diet with hay, fresh greens, and enough vitamin C helps your guinea pig fight off infections.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your guinea pig:

  • Stops eating or seems quiet and hunched
  • Has skin that is raw, bleeding, or oozing
  • Is scratching so hard it's causing wounds

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Bald or flaky patches anywhere, especially on the face
  • New patches during treatment
  • Anyone in the family with an itchy ring-shaped rash
  • A new guinea pig that hasn't had a checkup yet
