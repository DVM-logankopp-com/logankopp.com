Rabbit Eye Disease by Age, Sex, and Breed

November 2, 2026

Rabbit eyes tend to get a quick look with a penlight and not much more, unless the rabbit comes in for an eye problem. On a busy day it is an easy shortcut to take. A large retrospective series from a referral ophthalmology service makes a good case for a more deliberate eye exam, especially in certain rabbits.

The study in brief

Ohara and colleagues reviewed records from 205 client-owned rabbits (297 eyes) seen at a referral ophthalmology section of a single clinic between 2007 and 2022. They looked at which diseases were most common and how age, sex, and breed affected the odds of each one.

The four most common conditions were cataracts, glaucoma, uveitis, and corneal ulcers.

Three patterns worth remembering

1. One diagnosis often isn't the whole story

About a third of rabbits (31.7%) and about a quarter of eyes (26.9%) had more than one disease in the same eye. The most common pairing was cataract with uveitis.

That fits what I see. When a young rabbit comes in with a red, cloudy eye, I want to know what the lens looks like. Phacoclastic uveitis from Encephalitozoon cuniculi is a well-recognized cause in rabbits, and treating the uveitis without addressing the lens usually means it keeps coming back.

2. Age changes the differential list

Uveitis was more common in young rabbits (median age 1 year). Corneal ulcers were more common in older rabbits (median age 4 years). Glaucoma also became more likely with age.

For the older rabbit with an ulcer, I look for a reason the cornea broke down. The paper doesn't address cause, so this part is my clinical approach rather than the study's findings. Dental disease, nasolacrimal problems, eyelid abnormalities, and reduced blinking from facial nerve disease all go on my list, and a rabbit that can't groom its face well because of arthritis or obesity may also be at risk.

3. Holland Lops and neutered males deserve tonometry

In Holland Lops, glaucoma and cataracts made up more than half of the eye disease seen. Compared with neutered males, glaucoma was less frequent in intact males (OR 0.295) and in spayed females (OR 0.163).

The neutered-male finding is an association, not a mechanism. It could reflect something hormonal, or it could reflect which rabbits happened to be referred. Either way, it gives me a practical rule: older Holland Lops and older neutered males are the rabbits where I want an intraocular pressure reading, not just a look.

Strengths and limitations

This is a large dataset for rabbit ophthalmology, collected over roughly 15 years by an ophthalmology service, which should mean consistent diagnoses.

The limits are the usual ones for a referral retrospective:

  • Referral populations skew toward harder or more advanced cases, so the proportions won't match a general practice caseload.
  • Breed representation reflects local popularity at one clinic. A breed that looks overrepresented may just be common in that area.
  • Records-based work can't show cause. The sex and age associations need confirmation in other populations.
  • The abstract doesn't describe treatment or outcomes, so it can't tell us how these eyes did.

What I'd change in the exam room

Make tonometry routine for high-risk rabbits. A rebound tonometer is quick and well tolerated by most rabbits without sedation. Rabbit intraocular pressure varies with handling and time of day, so I compare both eyes and repeat a reading if something looks off.

Stain every red or squinting eye. Fluorescein is cheap, and corneal ulcers in older rabbits are common enough in this series that I don't want to miss one.

Look at the lens in every young rabbit with uveitis. Dilate if you can. If there is a cataract or a suspected lens capsule rupture, talk with the family about E. cuniculi and about referral to an ophthalmologist, since lens removal may be the definitive fix.

Think about the whole rabbit. Eye disease in rabbits often travels with dental disease, E. cuniculi, or problems with grooming. A skull radiograph or a dental exam under sedation may be part of the eye workup.

For discussion

  1. Do you check intraocular pressure in rabbits as part of a wellness exam, or only when there is an eye complaint?
  2. How do you decide when a rabbit with phacoclastic uveitis should go to an ophthalmologist?
  3. What is your first test for an older rabbit with a nonhealing corneal ulcer?

Citation

Ohara T, Kato K, Miwa Y, Nakata M, et al. A retrospective study of ophthalmic diseases in companion rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus): 297 eyes from 205 rabbits (2007 to 2022). Vet Ophthalmol. 2026 (epub ahead of print). doi:10.1111/vop.70290
