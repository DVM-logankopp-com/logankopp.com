Pulmonary Masses in Rabbits: When the Uterus Is the Primary

October 21, 2026

A lung mass on a rabbit radiograph or necropsy table raises an immediate question: infection, or cancer? In older intact does, that question has a familiar answer more often than many of us expect. A 2026 retrospective necropsy study from Subash and colleagues is a useful reminder to look at the uterus before we assume a primary lung tumor.

The study

Subash et al. reviewed 608 rabbit necropsies performed between May 2019 and December 2025. Twelve rabbits had pulmonary masses. Most were older than three years, and 10 of the 12 were female.

Half of the masses were non-neoplastic: abscesses, granulomatous inflammation, or bronchopneumonia. The other half were neoplastic. That puts neoplastic lung-mass prevalence at 0.98% (6/608) in this necropsy population. Of those six neoplastic cases, five were metastatic uterine adenocarcinoma. Only one was a primary lung tumor.

The abstract does not state whether these were pet or farmed rabbits, so the absolute prevalence may not match a companion-animal caseload. The pattern still matters for clinical practice.

What this means in practice

  • In an older intact doe, a lung mass is more likely metastasis than a primary lung tumor. Uterine adenocarcinoma is already high on our differential for intact females. This series reinforces that thoracic disease may be the first visible tip of that iceberg.
  • Abscess and granuloma can look the same as neoplasia on gross exam. Six of twelve masses were inflammatory. Do not treat a lung nodule as cancer on imaging alone.
  • Thoracic imaging before ovariohysterectomy in older intact does is worth discussing. Finding pulmonary disease changes staging, prognosis talk with the family, and whether surgery is still the right next step for comfort or local control.
  • Submit tissue for histopathology. Whether you are sampling a lung mass or removing a uterus, definitive diagnosis drives follow-up. Gross appearance is not enough.
  • Intact status and age matter in the history. Most affected rabbits in this series were over three years old and female. Ask about reproductive status early when thoracic findings appear.

Strengths and limitations

The strength is sample size: 608 necropsies give a clearer sense of how often we actually see pulmonary masses, and how often neoplasia is metastatic from the uterus rather than primary. The split between neoplastic and non-neoplastic disease is clinically useful.

Limitations are real. This is a necropsy series, so it overrepresents animals that died or were euthanized with significant disease. The abstract does not separate pet from production rabbits, and we do not get imaging correlation, clinical signs, or survival data from this summary. A single primary lung tumor among six neoplastic cases is too few to characterize primary pulmonary neoplasia in rabbits.

How I would use this

When I see a pulmonary mass or nodule in an older intact doe, I treat uterine adenocarcinoma metastasis as the leading neoplastic differential until histopathology says otherwise. I still keep abscess and granulomatous disease on the list, because half of the masses in this series were non-neoplastic.

Before elective OHE in a senior intact doe, I discuss thoracic radiographs or CT when available, especially if there are any respiratory signs, weight loss, or reduced activity. If we find lung disease, I am honest about staging uncertainty until we have tissue. If we proceed to surgery, the uterus and any accessible lesions go for histopathology.

For colleagues managing similar cases, the practical sequence is: reproductive history, thoracic imaging, plan surgery or medical management based on the whole picture, and never skip histopathology on excised tissue.

Citation

Subash A, Dhanush Krishna B, Naidile KR, et al. Pathology of pulmonary masses in rabbits: a retrospective study of 608 rabbit necropsy cases. J Vet Anim Sci. 2026;57(3):408-413. doi:10.51966/jvas.2026.57.3.408-413
