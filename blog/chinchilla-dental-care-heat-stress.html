Chinchilla Dental Care and Heat Stress: Two Things Every Owner Should Know

October 2026

Chinchillas are soft, quick and full of energy, and they can live a long time with good care. Two of the biggest threats to their health are dental disease and overheating. Both are largely preventable, and Middle Tennessee summers make the second one especially important.

Part 1: Chinchilla teeth

Teeth that never stop growing

All of a chinchilla's teeth grow continuously throughout life. Constant chewing on hay and safe chew items keeps them worn to the right length. When the diet doesn't provide enough chewing, or when genetics or injury play a role, teeth can overgrow, form sharp edges, or develop root problems.

Dental disease is one of the most common health problems in pet chinchillas, and it's often hard to spot at home until it is advanced. That's why a thorough dental exam is part of every annual chinchilla checkup.

Signs of dental problems

  • Wet chin fur or drooling
  • Dropping food or avoiding hard foods
  • Weight loss, sometimes while still seeming to eat
  • Smaller or fewer droppings
  • Watery eyes or squinting, which can happen when tooth roots press on the tear ducts
  • Overgrown front teeth, or front teeth that look pale or white instead of orange
  • Swelling along the jaw

Prevention

  • Unlimited, good-quality grass hay such as timothy. This is the single most important part of dental health.
  • A plain chinchilla pellet, not a mix with seeds, nuts, corn or colorful pieces
  • Very few sugary treats. Fruit only in tiny amounts.
  • Safe chew items such as untreated wood and pumice
  • Regular weigh-ins at home on a kitchen scale
  • Annual exams that include a dental check

Treatment

Diagnosis usually involves a careful oral exam under sedation and dental X-rays. Treatment may include trimming and reshaping overgrown teeth, treating infection, extracting diseased teeth, pain control and nutritional support. Dental disease often needs ongoing management with regular rechecks.

Part 2: Heat stress

Why chinchillas overheat

Chinchillas come from the cool, dry Andes Mountains. Their fur is incredibly dense, which is great for staying warm but makes it very hard to lose heat. Chinchillas also can't sweat. A warm, humid room can quickly become dangerous.

Keeping your chinchilla cool

  • Keep the room cool. Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital's care guide recommends keeping temperatures at or below about 75°F, with low humidity.
  • Keep the cage out of direct sunlight and away from windows, heaters and stuffy rooms.
  • Have a plan for power outages or AC failures during summer.
  • Offer cooling options such as a chilled granite or marble tile in the cage.
  • Never leave a chinchilla in a parked car, and plan travel during cooler times of day with good air conditioning.
  • Avoid wetting the fur to cool them, since their coat dries very slowly. Dust baths, not water baths, keep the coat clean.

Signs of heat stress

  • Lying stretched out, limp or weak
  • Rapid or open-mouth breathing
  • Bright red ears
  • Drooling or wet fur around the mouth
  • Restlessness, then lethargy
  • Collapse or seizures

Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency. If you suspect it, move your chinchilla to a cool, shaded area, place them on a cool (not icy) surface, and call your veterinarian or an emergency clinic right away.

When to call your vet

Seek care immediately if your chinchilla:

  • Shows any signs of heat stress
  • Hasn't eaten in about half a day, or has stopped producing droppings
  • Is breathing with effort or with an open mouth
  • Is hunched, unresponsive or having seizures

Book a prompt visit for wet chin fur, weight loss, dropping food, eye discharge or fur loss.
