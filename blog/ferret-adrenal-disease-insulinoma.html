Ferret Adrenal Disease and Insulinoma: The Basics for Owners

October 2026

Ferrets are playful, curious and full of personality. As they reach middle age, though, two hormone-related diseases become very common: adrenal disease and insulinoma. Many ferrets have one or even both. Knowing the signs helps you get your ferret help early.

Adrenal disease

What it is

The adrenal glands are two small glands near the kidneys. In ferrets with adrenal disease, one or both glands become enlarged (from overgrowth or a tumor) and produce too many sex hormones.

Signs to watch for

  • Hair loss, often starting at the tail and spreading over the hips, back and sides
  • Itchy skin
  • A swollen vulva in spayed females
  • Difficulty urinating or straining in males, caused by an enlarged prostate. This can become an emergency.
  • Return of mating behavior, aggression or a stronger musky odor
  • Thinning skin, muscle loss or low energy

Treatment

Options depend on your ferret's age and health and which gland is affected:

  • Hormone implants can calm the overactive glands and control signs. They manage the disease rather than cure it, and need to be repeated over time.
  • Surgery to remove an affected adrenal gland may be recommended for some ferrets. Dr. Kopp performs adrenal surgery in ferrets at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital.

Insulinoma

What it is

Insulinomas are tumors of the pancreas that release too much insulin, causing low blood sugar (hypoglycemia). It's the opposite of diabetes. Insulinoma is one of the most common diseases in middle-aged and older ferrets.

Signs to watch for

  • Lethargy or sleeping more than usual
  • Staring into space or seeming "spaced out"
  • Weakness of the back legs, wobbling or stumbling
  • Drooling or pawing at the mouth, which can look like nausea
  • Collapse or seizures in severe episodes

Signs often come and go, and they're more common after exercise or a long time without food.

Diagnosis and treatment

Your vet will check blood glucose. Because home glucometers aren't always accurate in ferrets, low or borderline readings are best confirmed with laboratory testing. Ultrasound may help look for tumors.

Treatment options include:

  • Surgery to remove pancreatic tumors when possible
  • Medications to help keep blood sugar up, with regular monitoring
  • Diet management: frequent small meals of a high-protein, low-sugar ferret diet, and avoiding sugary treats

With good management, many ferrets with insulinoma enjoy a good quality of life.

What to do during a low-sugar episode

If your ferret is weak or wobbly, your vet may advise rubbing a small amount of honey or syrup on the gums, then offering their normal food once they're alert, and calling the clinic. Don't put anything in the mouth of a ferret that is seizing or unconscious. Get emergency help right away.

Can these diseases be prevented?

There's no guaranteed prevention. Regular wellness exams, a high-quality ferret diet without sugary treats, and paying attention to small changes help catch problems early, when there are more treatment options.

When to call your vet

Call immediately if your ferret:

  • Collapses, has a seizure, or can't be roused
  • Is straining to urinate or producing little or no urine (especially males)

Schedule a prompt visit if your ferret:

  • Is losing hair, itchy, or has a swollen vulva
  • Has episodes of staring, weakness or drooling
  • Is less active or eating less

A weak or collapsed ferret should be discussed with the clinic by phone before you put them in the car.
