Rhabdomyosarcoma in a Rabbit: A Pathologic Fracture With a Rare Cause

November 2, 2026

An old rabbit that suddenly won't bear weight on a hind leg usually gets radiographs and a fracture plan. Sometimes the films tell you the fracture is the second problem, not the first.

This is a case my co-authors and I reported in 2024. At the time, we could find only two previously confirmed cases of rhabdomyosarcoma in rabbits.

The paper

Kopp L, Garner M, Priest K. Rhabdomyosarcoma in a rabbit. Top Companion Anim Med. 2024;61:100892. PMID: 38972503. doi:10.1016/j.tcam.2024.100892

Case presentation

A 12-year-old neutered male Holland Lop presented for sudden right hindlimb lameness. He weighed 1.67 kg with a body condition score of 4/9. He was non-weight-bearing on the right hindlimb and painful on palpation of the distal femur.

Imaging and staging

Under sedation with midazolam and buprenorphine, we took three-view thoracic, two-view abdominal, and two-view hindlimb radiographs. The lateral limb film showed moth-eaten lytic change of the distal femur and femoral condyles, with a comminuted fracture at the proximal margin of that lesion. Lysis plus a fracture in a 12-year-old rabbit is a pathologic fracture until proven otherwise.

Thoracic and abdominal radiographs showed no evidence of metastasis. The CBC showed a total white cell count of 7.39 × 10⁹/L with relative heterophilia, lymphopenia, and monocytosis, which we attributed to secondary inflammation. The chemistry panel was unremarkable.

We offered CT for a more sensitive look for metastasis in the chest and abdomen and a better view of the limb. The family declined, so "no overt metastasis on radiographs" is the accurate description of his staging.

Amputation

Amputation was scheduled for the next day. Until then he received multimodal analgesia (buprenorphine, gabapentin, meloxicam), enrofloxacin, and strict cage rest. Food and water were not withheld overnight. Food came out of the cage only 2 hours before surgery, and water stayed available.

We performed a routine coxofemoral amputation under isoflurane with a supraglottic airway and a lidocaine-ketamine CRI. The CRI was halved at the start of skin closure, halved again after extubation, and kept running until he was awake, moving, and eating. Recovery was uneventful.

After surgery he continued the same medications, with a commercial herbivore critical care diet by syringe every 6 hours until he showed real interest in his regular food. At rechecks on days 7 and 14 he was walking normally and eating normally. The family declined referral to a veterinary oncologist for chemotherapy. Meloxicam and gabapentin continued long term.

Histopathology and the IHC panel

Histology showed a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm made of sheets of round, polygonal, and spindloid cells. It infiltrated the periarticular soft tissue and joint capsule, projected into the stifle joint space, and caused lysis of cortical and medullary bone in the distal femur and proximal tibia. The mitotic count was 3 per 10 high-power fields (2.37 mm²), with rare multinucleated cells.

That description fits several tumors. Because the mass involved the periarticular soft tissue, stifle joint, and regional bone, the differentials included:

  • Joint-associated sarcomas: periarticular histiocytic sarcoma, giant cell sarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial myxosarcoma, synovial fibrosarcoma, the entity formerly called "synovial cell sarcoma," and undifferentiated sarcoma
  • Rhabdomyosarcoma
  • Osteosarcoma

Immunohistochemistry is what narrowed it down:

  • Desmin positive in about 70% of neoplastic cells
  • MyoD1 positive in about 90% of neoplastic cells
  • Negative for cytokeratin AE1/AE3, CD204, IBA-1, SMA, and SATB2

Desmin and MyoD1 are markers for rhabdomyosarcoma, and combined with routine histology they can confirm it in rabbits. The negative markers mattered just as much, because they argued against the other entries on the list: SMA against a smooth muscle tumor such as leiomyosarcoma, CD204 and IBA-1 against a histiocytic tumor, SATB2 against osteosarcoma, and cytokeratin against an epithelial component. Put together, the diagnosis was pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma (PRMS).

Without that panel, this tumor could have been reported as an undifferentiated, anaplastic, or poorly differentiated sarcoma. That kind of label is one reason rhabdomyosarcoma may be underrecognized in veterinary species. Transmission electron microscopy is considered the gold standard in dogs and humans, but it is often expensive and hard to access. Routine histology plus IHC is the practical route.

Survival and necropsy

He lived 396 days after amputation. He then presented for inappetence, anorexia, and lethargy. He was hypothermic at 98.5°F and bradycardic at 120 bpm, with a large, painful cranial abdominal mass and a palpable fluid wave. He died despite supportive care.

At necropsy there were about 400 mL of clear, viscous abdominal fluid, 20 mL of similar thoracic fluid, marked hepatomegaly, and an 8 × 6 × 4 cm multilobular, firm white mass invading the liver. Sarcoma was present in the urinary bladder, pericardium, omentum, diaphragm, kidney, lung, and liver. It resembled the original PRMS, now with moderate to marked anisokaryosis, frequent bi- and multinucleated cells, and a mitotic count of about 30 per 2.37 mm². The hip and its bone marrow were histologically normal.

For context, adult human PRMS is highly metastatic and tends to resist conventional chemotherapy. Wide excision with postoperative radiation is the mainstay in people, and one multicentre study reported a median survival of 12.8 months without overt metastasis at diagnosis and 7.1 months with it. Human numbers don't transfer directly to rabbits, and with only a handful of confirmed rabbit cases, we have nothing comparable for our own species yet.

Take-homes for lameness or a mass in an older rabbit

A pathologic fracture is a diagnosis that needs a cause. Lytic, moth-eaten bone around a fracture in a geriatric rabbit should prompt staging and tissue, not just stabilization. PRMS belongs on the differential list, particularly in aged rabbits presenting with pathologic fractures.

Stage before you commit. We used three-view thoracic and two-view abdominal radiographs and offered CT, which is more sensitive for metastasis.

Ask your pathologist for IHC. A "sarcoma" diagnosis on routine stains may not be the end of the story. In this case desmin and MyoD1 named the tumor, and the negative panel ruled out the look-alikes.

Amputation can buy good time. This rabbit walked normally within two weeks and lived more than a year after surgery, on long-term meloxicam and gabapentin.

Set expectations about metastasis. Clear radiographs at diagnosis did not mean the disease was confined to the limb.

This is a single case, so it can't give a prognosis or a best treatment. Which chemotherapy options, if any, help rabbits with PRMS is still an open research question.
