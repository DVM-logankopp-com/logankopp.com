Pasteurella Carriage in Pet Rabbits: Journal Club

November 16, 2026

Pasteurella has been the default explanation for rabbit nasal discharge for as long as most of us have been in practice. Families have often read about "snuffles" before they arrive, and some have already decided the rabbit has it. A small survey from Iran is a good excuse to revisit what a positive Pasteurella result does and doesn't tell us.

The paper

Gharibi D, Borujeni MP, Mosallanejad B, Khoonsari FSA. Molecular characterization, virulence gene profiling and antimicrobial susceptibility of Pasteurella multocida isolated from companion rabbits in southwestern Iran. Vet Med Sci. 2026;12(6):e71323. doi:10.1002/vms3.71323

What they did

The investigators collected nasal and oropharyngeal swabs from 65 companion rabbits. Sixty-two had no apparent clinical abnormalities and 3 had concurrent clinical findings. None had a recorded history of antimicrobial treatment before sampling.

P. multocida was isolated by conventional bacteriology and confirmed by species-specific PCR for the kmt1 gene. Confirmed isolates were capsular typed, LPS genotyped, and screened for virulence-associated genes. Susceptibility was tested by disk diffusion under CLSI-referenced conditions.

What they found

P. multocida was detected in 6 of the 65 rabbits (9.23%):

  • 3 were clinically asymptomatic.
  • 1 had ocular and nasal discharge with respiratory signs.
  • 2 had dental malocclusion with dental abscesses.

Put another way, the organism was found in 3 of the 62 rabbits with no apparent abnormalities, and in all 3 rabbits that had clinical findings. Carriage was not significantly associated with age, sex, or breed.

On typing, 5 isolates (83.3%) were capsular type A and 1 was non-typable. All 6 were LPS genotype L2. The genes sodA, sodC, exbD-tonB, and nanB were present in every isolate. ompH, hgbB, pfhA, and nanH were found in 4 isolates (66.7%), and ptfA, oma87, hgbA, and toxA in 3 (50%).

Disk diffusion produced variable zone diameters across the drugs tested. Because there are no rabbit-specific clinical breakpoints for P. multocida, the authors reported raw inhibition-zone diameters rather than calling isolates susceptible or resistant.

Strengths and limitations

I like the honesty of the susceptibility reporting. Most of us receive rabbit culture reports with S, I, and R printed next to each drug, and it's easy to forget that those categories come from breakpoints set for other species. These authors declined to make that leap.

The limits are real, though. Only 6 rabbits were positive, which is too few to say much about associations with age, sex, or breed, or about which genes matter clinically. The population came from one region of Iran, and strain distribution elsewhere may differ. Detection relied on growing the organism first, with PCR used to confirm isolates. The samples were nasal and oropharyngeal swabs, so the study tells us what these rabbits carried in the upper airway, not what was growing inside the abscesses. Virulence genes were detected, but their presence doesn't establish that a given isolate caused disease in a given rabbit.

What it means in practice

A rabbit can carry P. multocida and look completely normal. Finding it on a nasal swab from a sneezing rabbit is consistent with infection, but it doesn't prove that Pasteurella is the reason the rabbit is sneezing. Dental disease, foreign material, and other bacteria remain on the list, and in rabbits the teeth deserve a look in every case of nasal discharge.

For dental abscesses, a nasal swab is the wrong sample. If the abscess is what you are treating, culture the abscess. Rabbit dental abscesses are often mixed infections, including anaerobes, so a sample from the abscess wall or capsule taken at surgery tells you far more than a swab of the nares or the pus alone.

When the susceptibility report comes back, read it as in vitro data. An "S" built on dog or human breakpoints is a reasonable starting point, but it isn't a guarantee that the drug will reach effective concentrations in a rabbit's skull or nasal cavity.

How I'd use this

  1. Look at the teeth in every rabbit with nasal discharge. Skull radiographs or CT are often needed to see tooth roots that oral exams can't.
  2. Sample the lesion you are treating. Deep nasal culture for rhinitis, abscess wall or capsule for abscesses.
  3. Don't treat a positive result on its own. A Pasteurella-positive rabbit with no clinical signs is a carrier until proven otherwise.
  4. Read susceptibility results with the species in mind, and pick drugs based on the infection site, the rabbit's gut, and how the drug will be given at home.
  5. Fix what you can around the infection. Dusty hay, ammonia from soiled litter, and dental disease all make upper respiratory disease harder to control.

Questions for rounds

  1. Do you culture asymptomatic in-contact rabbits when one rabbit in a household has respiratory disease? What would you do with a positive result?
  2. How does your diagnostic lab handle breakpoints for rabbit isolates, and does the report say so?
  3. In a rabbit with a mandibular abscess, where do you take your culture sample, and when?
