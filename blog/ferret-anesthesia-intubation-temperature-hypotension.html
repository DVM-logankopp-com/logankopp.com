Ferret Anesthesia: Intubate Every Time, and Watch Pressure, Ventilation, and Temperature

November 10, 2026

Of the small mammals we anesthetize routinely, ferrets carry the lowest anesthetic-related risk of death, 0.33% in the study Kapaldo and Eshar cite, compared with 0.17% in dogs and 0.24% in cats. That number can make ferret anesthesia feel routine.

The physiology data in this review argue otherwise. At inhalant doses we use every day, ferrets hypoventilate and become hypotensive, and many of them do it quietly on a face mask during a "quick" procedure.

The article

Kapaldo N, Eshar D. Ferret sedation and anesthesia. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2022;25:273-296. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2021.08.006

This is a narrative review by a veterinary anesthesiologist and an exotic companion mammal clinician. The authors note that few new ferret-specific anesthesia papers appeared in the decade before it, so many recommendations rest on other species and on the authors' experience. Doses below are as the authors list them. Check them against a current formulary.

Before anesthesia

  • Stabilize first. Correct dehydration or hypovolemia before induction. Induction drugs and inhalants unmask even mild volume deficits through vasodilation.
  • Bloodwork. A CBC and chemistry panel is recommended before anesthesia in sick ferrets, though some ferrets need sedation to get it.
  • Short fast. Ferrets have a gastric transit time of about 4 hours and are prone to hypoglycemia. The authors recommend fasting for only 2 to 4 hours.
  • Vascular access. The authors recommend a catheter for any surgical or prolonged diagnostic procedure. Ferret skin is thick, and a small pilot hole with a 20- to 22-gauge needle keeps a 24- or 26-gauge catheter from burring.

Premedication

The authors' common premed is midazolam and butorphanol at 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg each, which gives moderate sedation within about 5 minutes that lasts 30 to 40 minutes. They substitute a full mu agonist for invasive surgery, and they give premeds 10 to 15 minutes before catheter placement.

Opioids can make ferrets nauseous. In the study cited, morphine at 0.1 and 0.3 mg/kg SC caused emesis in 17% and 100% of ferrets, respectively. The authors suggest considering maropitant or ondansetron before hydromorphone or morphine. Maropitant has not been evaluated in ferrets.

Dexmedetomidine causes vasoconstriction and a dose-dependent reflex bradycardia, and the bradycardia and reduced vascular tone can outlast its peripheral effects.

Induction

Induce in a quiet room with intubation equipment ready, and ideally with ECG leads on so you have a baseline rhythm. Preoxygenation helps most in ferrets with pneumonia, intrathoracic disease such as a mediastinal mass or effusion, or a difficult airway.

The induction doses the authors list include propofol 3 to 8 mg/kg IV (never SC or IM), alfaxalone 3 to 5 mg/kg IV, and ketamine 7 to 10 mg/kg with midazolam co-induction if the ferret is not well sedated. I generally give 0.5 mg/kg of propofol or alfaxalone, wait 30 to 60 seconds, then give more as needed, and co-induction with ketamine 0.25 to 1 mg/kg IV helps minimize the propofol or alfaxalone dose. Ketamine gives a more stable hemodynamic profile in a dehydrated or hypotensive ferret. The authors also describe mixing ketamine and propofol in an equal mg/kg ratio and titrating, though that combination hasn't been evaluated in ferrets. In their experience, alfaxalone causes post-induction apnea about as often as propofol.

Mask induction of an unpremedicated ferret is stressful and causes more cardiopulmonary depression. In sick dogs and cats it has been associated with higher odds of anesthetic death than IV induction. Premedicate first if a mask induction is unavoidable.

Intubate every ferret

The authors' position is clear. Ferrets are straightforward to intubate, so intubate whenever general anesthesia is needed, regardless of duration. A tube protects against aspiration, allows ventilation and a high FiO2, reduces staff exposure, and gives you an airway if the ferret arrests.

Their technique notes:

  • Place the ferret sternal, extend the head and neck, pull the tongue rostrally, and visualize the larynx.
  • Laryngospasm is uncommon, but topical lidocaine can be sprayed on the larynx 30 to 60 seconds beforehand. Count the dose. One 0.1 mL spray of 2% lidocaine is 2 mg/kg in a 1 kg ferret.
  • Suggested tube sizes are 2.0 to 3.5 mm uncuffed or 2.5 to 3.5 mm cuffed, depending on body weight. Use a cuffed tube when you can.
  • Advance the tube to the thoracic inlet, and trim it so it ends at or just past the incisors to limit dead space.
  • Tie the tube behind the head and around behind the forelimbs, because the ferret's head and neck shape makes a standard tie slip.
  • A laryngeal mask airway can work but dislodges easily when the ferret is moved.

What isoflurane does to a ferret

The numbers here are the reason to monitor.

Blood pressure. In unpremedicated ferrets, MAP was 75 mm Hg at 0.8 MAC isoflurane, 57 mm Hg at 1 MAC, 38 mm Hg at 1.5 MAC, and 36 mm Hg at 2 MAC. Isoflurane MAC in ferrets is 1.5% to 1.7%, and sevoflurane MAC is 2.6%. Think about what a ferret on a mask at an unknown inspired concentration is likely experiencing during a short procedure.

Ventilation. In spontaneously breathing, intubated ferrets on isoflurane, PaCO2 was 71, 78, 83, and 158 mm Hg at 0.8, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 MAC, even though respiratory rate rose to 25 to 40 breaths per minute. A normal respiratory rate does not mean adequate ventilation. The authors recommend IPPV if a spontaneously breathing ferret can't keep EtCO2 below 55 mm Hg. Ferret lungs are compliant, so a peak inspiratory pressure under 5 cm H2O may deliver an adequate tidal volume. The authors note that 10 to 15 cm H2O can be reached without significant adverse effect, and their ventilation table says not to exceed 20 cm H2O.

PCV. At 1 MAC, isoflurane and sevoflurane dropped PCV by 38% and 43% within 15 minutes through splenic sequestration, and PCV returned to normal 2 hours after recovery. Keep that in mind before you call an anesthetized ferret anemic.

Capnography has limits. A ferret's tidal volume may be 5 to 15 mL, and a sidestream capnograph samples about 50 mL/min. With high fresh gas flow and circuit dead space, the displayed EtCO2 can read lower than the true value. If there's no expiratory plateau, the number is an underestimate. Remove unnecessary adapters and elbows.

Monitoring targets

The authors recommend monitoring ECG, core temperature, pulse oximetry, EtCO2, blood pressure, and depth in every anesthetized ferret. Their targets:

  • Heart rate 180 to 260 bpm
  • Doppler systolic pressure above 90 mm Hg, or MAP above 60 mm Hg
  • EtCO2 35 to 55 mm Hg
  • SpO2 above 93%. The authors use 93%; I aim for about 96%.
  • Temperature 99 to 101 °F

For blood pressure, they describe a Doppler probe on the tail with a #1 neonatal cuff at the tail base as the most consistent indirect method. It underestimates true systolic pressure but tracks trends well. In their hands, standard oscillometric units are not accurate in ferrets. High-definition oscillometry correlated well with direct pressure in one report.

Jaw tone is one of the more useful depth indicators. Ferrets don't show lacrimation or nystagmus at light planes, and the palpebral reflex disappears early.

When pressure drops

The authors call hypotension the most common adverse event in anesthetized ferrets. Their approach:

  1. Check depth first. If the depth is appropriate, don't lighten the ferret further.
  2. Volume deficit? Give 10 to 20 mL/kg of crystalloid over 5 to 10 minutes, knowing the effect may be brief. Hypertonic saline (7.2%, 2 to 4 mL/kg) is an option.
  3. Bradycardia below about 160 bpm? Consider an anticholinergic.
  4. Normal heart rate? Consider a vasopressor or positive inotrope. Ephedrine is their first step, and norepinephrine is their first choice when ephedrine gives little to no response. Dopamine and dobutamine are also listed. I usually go to norepinephrine first, though ephedrine can be used.

Temperature, both ways

Ferrets lose heat fast, and heat loss is fastest in the first hour of anesthesia as heat redistributes from the core to the periphery. No study has measured the incidence of perioperative hypothermia in ferrets, but the authors suspect it approaches or exceeds the reported incidence in cats. They define it as a core temperature below 97.0 °F (36.1 °C). Their recommendations:

  • I monitor core temperature continuously and record it every 5 minutes. The authors' minimum is a check at least every 10 to 15 minutes.
  • Put a barrier between the ferret and any cold surface.
  • Use warm water or forced air blankets, covering as much body surface as possible.
  • Raise the operating room temperature to at least 75 °F (23.9 °C). In one veterinary study, that cut the incidence of hypothermia by about half.
  • Don't use warmed fluid bags, heated grain bags, or a forced air hose aimed at the ferret, because they concentrate heat and cause burns.

The other half is easy to miss. In the authors' experience, perioperative hyperthermia above 102 °F is as common as hypothermia or more so in draped ferrets on active warming. They recommend reducing heat support once core temperature passes about 99 °F, and stopping it entirely if the ferret becomes hyperthermic.

Glucose and blood loss

Measure glucose in every ferret. For ferrets with insulinoma or a history of hypoglycemia, the authors check every 30 to 60 minutes under anesthesia and supplement with 2.5% dextrose at 5 to 10 mL/kg/h to keep glucose between 70 and 150 mg/dL.

Rollins K, Rishniw M, Donnelly TM, Kopp L. Portable blood glucose meters do not reliably detect hypoglycemia in ferrets (Mustela putorius furo) compared with a point-of-care chemistry analyzer. Am J Vet Res. 2026:1-8. doi:10.2460/ajvr.26.05.0224

Ferret blood volume is about 60 mL/kg. The authors suggest a 10% loss is usually tolerated, and that transfusion should be considered as losses approach 20%. Ferrets have no documented blood types, so any healthy ferret can donate. I covered how long ferret packed red cells can be stored earlier this fall.

Regional anesthesia

The authors recommend considering a lumbosacral epidural for invasive procedures of the tail, perineum, pelvic limbs, or abdomen. They give a lidocaine epidural 1 to 2 hours of effect, ropivacaine or bupivacaine 4 to 6 hours, and morphine 12 to 18 hours of segmental analgesia. Watch for urine retention after epidural opioids, and for hypotension from sympathetic blockade when the block spreads cranially for abdominal surgery.

Recovery

Take a final temperature before turning off the inhalant and correct severe derangements first. Redose analgesia so the ferret doesn't wake up painful.

Watch closely, and monitor and record values during recovery, including temperature, mentation, heart rate, respiration, and SpO2. Supplement oxygen if the saturation drops.

Limitations

Much of this review comes from other species or from the authors' experience. The cardiovascular effects of propofol, alfaxalone, and ketamine haven't been measured in ferrets. The opioid MAC-sparing data come from dogs, and the hypothermia incidence figures come from dogs and cats. The isoflurane blood pressure and ventilation data come from small numbers of healthy, unpremedicated ferrets.

How I'd use this

  1. Consider intubating ferrets, even for quick procedures. If you use a mask, watch for hypotension and hypoventilation.
  2. Put the Doppler on the tail before induction. Watching the trend matters more than any single reading.
  3. Treat warming as a dial, not a switch. Turn it down as the ferret warms up.
  4. Check glucose in any middle-aged ferret where insulinoma is on the list, and recheck it during the procedure. I was part of a 2026 study on how well portable glucometers detect hypoglycemia in ferrets.
  5. Keep the pulse oximeter on through recovery.
