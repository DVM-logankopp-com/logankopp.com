Recognizing Pain in Rodents: Grimace Scales, Behavior, and When to Treat

November 10, 2026

Pain is underdiagnosed and undertreated in every species that has been studied, and the limited data we have suggest analgesic use in companion rodents is considerably lower than in cats and dogs. One reason is that rodents are prey species that hide pain, and many are active when we're not watching.

Oliver and Pang's review is a practical guide to what we can measure, how well each method has been validated, and what to do when the evidence runs out.

The article

Oliver VL, Pang DSJ. Pain recognition in rodents. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2023;26:121-149. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2022.07.010

This is a narrative review focused on acute pain. Most of the tools it describes were developed in laboratory rats and mice. Guinea pig, chinchilla, hamster, and gerbil tools are limited or absent.

"Validated" is a process

The authors note that "validated" is often used loosely. Validation covers several properties:

  • Validity: whether the scale measures pain.
  • Reliability: whether different raters get the same result.
  • Generalizability: whether it works for other procedures or conditions.
  • Practicality: whether it can be used in a clinic.

It is an ongoing process, not a one-time stamp. When you pick a tool, ask which of these it has been tested for.

Grimace scales

The Mouse Grimace Scale (MGS) and Rat Grimace Scale (RGS) have had more validation and reliability testing than any other grimace scales. The authors' main argument for them is that they reflect the emotional experience of pain, not just nociception, and they don't require handling the animal.

How they work. Score each facial action unit 0 (confident it's absent), 1 (moderately present, or unsure), or 2 (confident it's present), then average the scores.

  • MGS, 5 units: eyes, ears, nose, cheeks, whiskers.
  • RGS, 4 units: eyes, ears, nose/cheek, whiskers.

One difference trips people up. In mice, the nose and cheek bulge with pain. In rats, they flatten.

Eyes are the easiest unit to score and whiskers the hardest. Both scales still perform well if whiskers are left out.

An intervention threshold. The RGS is currently the only rodent scale with a defined analgesic intervention threshold. An average score above 0.67 increases the probability that pain is present, with a sensitivity of 84.6% and specificity of 88.6%. You can lower the threshold to catch more painful rats or raise it to reduce false positives.

Cage side scoring works. The original method relied on video and still images, which is impractical in a clinic. In one study, real-time RGS scoring during 2 minutes of continuous observation matched the standard method. In that study, the rats were in an observation cage, knew the observer, and the observer was trained. Training improved RGS scoring so it matched an expert, and the training was simple: scoring randomized, blinded images with a brief discussion. Free MGS and RGS posters and an RGS training manual are available from the NC3Rs.

Confounders:

  • Isoflurane anesthesia raises MGS and RGS scores for less than 3 hours. The increase can be enough to push a rat past the 0.67 threshold. Keeping the rat normothermic reduces this.
  • Ketamine-xylazine raises MGS scores in mice for an unclear length of time.
  • Stressful restraint raises MGS scores, but gentle restraint doesn't.
  • Fear, nonpainful illness, sleep, and grooming can produce some of the same facial changes.
  • There is some concern that expressions may be suppressed in front of male observers or potential predators.
  • Opioids do not appear to affect MGS or RGS scores, unlike several other methods.

Composite pain scales

Partially validated composite scales exist for rats (after laparotomy) and mice (after vasectomy). Their key behaviors are rearing, attention to the incision, posture, writhing, back arching, and gait. The rat scale takes about 5 minutes. Neither has an intervention threshold. Buprenorphine alters activity, so it can interfere with these scales.

Guinea pigs. A validated guinea pig scale looks at two groups of behaviors:

  • Passive behaviors that increase with pain: eyes closed or squinting, piloerection, weight shifting, subtle body movements, and incomplete movements.
  • Active behaviors that decrease with pain: forward or backward movement, turning, head or neck movement, rearing, and coprophagy.

Buprenorphine sedates guinea pigs and lowers locomotion scores.

Chinchillas. No ethograms or composite scales exist. Pain is often considered when a chinchilla chews its fur, but fur chewing more often reflects stress. Still, rule out pain.

Hamsters and gerbils. General ethograms are available through the NC3Rs, and a postoperative hamster ethogram needs more work before it's reliable.

Other indicators

Weight loss is neither sensitive nor specific for pain. In a mouse cancer model, mice sought analgesia up to 10 days before reaching the common 15% weight loss end point. Based on that, the authors suggest a threshold closer to 5% when weight loss is used as a marker of illness. In rats, weight loss didn't separate those given analgesia from controls, and buprenorphine itself reduced food intake and weight. That's one more reason home weights are valuable. We've written about weighing small pets at home for families.

Food consumption isn't sensitive or specific for pain. Most rodents eat at night, and species that cache food can look like they're eating when they're not. Buprenorphine causes pica and reduced intake in rats, and hydromorphone reduced food intake in chinchillas.

Response to touch around an incision is useful within a composite scale. Telling a withdrawal reflex apart from a pain response, such as vocalizing or turning toward the site, isn't always easy.

Nesting and burrowing are highly motivated behaviors that fall off with pain and recover with analgesia. One test asks whether a mouse incorporates fresh nesting material into its nest within 10 minutes. Burrowing tests measure how much substrate is left in a tube after 2 hours. Burrowing varies between individuals and improves with practice, so a baseline from home helps. The authors suggest motivated families could record burrowing for a few days before a planned procedure. Cagemates, cold, sedation, and other conditions can all change these behaviors.

Heart rate, blood pressure, and temperature change with pain in rats and mice but can't be separated from stress, so the authors recommend using them only alongside other methods.

When no validated tool exists

For species without validated scales, the authors describe a "triangulation" approach: combining several measures and behaviors to reach a decision. Many of us already do this informally. It's vulnerable to inconsistency, confirmation bias, and poor reliability, so it needs careful records, and it should give way to validated tools as they appear.

When to assess and when to treat

In mice and rats after laparotomy without analgesia, pain lasted from a few hours to close to 2 days. Orthopedic pain may last a week or more. The authors suggest assessing:

  1. Once the animal has recovered from anesthesia and is normothermic, often 1 to 3 hours after surgery.
  2. When the analgesic is expected to wear off.
  3. When pain should be gone, to confirm it is.

If you can't tell whether pain is present, the authors give two options:

  1. Treat and reassess at a set time, matched to the drug's expected onset.
  2. Withhold and reassess within 30 to 90 minutes. This is only appropriate if any pain is likely to be mild.

They prefer the first option. If a procedure would hurt a person, assume it hurts the rodent. They recommend giving analgesia unless the drug's adverse effects outweigh its likely benefit.

Limitations

This is a narrative review. The grimace and composite scales were developed mainly in laboratory rats and mice, often after standardized surgeries, and their performance in pet rodents with clinical disease hasn't been fully established. Guinea pig tools are limited, and no pain tools exist for chinchillas.

How I'd use this

  1. Learn the RGS and MGS, and train the team on them. The materials are free, and the training is short.
  2. Score at fixed times after surgery, and don't trust a high score during the first hours of recovery in a cold rat.
  3. Use a passive and active behavior checklist in guinea pigs.
  4. Ask families about baseline weight, nesting, and burrowing before elective surgery.
  5. If I can't tell sedation from pain, I assume pain and reassess.

Questions for rounds

  1. Who on your team could score an RGS today, and how would you train the rest?
  2. How would you handle a rat with an RGS of 1.0 at 45 minutes after isoflurane?
  3. What would you record to assess pain in a chinchilla after a dental procedure?
