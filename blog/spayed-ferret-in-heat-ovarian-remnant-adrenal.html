Spayed Ferret Acting Like She's in Heat? Ovarian Remnant and Adrenal Disease

November 2, 2026

You check under your ferret's tail one evening and something looks wrong. Her vulva is puffy and swollen, the way it would be if she were in heat. But she's spayed. The pet store said so, and she has the two little tattooed dots on her right ear to prove it.

In a spayed female ferret, a swollen vulva usually means one of two things: adrenal disease, or a small piece of ovary that was left behind during her spay. Both need a vet. One of them can become dangerous if it goes on too long.

Signs to watch for

The signs of an ovarian remnant and of adrenal disease can look almost exactly alike. The most common ones are:

  • A swollen vulva, sometimes with discharge
  • Hair loss
  • Itchy skin
  • Occasionally, a lump your vet can feel in the belly

A spayed ferret with any of these signs has a hormone problem, and it needs a diagnosis.

Why it happens

Ovarian remnant

Most ferrets sold in the United States come from one large breeding farm, where they are spayed or neutered and descented as very young kits. The two dots on the ear are the farm's mark that this was done. Sometimes a small bit of ovarian tissue is accidentally left behind. That tissue can keep making hormones, and eventually your ferret starts acting like an unspayed female.

These signs often show up somewhere between one and three years of age. They can show up much earlier. In a case I published in 2025, the ferret was not quite six months old.

Adrenal disease

The adrenal glands are two small glands near the kidneys. In ferrets, they can start overproducing sex hormones, and the result looks a lot like heat. Spaying and neutering ferrets has been linked to adrenal disease. If your ferret develops it, this is biology, not something you did wrong.

Why "in heat" is a real problem for a ferret

Ferrets are induced ovulators. A female in heat stays in heat until she is bred, given a hormone injection, or spayed. That means the high estrogen of heat doesn't just fade away.

Estrogen that stays high for a long time can shut down the bone marrow. The marrow stops making enough red blood cells and platelets, and that can lead to severe anemia (a low red blood cell count) that can be fatal. A ferret with an ovarian remnant is at risk for this. Ovarian remnants are also commonly associated with tumors of the ovary.

What a vet visit looks like

Because the two conditions look so alike, sorting them out takes a few steps. Your vet may suggest:

  • An exam and bloodwork. A blood count shows whether anemia or low platelets have already started.
  • A hormone panel. It confirms the hormones are high, but on its own it can't tell an ovarian remnant from adrenal disease.
  • A hormone injection test. An injection that makes ovarian tissue release an egg. If the swelling goes down afterward, a remnant is the likely cause. Sometimes a second injection a week later is needed before it works.
  • An abdominal ultrasound. This looks for the leftover ovary and checks the adrenal glands.
  • Surgery, if a remnant is found. The tissue is removed and sent to a lab to make sure it isn't a tumor.

In my own case, the swelling went down after the second injection, the ultrasound found a small piece of ovary near the left kidney, and surgery removed it. A hormone panel six weeks later was normal, and she was doing well months afterward.

Treatment for adrenal disease is different, so getting the diagnosis right matters before anyone picks a plan.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your ferret:

  • Has a swollen vulva and seems tired, weak, or less interested in play
  • Has pale gums
  • Is eating less
  • Has any unusual bleeding or bruising

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • A swollen vulva in a spayed ferret, even if she seems fine otherwise
  • New hair loss
  • More scratching than usual

Before the appointment, take a few photos of the swelling and any bald patches so your vet can see how things change.
