Ovarian Cysts in Guinea Pigs and Why Spaying Can Help

October 2026

If you have an older female guinea pig (a sow), ovarian cysts are one of the health problems worth knowing about. They're common, they often develop slowly, and the signs can be easy to mistake for a skin problem or simply getting older.

What are ovarian cysts?

Ovarian cysts are fluid-filled sacs that form on or in the ovaries. They are common in intact female guinea pigs, and the risk rises as sows get older. Some cysts are small and cause no trouble. Others grow large, press on nearby organs, and in some cases produce hormones that affect the skin and other parts of the body.

Female guinea pigs can also develop other reproductive problems, such as uterine infections and tumors of the ovaries or uterus.

Signs to watch for

  • Hair loss on both sides of the body (the flanks), usually without itching. This is one of the classic signs.
  • A larger or rounder belly
  • Crusty or enlarged nipples
  • Changes in behavior, such as mounting or irritability
  • Reduced appetite, weight loss or lethargy
  • Discomfort when picked up, or a hunched posture
  • Bloody vaginal discharge or blood in the urine

Some sows show no outward signs until the cysts are quite large, which is one more reason regular wellness exams matter.

How ovarian cysts are diagnosed

Your veterinarian may be able to feel enlarged ovaries during an exam, but ultrasound is the most useful tool to confirm cysts and judge their size. X-rays, bloodwork and urine tests may also help rule out other causes, such as bladder stones, which are another common problem in guinea pigs.

Treatment options

Surgery (spaying). Removing the ovaries, and sometimes the uterus, is usually the most definitive treatment. It removes the cysts and prevents them from coming back. Dr. Kopp performs guinea pig spays (ovariectomy and related procedures) and will talk with you about the best surgical approach for your pig.

Hormone treatment. For some guinea pigs, especially those that aren't good anesthetic candidates, medications that affect reproductive hormones may help manage signs. They don't remove the cysts, so ongoing monitoring is needed.

Draining cysts. In certain cases, a cyst may be drained with a needle using ultrasound guidance. This can give temporary relief, but cysts often refill.

Your vet will help you weigh these choices based on your guinea pig's age, health and the size of the cysts.

The case for spaying younger sows

Spaying a healthy younger female before problems develop can prevent ovarian cysts and lower the risk of other reproductive diseases, including uterine infections and ovarian and uterine tumors. Spaying also allows sows to live safely with neutered males.

What about males?

Neutering male guinea pigs (boars) prevents unwanted litters and allows them to live with females. It may also reduce some hormone-driven behaviors. Ask your vet whether neutering makes sense for your household.

Recovery at home

After surgery, keep your guinea pig on soft, clean bedding such as fleece or towels. Offer hay, fresh vegetables and their usual pellets right away. Watch closely to make sure they're eating and producing normal droppings, and give pain medication exactly as directed.

When to call your vet

Call your veterinarian if your guinea pig:

  • Has hair loss on her sides or a growing belly
  • Has bloody discharge or blood in the urine
  • Is eating less, losing weight or seems painful
  • Is not eating at all or has stopped producing droppings (urgent)
  • Has swelling, discharge or redness at an incision after surgery
