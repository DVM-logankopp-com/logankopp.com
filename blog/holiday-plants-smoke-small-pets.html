Holiday Plants, Fireplace Smoke, and Fumes: A Winter Guide for Small Pet Families

November 23, 2026

Plants are the single biggest category of poison control calls about pet mammals other than dogs and cats. For the group of plants reported most often, rabbits made up the large majority of calls, which is about what you'd expect from an animal that explores the world by tasting it. December adds more plants to the house than any other month. It also closes the windows and lights the fireplace.

Here's how I'd think about both.

Houseplants with sharp crystals

Many popular houseplants contain tiny needle-shaped crystals of calcium oxalate. When an animal chews the leaf, the crystals are forced out into the mouth and throat. The result is pain, swelling, and drooling, sometimes with stomach upset and diarrhea. These are the plants poison control hears about most often in small mammals.

Common examples include:

  • Pothos (golden or silver)
  • Philodendron and Monstera (Swiss cheese plant)
  • Peace lily
  • Dieffenbachia (dumbcane)
  • Calla lily
  • Chinese evergreen
  • Arrowhead vine
  • Elephant's ear and caladium
  • Anthurium (flamingo flower)

Signs are usually painful but short-lived. A rabbit or guinea pig with a sore mouth may stop eating, though, and that turns a minor problem into a bigger one quickly.

Holiday plants and gift plants

Christmas rose (hellebore) and kalanchoe, which is often kept as a potted plant, both appear on veterinary lists of plants that can cause illness. Flower arrangements and bouquets are another easy way for a toxic plant to land at rabbit height without anyone noticing what's in them.

Research on exactly which holiday plants are dangerous to rabbits and rodents is thin. Toxins also vary by the part of the plant, the season, and how it was grown, so a plant your pet nibbled last year without trouble isn't proof it's safe. My advice hasn't changed: treat every holiday plant and bouquet as off limits, and keep them on surfaces your pet can't reach.

Greenery from the yard

If you cut branches or berries from outside to decorate, know what you're bringing in.

  • Yew is a common landscaping evergreen with small red berries. The leaves, bark, and seeds are toxic to the heart, and poisoned animals often die suddenly.
  • Azaleas, rhododendrons, and mountain laurel contain toxins that affect the heart and nervous system.
  • Nandina (heavenly bamboo), cherry laurel, and wild cherry leaves can release cyanide, especially when they are wilted or the plant is stressed by frost or drought.

The same goes for anything you forage for your rabbit or guinea pig. Rhubarb leaves, dock, sorrel, wood sorrel, and lamb's quarters are high in oxalates that can affect calcium levels and the kidneys. If you can't name the plant with confidence, don't feed it. A photo of the plant (or the plant itself) is the most useful thing you can bring if your pet ate something unknown.

Smoke from fireplaces, cooking, and tobacco

Smoke irritates the eyes, nose, and lungs. In a laboratory study, cigarette smoke caused significant lung damage in rabbits, and smoke from burning wood and other biomass fuels caused damage too, though less severe. Rabbits and rats are also prone to underlying respiratory infections, and stress can make those infections flare.

Practical steps:

  • Keep enclosures out of the room with the fireplace or wood stove, and away from the kitchen when you're searing and roasting.
  • Don't smoke or vape near your pet. In a lab study, a single puff of e-cigarette vapor caused the airways of guinea pigs to tighten, and the nicotine was the cause.
  • Keep cigarettes, cigarette butts, nicotine gum, and vape liquid out of reach. Eating nicotine can cause vomiting, diarrhea, agitation, seizures, and death.

Most of the research on scented candles, air fresheners, and plug-ins has been done in birds, which are especially sensitive. I'd still keep them well away from a small mammal's enclosure. There's no good reason to add another airway irritant to the room.

Carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide is colorless and has no smell. It comes from fireplaces, wood stoves, central heating systems, and car exhaust, and it can kill people and pets alike. Rabbits exposed to low levels in laboratory studies developed changes in their blood and liver values. If your pet's enclosure is in a garage, car exhaust is one more reason to move it.

Use carbon monoxide alarms, and test them regularly. If an alarm goes off, get everyone, pets included, into fresh air first and then call for help.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Is breathing with effort, breathing with the mouth open, or coughing after smoke exposure
  • Seems weak, wobbly, or collapses
  • Ate yew, azalea, rhododendron, mountain laurel, nandina, cherry leaves, or any plant you can't identify
  • Has a swollen face or mouth, or is drooling heavily
  • Got into nicotine products
  • Was in a space where a carbon monoxide alarm went off

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Red or watery eyes, sneezing, or nasal discharge that started after smoke exposure
  • A smaller appetite or fewer droppings a day or two after nibbling a houseplant

Signs after smoke inhalation can get worse over the first day or two, so an animal that seems fine an hour later still deserves a call.
