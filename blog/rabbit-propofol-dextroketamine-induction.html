Does Adding Ketamine to Propofol Induction Help Rabbits?

October 2026

We use a lot of ketamine in our rabbit anesthetic protocols because it brings many benefits. We use it either in the premedication or as a co-induction agent with propofol. Part of the reason is analgesia. Part of it is the assumption that ketamine's sympathetic support offsets some of propofol's vasodilation.

That second part is a reasonable assumption borrowed from dogs and cats. Fukuzaki and colleagues tested it in rabbits with echocardiography, which is more than most induction studies bother to do.

What they did

This was a randomized, blinded trial in 22 healthy male New Zealand White rabbits. All rabbits were premedicated IM with dexmedetomidine 5 µg/kg, butorphanol 0.5 mg/kg, and midazolam 0.3 mg/kg.

Induction was propofol given at 3 mg/kg/min to effect, either alone or with dextroketamine 2 mg/kg. The authors measured mean arterial pressure and used echocardiography to look at systolic function and cardiac output.

What they found

MAP dropped in both groups, but less when dextroketamine was added. With propofol alone, MAP went from 76 to 66 mmHg. With propofol and dextroketamine, it went from 70 to 65 mmHg. The group by time interaction was significant, though only just (p = 0.049).

Echocardiographic systolic function and cardiac output were preserved in both groups. No group did meaningfully worse on the heart itself.

How much should this change what we do?

Honestly, not a lot, and that is a useful answer.

The difference in MAP between groups was a few mmHg in healthy young rabbits, and the baseline values were not identical. On average, neither group dropped into what most of us would call hypotension during induction. The ketamine group simply started lower and fell less.

What the study does support is that a ketamine co-induction does no apparent harm to cardiac function in premedicated rabbits, and it may blunt the propofol-related pressure drop a little. If you already use ketamine at induction for its analgesic and propofol-sparing value, keep doing it. If you don't, this paper alone is not a strong reason to start.

Strengths and limitations

Randomization, blinding, and echocardiographic data make this a cleaner study than most rabbit anesthesia work. Using a realistic, commonly used premedication protocol also helps.

The limits matter for clinical patients:

  • Healthy male NZW rabbits. Our patients are often dwarf breeds, older, dehydrated, septic, or in pain. Those are the rabbits where a few mmHg may matter.
  • Dextroketamine, not racemic ketamine. Most US practices stock racemic ketamine. The two are not interchangeable mg for mg, so do not copy the dose directly.
  • Dexmedetomidine in the premed. Alpha-2 agonists raise vascular resistance early, which may mask some of propofol's vasodilation. A protocol without an alpha-2 agonist could look different.
  • Induction only. The study does not tell us what happens over a long procedure on isoflurane.

How I would use this

For a healthy rabbit having a spay or a dental procedure, I would not change much. Ketamine as part of premedication or co-induction stays in my protocol because of its analgesic and anesthetic-sparing effects, and this paper reassures me it isn't costing the heart anything.

For the sick rabbit, the bigger lever is still preparation. Correct volume deficits before induction when the case allows it. Give propofol slowly and to effect, since rate matters as much as the total dose. Have blood pressure monitoring on before induction, not after. A hypovolemic rabbit at induction is a much bigger risk than the choice of co-induction agent.

Discussion questions

  1. Do you measure blood pressure before induction in rabbits, or only once they are on the table?
  2. How do you adjust a propofol and ketamine co-induction for a septic or dehydrated rabbit?
  3. Would you expect the same result without dexmedetomidine in the premedication?

Citation

Fukuzaki LM, Sprengel HZ, Vila BD, et al. Cardiovascular and echocardiographic effects of propofol induction, with or without dextroketamine, in healthy premedicated rabbits: a randomized study. Vet Anaesth Analg. 2026;53(6):101332. doi:10.1016/j.vaa.2026.101332
