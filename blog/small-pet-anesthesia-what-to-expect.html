Anesthesia for Rabbits, Guinea Pigs, Ferrets, and Other Small Pets: What to Expect

November 10, 2026

If your rabbit needs a dental procedure or your guinea pig needs a lump removed, one of your first questions is probably whether anesthesia is safe. It's a fair question. Small mammals do carry more anesthetic risk than dogs and cats, and I'd rather explain why than tell you not to worry.

The good news is that we know a lot about where that risk comes from, and most of it can be managed.

Why small pets need extra care

A few things make anesthesia harder for small mammals:

  • They hide illness. Rabbits and rodents are prey animals. A pet that looks fine at home may have a heart, lung, or kidney problem that only shows up on an exam or bloodwork.
  • They get cold fast. A small body loses heat quickly, and anesthetic drugs make it harder to stay warm. Getting cold can slow recovery.
  • Their airways are small. Rabbits and rodents breathe through their noses, and a guinea pig's throat opening is only a few millimeters wide. That makes placing a breathing tube more challenging than in a dog.
  • Their bodies run fast. Rodents use oxygen and sugar quickly and have small energy reserves, so a short problem can become a big one.
  • Recovery matters as much as the procedure. In rabbits, published studies show that problems can happen in the hours after anesthesia, not only during it.

None of this means your pet shouldn't have a needed procedure. It means the plan should be built for their species.

Before the procedure

An exam and bloodwork. I want to listen carefully to the heart and lungs, weigh your pet accurately, and run bloodwork when possible. For some pets, such as a chinchilla with a loud heart murmur, an ultrasound of the heart may be recommended first. If your pet is dehydrated or not eating, we may need to stabilize them before anesthesia.

Food the night before. This is different from dogs and cats. Rabbits and rodents can't vomit, and going without food can slow their gut and drop their blood sugar. Most rabbits and small rodents shouldn't be fasted the way a dog would be. Ferrets are different again and usually need a short fast. Please follow the specific instructions you're given for your pet, and call us if you aren't sure.

What to bring. Bring your pet in a secure carrier with a towel or familiar bedding, plus some of their usual food. A list of any medications and supplements is helpful too.

Tell us about changes. Let us know if your pet has been sneezing, eating less, producing fewer or smaller droppings, or acting differently, even if it seems minor.

During the procedure

Before anesthesia, your pet usually gets a sedative and pain medicine. That calms them, reduces stress, and lets us use less anesthetic gas. For many procedures, a breathing tube or a small airway device is placed so breathing can be supported and oxygen delivered directly.

Your pet is monitored closely throughout anesthesia. At Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital, we can monitor:

  • Blood pressure, with Doppler and oscillometric monitors
  • Oxygen levels, with pulse oximetry
  • Heart rhythm, with an EKG
  • Breathing and carbon dioxide levels, with ETCO2 monitoring
  • Bloodwork and blood gases, using our in-house lab and i-STAT analyzer

For dental patients, we have dental stations and dental X-rays.

We also keep your pet warm from the moment they're sedated until they're fully awake, while making sure they don't overheat. Chinchillas and ferrets in particular can get too warm.

Pain control

Every pet who has surgery should have pain control. I usually combine more than one type of medicine, such as an opioid, an anti-inflammatory, and a local anesthetic or "numbing" block at the surgery site. Using several together works better and means each one can be given at a lower dose.

Pain control also helps your pet eat sooner. In rabbits and guinea pigs especially, pain can slow the gut, and eating soon after surgery is one of the most important parts of recovery.

After the procedure

If your pet has surgery, we'll call you afterward to let you know how it went. Your pet will recover in a warm, quiet space while we watch their breathing, temperature, and comfort. We offer food as soon as they're steady.

At home:

  • Keep them warm and quiet. Set up a small, padded space away from other pets, drafts, and direct heat. A hide or a towel over part of the carrier helps.
  • Offer food right away. Fresh hay and favorite greens for rabbits and guinea pigs, or their usual diet for ferrets and rats. If we've sent home a recovery diet, follow the feeding instructions.
  • Watch droppings. For rabbits, guinea pigs, and chinchillas, normal droppings are a sign the gut is moving.
  • Give medicines exactly as prescribed, and don't stop pain medicine early because your pet seems fine. Small pets are good at hiding discomfort.
  • Check the incision once or twice a day for swelling, redness, discharge, or chewing.
  • Weigh your pet if you can. A kitchen scale that reads in grams makes it easy to spot weight loss early. Here's how to weigh your small pet at home.

If you're not sure whether your pet is sleepy or uncomfortable, call us. Our companion article on signs of pain in rabbits and rodents can help you know what to look for.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Won't eat at all after coming home, even favorite foods
  • Has produced no droppings, or very few, small, dry ones
  • Is breathing with an open mouth, struggling to breathe, or breathing much faster than usual
  • Sits hunched and won't move, grinds their teeth, or presses their belly to the floor
  • Feels cold to the touch or is very weak
  • Has an incision that is open, bleeding, or being chewed

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Eating less than usual for more than a day
  • Weight loss on your home scale
  • Mild swelling or redness at the incision
  • Trouble giving medicines
