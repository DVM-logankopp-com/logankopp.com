Dietary, Plant, and Mineral Toxicoses in Rabbits, Rodents, and Ferrets

November 23, 2026

A lot of small mammal toxicology starts with something the animal ate, whether that's a houseplant, a holiday treat, a supplement, or a diet that was formulated wrong. Several articles in the January 2027 toxicology issue of Veterinary Clinics of North America: Exotic Animal Practice cover this ground, and together they make a useful reference for the patients I see.

What the poison control data show

Willens and Wismer searched ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center calls from 2021 to 2025. Of 8784 calls about exotic pets, 6206 (70.7%) involved mammals other than dogs and cats. For those mammals:

  • Plants were the largest category, at 2093 calls (33.7%). Insoluble oxalates were the largest plant subgroup (625 calls), and lagomorphs accounted for 520 of those (83.4%).
  • Chocolate came next, at 923 calls (14.9%), and lagomorphs were involved in 791 (85.7%).
  • Xylitol accounted for 107 calls (1.7%).

The authors suggest rabbits may be overrepresented because so many are free-roaming in the home. These are call records rather than a retrospective study. About 85% of calls came from pet families rather than veterinarians, and the center doesn't have complete follow-up on case outcomes, so the numbers reflect reported exposures rather than confirmed toxicoses.

Avocado

All parts of the avocado tree are potentially toxic. The main toxin is persin, an aliphatic acetogenin, and the heart appears to be the primary target. Different cultivars may differ in toxicity.

The rabbit reports are sobering. Fourteen farmed rabbits died within 30 hours of eating fresh avocado leaves, with pleural and pericardial effusion, pulmonary edema, and myocardial degeneration and necrosis. In a laboratory colony that accidentally ate avocado leaves overnight, 11 of 20 rabbits were found dead the next morning. The remaining 9 were weak, anorexic, dull, and dyspneic, and 4 of them later became comatose and died. Sterile mastitis has also been described in rabbits. Rats and mice, by contrast, tolerate high doses.

There is no antidote. Management is cardiorespiratory support with gentle, low-stress handling, and the prognosis in rabbits is guarded to poor. Differentials for acute cardiac damage include cardiac glycoside plants, yew, ionophores, and selenium and lead toxicoses.

Allium

Garlic, onion, scallions, chives, leeks, and wild garlic are all potentially toxic whether raw, cooked, dried, or as extracts. Organosulfur compounds cause oxidative hemolytic anemia. Specific toxic thresholds aren't available for exotic species, and the one rabbit data point is an experimental garlic extract LD50 of 3200 to 4200 mg/kg, with anorexia, depression, and partial paralysis.

A few points matter clinically:

  • Heinz bodies may be absent or infrequent in species other than dogs, cats, and livestock. Their absence doesn't rule out Allium toxicosis.
  • Anemia can take several days to develop.
  • Acetaminophen toxicosis in ferrets, zinc toxicosis, and the hemolytic phase of chronic copper toxicosis are all on the differential list.

Treatment is decontamination in asymptomatic patients, oxygen for dyspneic patients, and transfusion for severe anemia, ideally from a conspecific donor with major and minor cross-matching. Red cell abnormalities can take weeks to resolve.

Chocolate and other methylxanthines

Theobromine is more potent than caffeine and is present at higher concentrations in chocolate. Content runs from cacao beans and powder at the top, through baking, dark, and milk chocolate, down to white chocolate, which has negligible amounts. No toxic thresholds have been established for exotic species, and published exotic reports are few and mostly avian.

Signs follow a sympathomimetic pattern: tachycardia, tachypnea, hyperthermia, agitation, diuresis, and seizures at high doses. Treatment is supportive, with antiarrhythmics such as propranolol for tachyarrhythmias, sedation for agitation, antiepileptics for seizures, and fluid diuresis because methylxanthines are excreted mainly in urine. Chocolate can form bezoars, which affects decontamination choices.

Salt and sodium

Rabbits were the most resistant of three species in an experimental salinated water study, with pigs the most sensitive. A pet rabbit still developed seizure-like activity, ataxia, vestibular rolling, and obtundation after eating a "rabbit-friendly" salt supplement bought at a pet store, despite constant access to water.

In chinchillas, more than 1200 animals on one farm died or were euthanized after sodium carbonate was inadvertently added to the feed. Signs within 2 weeks included lethargy, decreased appetite and mobility, and increased reactions to stimuli. Over months, the farm saw hair loss, white dental discoloration, polyuria, abortions, and kit deaths from agalactia.

Correct hypernatremia over 48 to 72 hours, ideally with IV fluids, because faster correction risks cerebral edema. Use antiepileptics as needed. Severe signs carry a guarded to poor prognosis, and blindness may be permanent.

Toxic plants

DiGeronimo and Barr's review is written for zoo medicine, but its framework applies directly to companion mammals:

  • Identify the plant by scientific name. Common names overlap, and closely related plants can differ in toxicity. Ask the family to bring a photograph or the plant itself.
  • Find out which part was eaten. Toxins aren't evenly distributed. The green parts of nightshade family plants are toxic, while their fruits or tubers may be edible.
  • Don't assume a previous uneventful exposure means safety. Toxin concentrations vary with growth stage, season, soil, and rainfall.
  • Monitor for up to a week after exposure. Antidotes are rare, and treatment is mostly decontamination, symptomatic therapy, and supportive care.

Plant groups worth knowing for small mammal patients include:

  • Cardenolides (cardiac glycosides), grayanotoxins from the heath family, and taxines from yew. All three should be considered toxic across taxa, and signs can begin within 30 minutes to several hours. Guinea pigs have been used in experimental grayanotoxin models.
  • Pyrrolizidine alkaloids. In one comparison of sensitivity in males, hamsters were the most sensitive species listed, followed by rabbits, mice, and rats. Liver damage can progress for weeks, months, or years after ingestion.
  • Verticillate milkweeds. Some species have been inadvertently baled into alfalfa hay, where they're hard to spot, and their toxicity doesn't degrade when dried. The species data cited are from horses, sheep, cattle, and poultry, but it's a reason to look at hay when a herbivore has unexplained neurologic signs.
  • Cyanogenic glycosides. Concentrations rise with plant stress such as drought and frost. Wilted material releasing cyanide can affect any species.
  • Insoluble oxalates. Raphides in many common houseplants cause oral and GI irritation, usually self-limiting. Most animals learn to avoid these plants. Calcium-containing foods are sometimes suggested in small animal literature, but the authors note this may not be appropriate for most species.
  • Soluble oxalates. These bind serum calcium. Signs can start within 2 to 6 hours or as late as 24 to 36 hours and can progress to tremors, tetany, and renal failure. Treatment includes fluids and parenteral calcium if hypocalcemia is confirmed.

Copper

Applegate and Radke describe copper toxicosis in rabbits as resembling the disease in sheep. Copper accumulates silently from a misformulated or oversupplemented diet, and the rabbit appears normal until a stressor such as gestation, parturition, lactation, or transport triggers release from the liver and a hemolytic crisis. Rats and sugar gliders, by contrast, may show signs during accumulation. Rats given copper for 90 days developed reduced grip strength and cognitive deficits, and sugar gliders may show lethargy, ataxia, inappetence, and weight loss.

Expect elevated liver enzymes, BUN, and creatinine, although creatinine may stay normal in rats. Liver copper above 300 ppm and kidney copper above 8 ppm are considered elevated, compared with normal values of 8 to 50 ppm and 4 to 6 ppm. Feed with excess copper may look blue to green. Breeding does should get a lower copper diet, ideally around 25 ppm.

Copper deficiency causes predominantly neurologic disease in rabbits and guinea pigs, including ataxia and progressive pelvic limb paralysis in rabbits and head bobbing, tremors, and unsteady gait in guinea pigs.

Zinc

Rodents housed in wire enclosures are susceptible to zinc toxicosis, and Willens and Wismer describe a region-wide outbreak in sugar gliders traced to galvanized wire cages. Other sources include pennies minted after 1982, padlocks, washers, toys, and contaminated diets. Diets containing 500 to 1500 ppm zinc have poisoned ferrets, which may present with pale mucous membranes from anemia. Pancreatitis and nephrosis are the most common postmortem findings across species.

Radiographs are especially useful because many cases involve a metallic foreign body, and imaging helps decide whether it will pass or needs removal. Treatment is supportive: remove the source, consider chelation, and use laxatives only once radiographs show the object can pass. The published chelation protocol in this article is from birds.

Vitamins C and D

Two nutritional points round this out. Guinea pigs can't synthesize vitamin C, and scurvy, with hemorrhage into connective tissue, intestines, muscle, and joints, develops after about 3 weeks on a deficient diet. On the other end, New Zealand White rabbits experimentally fed 10,000 IU/day of vitamin D2 developed hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, and hypercholesterolemia.
