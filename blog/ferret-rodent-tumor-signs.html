Lumps, Hair Loss, and Other Signs of Tumors in Ferrets and Rodents

November 23, 2026

Better care means pet ferrets and rodents live longer than they used to, and tumors become more common with age. That sounds grim, but a lot of the tumors in these species are benign, and many can be removed or managed. Surgery is the treatment of choice for most of them, and it's done routinely even in very small patients.

The trouble is that the same sign can mean very different things. A lump can be a fatty tumor, an abscess, or cancer. Hair loss can be hormones, skin infection, or lymphoma. Here's how I'd think about the signs families most often notice.

"My guinea pig has a lump"

Most lumps you can feel on a guinea pig are in the skin or just under it. In a large study of guinea pig masses sent to a pathology lab, lipomas (benign fatty tumors) were the most common tumor. These usually sit on the belly but can show up anywhere.

Another common one is the trichofolliculoma, a benign tumor of the hair follicle. These tend to appear on the back, often near the rump, and are more common in males. They can fill with cheesy material, grow large, and break open.

Both are generally cured by complete surgical removal. Many smaller skin lumps can be removed under sedation with local anesthetic rather than full general anesthesia.

Lumps on the belly near the nipples are different. Guinea pigs of both sexes get mammary tumors, and males actually get them more often. A real share of these are malignant. A needle sample or biopsy tells us which kind we're dealing with, and it also rules out an abscess, which can look very similar.

"My guinea pig is losing weight but eating everything"

In guinea pigs over about three years old, a thyroid tumor can overproduce thyroid hormone and cause weight loss despite a big appetite, hyperactivity, drinking and urinating more, and sometimes a lump on the underside of the neck. These are treatable, so it's worth getting bloodwork.

Symmetrical hair loss on both sides of the body, thin skin, and drinking more can point to a pituitary or adrenal tumor. Itchy hair loss with flaky skin can be a skin form of lymphoma. Both need a proper workup to sort out the cause.

"My female guinea pig has a swollen belly or bloody discharge"

Uterine tumors are well documented in female guinea pigs. More of them are benign than malignant, but some get very large, and they often show up alongside ovarian cysts. Signs include a swollen or painful belly, a mass you can feel, and bloody vaginal discharge. Surgery is the recommended treatment. I've covered the hormone side of this in my post on ovarian cysts in guinea pigs.

"My ferret is losing hair"

Hair loss that starts at the base of the tail and works its way up the back is the classic sign of adrenal disease, one of the most common tumors in ferrets. You may also notice itching, a stronger odor, a swollen vulva in females, or mating behavior in males. Male ferrets with adrenal disease can develop an enlarged prostate. A male ferret straining to urinate needs to be seen the same day.

Adrenal disease can be managed with hormone implants or treated surgically. I go into the options in my family guide to ferret adrenal disease and insulinoma.

"My ferret stares into space or has weak back legs"

Insulinoma, a tumor of the pancreas that drives blood sugar too low, is the most common tumor in ferrets. Signs include lethargy, staring spells, weak hind legs, drooling, pawing at the mouth, and in severe cases, seizures. These episodes often come and go and may improve after a meal. A ferret having a seizure is an emergency.

"My ferret has a bump on the skin"

Skin tumors are common in ferrets, and most are benign. Mast cell tumors are often itchy and may bleed. Sebaceous tumors can look warty and aggressive but are usually benign. A firm, club-shaped lump on the very tip of the tail is often a chordoma, which is usually easy to remove.

The exception is a lump on or near the prepuce of a male ferret. Preputial gland tumors are malignant, and early removal gives the best chance of a good outcome.

Rats, mice, hamsters, and gerbils

  • Rats: Mammary tumors are the most common tumor in female rats. Most are benign, but they can grow quickly and get very large. See my post on mammary tumors in pet rats. A firm lump just below the ear that breaks open is different. It may be a malignant tumor of a gland in that spot. A rat with a head tilt, wobbly walking, weak hind legs, or vision changes may have a pituitary tumor.
  • Mice: Lumps on the belly or flank of a mouse are often mammary tumors, and in mice these are usually malignant.
  • Hamsters: Lymphoma is the most common tumor in hamsters. Watch for weight loss, enlarged lymph nodes, a swollen belly, or skin sores, scaling, and patchy hair loss that don't improve with standard skin treatment. Dwarf (Djungarian) hamsters get skin tumors often. Syrian hamsters, males especially, can develop melanomas on the flank glands.
  • Gerbils: Older gerbils can develop tumors of the scent gland on the belly. Redness, swelling, or an open sore there should be checked. The gland itself is normal, and it's naturally larger in males.

Chinchillas get tumors less often than guinea pigs or rats, but any new lump or change is still worth an exam.

Why see an exotic companion mammal specialist

Tumors in ferrets don't behave like the same tumors in dogs and cats, and treatment differs. In these species, a lump needs a needle sample or biopsy, imaging is often recommended, and surgery has to be planned around a very small patient. Chemotherapy and radiation therapy are being used more often in ferrets and rodents, and even when a tumor can't be fully removed, partial removal can improve quality of life.

What helps most is catching things early. Run your hands over your pet during handling time, weigh them at home regularly, and write down anything new.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Has a seizure, collapses, or is stargazing and unresponsive
  • Is a male ferret straining to urinate
  • Has a lump that is bleeding or has broken open
  • Has a swollen belly with pain or bloody discharge

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Any new lump, or one that is growing
  • Hair loss, especially if symmetrical or starting at the tail
  • Weight loss, even with a good appetite
  • Drinking or urinating more than usual
  • A head tilt or new trouble walking
