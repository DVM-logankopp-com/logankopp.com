Pills, Edibles, Flea Drops, and Mouse Bait: Holiday Poison Risks for Small Pets

November 23, 2026

A dropped pill rolls under the couch. A guest's overnight bag sits open on the floor. A tin of edibles ends up on the coffee table. None of that is unusual during the holidays, and for a ferret or a free-roaming rabbit, all of it is within reach.

Medications and household chemicals are a smaller share of small pet poisonings than plants or food, but they include some of the most dangerous ones. Here are the ones I'd worry about most.

Pain relievers

Ibuprofen is the big one for ferrets. Ferrets are very sensitive to it, and a single over-the-counter tablet can be fatal. Nearly all ferrets with ibuprofen poisoning develop neurologic signs, such as wobbling, tremors, or seizures. Anti-inflammatory drugs in this family can also injure the stomach and kidneys.

Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is also dangerous for ferrets. Their bodies don't process it well, and any exposure should be treated as serious.

For rabbits and rodents, these drugs aren't safe either. Please don't give any human pain reliever to your pet unless a veterinarian who knows your animal has told you to.

Your pet's own medicine

This one surprises people. A good number of the anti-inflammatory calls poison control gets about small mammals involve a measuring mistake with the pet's own prescription. With the tiny volumes small pets need, a misplaced decimal point means ten times the intended amount. Meloxicam, an anti-inflammatory commonly prescribed for rabbits, was the one reported most often, largely because it's so widely used.

A few habits help:

  • Read the syringe markings out loud, especially if more than one person in the house gives medicine.
  • Keep your pet's medication separate from everyone else's.
  • If a houseguest or pet sitter is giving medicine, show them once in person.
  • If you aren't sure you gave the right amount, call. Don't give another dose to "even it out."

Antibiotics meant for someone else

Leftover antibiotics are not interchangeable between species. Several common oral antibiotics, including penicillin-type drugs and clindamycin, can cause severe, sometimes fatal gut problems in rabbits, guinea pigs, and hamsters. Never share an antibiotic from another pet or a family member.

Cannabis edibles and other human drugs

Marijuana products are now legal in many places and easy to find in homes. In small mammals, THC poisoning can cause deep sleepiness, a low body temperature, a slow heart rate, trouble breathing, seizure-like activity, coma, and death. Sleep aids, ADHD medications, and other drugs that act on the brain also show up in poison control calls about small mammals. If your pet may have eaten any human medication, bring the bottle or a photo of the label.

Hamster families, one extra note. Hamsters stash food in their cheek pouches. A hamster that found a pill may still be carrying it, so tell us right away so the pouches can be checked and emptied.

Flea, tick, and bug products

Don't use dog or cat flea products on a rabbit without asking your vet first. Fipronil, an ingredient in some topical flea products, has caused severe reactions in rabbits, including not eating, low body temperature, seizures, and death. The manufacturer discourages its use in rabbits. If a product was already applied, call before you bathe your pet. Bathing a small animal at home can drop their body temperature fast, and we may want to do it here.

Household bug sprays, ant baits, and borax-based products also show up in small mammal exposures, either licked off fur or eaten. Keep pets out of rooms that were just treated.

Mouse and rat bait

If you're putting out bait for mice this winter, think about where your pets go. Rabbits are the companion mammals most often reported to poison control for rodenticide exposure. Many baits are anticoagulants, which keep blood from clotting. Call as soon as you know your pet got into bait, even if they look completely fine, and bring the package so we know the active ingredient.

Glue traps are their own problem. If your pet gets stuck to one, don't pull. Oil can help release the glue, but call us first, because the fur and skin need careful handling and the pet will need a bath afterward.

Coins, hardware, and cage wire

Zinc poisoning is less common, but it happens. Rodents housed in wire cages can be exposed by chewing, sugar gliders have been poisoned by galvanized wire cages, and ferrets have been poisoned through contaminated diets. Pennies minted after 1982, washers, padlocks, and some toys are other sources. Signs include weakness, a poor appetite, weight loss, and pale gums.

The first few minutes

  1. Get your pet away from the source and pick up what's left.
  2. Save the packaging, or take a photo of the label and ingredients.
  3. Write down what time it happened and your best guess at how much.
  4. Call us, or an animal poison control center.
  5. Don't try to make your pet vomit, and don't bathe them unless you've been told to. Rabbits and rodents can't vomit, and even in ferrets, vomiting is only safe for certain substances.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Ate or chewed any human or pet medication that wasn't prescribed for them, or got a double dose
  • Got into a cannabis product
  • Had a dog or cat flea product applied, or licked one
  • Got into mouse or rat bait, or is stuck to a glue trap
  • Is trembling, wobbly, seizing, unusually sleepy, or cold to the touch

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • A smaller appetite, softer droppings, or less energy after a possible exposure, even if it seemed minor

Poison help for small pets in Middle Tennessee

If you think your ferret, rabbit, or other small pet got into a medication or chemical, call right away and have the packaging with you.
