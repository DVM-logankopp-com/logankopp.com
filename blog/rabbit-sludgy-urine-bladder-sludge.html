Sludgy Urine in Rabbits: What Families Should Know

October 2026

If you've ever cleaned a litter box and found a thick, chalky smear that looks more like toothpaste than urine, you've probably met bladder sludge. Rabbit urine can look a little strange even when it's normal: cloudy, and anywhere from pale yellow to orange or reddish from plant pigments. Sludge is different. It's thick, gritty and heavy, and it's one of the more common problems I see in middle-aged and older rabbits.

Vets call it hypercalciuria, which just means too much calcium in the urine.

Why rabbits get sludge

Rabbits handle calcium differently from most mammals. They absorb much of the calcium in their food whether they need it or not, then get rid of the extra through their urine. That's normal rabbit biology. Trouble starts when the urine carries more calcium than it can hold, and the excess settles in the bladder.

Several things usually work together to push a rabbit in that direction. A high-calcium diet, especially alfalfa hay or alfalfa-based pellets, is a big one. So is low water intake, and many rabbits drink more from a bowl than a bottle. Extra weight, arthritis or back pain make it hard to posture and fully empty the bladder, and a small enclosure means less movement. Sore hocks (pressure sores on the heels) often show up alongside sludge for the same reasons.

Movement matters more than people expect. A rabbit that hops around keeps the sludge stirred up so it can be passed, while a rabbit that sits still lets it settle. None of this is anyone's fault. It's just how rabbits are built.

Signs to watch for

The early signs are often about the litter box: peeing outside it, dribbling, straining, or visiting often with little result. You may see thick, beige or gritty urine. A wet, stained bottom is an important clue, because urine-soaked skin quickly becomes red, sore and infected, and it's very painful.

Red urine can be harmless plant pigment or real blood, and only a test can tell the difference. Some rabbits with bladder problems first look like they have a gut problem, eating less and leaving smaller droppings.

How we diagnose it

I'll feel the bladder, which is often large, check the spine, hips and knees for pain, and look at the feet. X-rays confirm sludge and also show arthritis or bladder stones. Bloodwork and a urine test with culture look for infection and kidney disease. Urine collected directly from the bladder gives a cleaner sample. When the case needs it, we can arrange an abdominal ultrasound, and board-certified veterinary radiologists read it.

How it's managed

There's no single fix. The best results come from working on several things at once.

Diet comes first: switch from alfalfa to grass hays such as timothy, measure the pellets, and offer leafy greens, and we'll go over which greens make sense. Encourage drinking with a heavy, fresh water bowl. Weight loss and more floor time help keep sludge moving and ease the strain on sore joints.

Pain control is a big part of treatment, because most rabbits with sludge also have arthritis, and a comfortable rabbit moves, postures and empties the bladder better. A gentle clip of soiled fur, sometimes under sedation, plus a soothing medicated cream heals urine scald.

Fluids under the skin can help in the short term, but in my experience sludge often returns once they stop, especially when the bladder has been stretched for a long time. For rabbits whose sludge keeps coming back, I sometimes add long-term medication to reduce calcium in the urine, with regular bloodwork and urine checks.

Sludge is usually managed rather than cured. With steady changes, many rabbits become much more comfortable.

When to call your vet

Call the same day if your rabbit is straining and producing little or no urine, has stopped eating or is producing few droppings, or seems painful, hunched or very quiet.

Book a prompt visit for peeing outside the box, dribbling, a wet bottom, thick or gritty urine, or red-tinged urine you can't explain.
