Isoflurane and the Rabbit Gut: Is the Gas to Blame?

November 2, 2026

Ask a room of veterinarians why rabbits stop eating after surgery and you will hear "the anesthesia" more than once. It is a reasonable guess. Rabbits that go home after a procedure and then produce small, sparse droppings for two days are a familiar problem.

What we haven't had is much data on the inhalant itself. Zarein and colleagues looked at isoflurane alone, without surgery, to see whether the gas by itself changes gut transit.

Study design

Five healthy adult male New Zealand White rabbits went through four conditions in a repeated-measures crossover: awake, and 15, 30, and 60 minutes of isoflurane. There was a 6-day washout between conditions.

Induction was by chamber with 5% isoflurane in oxygen. The rabbits were then intubated and kept at a deep surgical plane on 1.5% to 3% isoflurane. No surgery was done.

Transit was tracked with barium contrast radiographs taken at set times from 1 to 420 minutes after administration. The authors measured gastric transit, small intestinal transit, and time to hard feces formation. Food intake and fecal output were recorded daily for 6 days after each condition.

Results

  • Gastric transit was the same in every condition.
  • Small intestinal transit differed overall (Friedman p = 0.015). The median after 60 minutes of anesthesia was 120 minutes, compared with 60 minutes in the other conditions. No individual anesthesia condition was significantly different from the awake control.
  • Time to hard feces didn't differ.
  • Food intake and fecal output were unchanged across all anesthetic durations.

The authors concluded that isoflurane, under these conditions, was not associated with significant changes in gastric transit, hard feces formation, intake, or output. They were also clear that the small sample and widely spaced radiographs limit what can be said about a modest delay.

Reading it carefully

With five rabbits, a negative result is weak evidence. The longer small intestinal transit after an hour of isoflurane may be real and simply underpowered. Radiographs taken at fixed intervals also can't pick up small shifts in timing, so a modest delay could easily slip between two films.

The model is also very different from a clinical patient. These rabbits had no surgery, no tissue trauma, no opioids, no alpha-2 agonist, and no illness. Chamber induction is also stressful, and it isn't how most of us would induce a pet rabbit, so stress was not absent from the model either.

Still, the direction of the result is useful. If isoflurane alone barely moves the needle in healthy rabbits, then the hypomotility we see after real procedures is more likely to come from everything around the anesthetic.

Where I look instead

When a rabbit doesn't eat after anesthesia, this is the list I work through:

Pain. Pain drives sympathetic tone, and sympathetic tone slows the gut. In my experience, undertreated pain is the first thing to rule out, and multimodal analgesia is the treatment.

Stress. Travel, handling, and a recovery ward full of predator smells all add up. A covered carrier, a hide box, familiar hay, and a quiet ward help, and so does getting the rabbit home as soon as it is safe.

Hypothermia. Cold rabbits recover slowly and eat late. Active warming during and after anesthesia matters in small patients.

Blood pressure. Isoflurane is a vasodilator, and a hypotensive rabbit is a poorly perfused rabbit, gut included. Good blood pressure control under anesthesia is part of protecting GI function. I monitor it on every case, keep the vaporizer as low as the patient allows by leaning on balanced analgesia, and treat hypotension early with fluids and other support instead of waiting it out.

Fasting and dehydration. Rabbits should not be fasted the way dogs and cats are, and they come into surgery better hydrated and with food in the gut when you skip the fast. IV or SC fluids help too.

Drugs. Opioids can slow the GI tract in rabbits, and opioid-induced ileus is not reversed by cisapride. That doesn't mean withholding opioids, which would just trade one cause of stasis for another. It means using them at an appropriate dose and watching output closely.

Underlying disease. Dental disease, urinary sludge, and uterine disease often come with a rabbit to surgery. A rabbit that stays anorexic after the expected window may have a problem that started before the anesthetic.

Practical takeaways

  • Don't let a postoperative rabbit go a day without eating on the assumption that it is "just the anesthesia."
  • Offer familiar greens and hay as soon as the rabbit is sternal and swallowing normally.
  • Ask families to count droppings and report back the next morning.
  • Monitor blood pressure under anesthesia and treat hypotension early.
  • Treat pain first when intake drops, then work through the rest of the list.

When intake still hasn't come back and you are dealing with true GI stasis, that is a reasonable point to refer the rabbit with suspected GI stasis or pick up the phone.
