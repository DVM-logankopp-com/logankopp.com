Lidocaine-Ketamine-Propofol TIVA in Two Chinchillas: An Inhalant-Free Option to Consider

October 2026

Chinchilla anesthesia is something many of us approach carefully. The patients are small, the airway is tight, and in dental cases we're sharing the mouth with the procedure. Inhalant anesthesia works well in many cases, but it isn't the only option. I recently published a short case report in Veterinary Anaesthesia and Analgesia describing lidocaine-ketamine-propofol total intravenous anesthesia (LKP-TIVA) in two chinchillas. Here is a summary from Dr. Logan Kopp, Nashville's chinchilla specialist: what was done, and some options to think about for your own cases.

The cases

Two chinchillas (Chinchilla lanigera) weighing 0.60 kg and 0.63 kg had very different procedures:

  • An ovariohysterectomy for open pyometra/uterine adenocarcinoma
  • A cheek-tooth extraction

The report also includes the first described case of uterine adenocarcinoma in a chinchilla.

The protocol

Premedication: buprenorphine, dexmedetomidine, midazolam and ketamine.

Induction: propofol, with lidocaine 2 mg/kg and ketamine 1.6 mg/kg.

Maintenance (TIVA):

Drug Infusion rate
Lidocaine 100 µg/kg/min
Ketamine 0.6 mg/kg/h
Propofol 1.6–2.0 mg/kg/h

Both chinchillas were intubated with 2.0-mm endotracheal tubes and breathed spontaneously.

Key findings

  • Both chinchillas recovered uneventfully, one from abdominal surgery and one from dental surgery.
  • Hypoventilation and mild hypercapnia occurred, which is not surprising in spontaneously breathing patients on a combined injectable protocol.
  • Hypothermia occurred, as it so often does in patients this size.

What this means in practice

This is two cases, so it shows the protocol is feasible. It doesn't show that LKP-TIVA is better than inhalant anesthesia. Still, there are situations we run into where an inhalant-free plan is worth having in the toolbox:

  • Dental work. Without a mask or gas circuit near the face, you have more room in the oral cavity.
  • Balanced anesthesia. Spreading the load across three drugs at modest rates is a familiar idea from other species. A lidocaine-ketamine CRI also adds analgesic support during the procedure.
  • Reproductive surgery. Reproductive disease is common in chinchillas. In a Tokyo biopsy series published this quarter, female reproductive disease made up 81.2% of chinchilla biopsy cases, mostly endometritis (Ueda et al.). Having more than one anesthetic plan can help.

If you'd like to try something similar, a few things to consider:

  1. Intubate and be ready to ventilate. Hypoventilation and mild hypercapnia should be expected. Capnography, if available, makes this much easier to manage.
  2. Start thermal support early, from premedication through recovery, and continue it.
  3. Use reliable infusion equipment. At around 0.6 kg, small-volume syringe drivers and careful dilution planning matter.
  4. Adapt the plan to the patient. These were the rates used in these two cases. Your patient's condition, procedure and monitoring capability will guide what makes sense.
