Rabbit Spays: Opioid-Free, Morphine, or Tramadol?

November 2, 2026

Ovariohysterectomy is probably the most common major surgery most of us do in rabbits. It is also a procedure where I think rabbits are still undertreated for pain, partly because a quiet rabbit in a recovery cage looks comfortable to a busy team.

Surita and colleagues tested three premedication protocols in client-owned rabbits having elective spays. The result is not flattering to any of them, and that is exactly why it is worth reading.

The study

This was a prospective, randomized, blinded clinical study with 39 rabbits, 13 per group. Each group got a different premedication:

  • Opioid-free: dexmedetomidine 50 µg/kg alone
  • Morphine: dexmedetomidine 50 µg/kg plus morphine 2 mg/kg
  • Tramadol: dexmedetomidine 50 µg/kg plus tramadol 10 mg/kg

Every rabbit also received meloxicam 0.6 mg/kg IV. Anesthesia was maintained on sevoflurane with mechanical ventilation, and a norepinephrine infusion was started if arterial hypotension developed. The team recorded heart rate, arterial blood pressure, capnography, and expired sevoflurane during surgery.

After recovery, pain was scored with the Rabbit Pain Behaviour Scale (RPBS) from 2 to 6 hours, and sedation was scored from 1 to 6 hours. Rescue analgesia was given based on pain scores.

What happened

All three protocols got the rabbits through surgery without an exaggerated cardiovascular response to surgical stimulation. The differences showed up elsewhere.

Blood pressure. The opioid-free group had higher blood pressure and needed less norepinephrine than the morphine group.

Rescue analgesia. This is the number that matters. Rescue was needed in 12 of 13 morphine rabbits (92.3%) and in 7 of 13 rabbits in both the tramadol and opioid-free groups (53.8%).

Sedation and pain scores. Morphine rabbits were more sedated after surgery and also had higher pain scores at some time points. The authors felt sedation influenced pain scoring, particularly in the morphine group.

Their conclusion was direct: none of the protocols provided adequate postoperative pain control after ovariohysterectomy.

Strengths and limitations

The design is good. Randomization, blinding, real surgical patients, invasive blood pressure, and a validated rabbit pain scale put this ahead of a lot of what we have for rabbit analgesia.

A few things keep me from reading too much into the group differences:

  • Thirteen rabbits per group is small, and pain scoring is noisy.
  • The observation window was only 6 hours after recovery. Spay pain doesn't end there.
  • Sedation confounds behavior-based pain scales. A heavily sedated rabbit may look painful, or may hide pain, and the RPBS can't fully separate the two.
  • Mechanical ventilation and sevoflurane are not how many general practices run a rabbit spay, so the intraoperative blood pressure data may not transfer directly.
  • Buprenorphine, which is what many of us actually use in rabbits, wasn't tested.

What I take from it

My main takeaway is that one premedication drug plus an NSAID doesn't make a complete analgesic plan for a rabbit spay, whichever drug you pick. I wouldn't read this as a case for dropping opioids altogether.

Morphine at this dose made things worse, not better. More hypotension, more sedation, and nearly every rabbit still needed rescue. I wouldn't reach for morphine as the main opioid for a rabbit OHE based on this.

Tramadol and the opioid-free protocol performed the same, and about half of those rabbits still needed rescue. Those are not good odds for the patient.

How I'd use this

For a routine rabbit spay in my practice, I think about analgesia in layers rather than looking for one perfect drug.

  1. Local anesthesia. An incisional line block or a regional technique is cheap, fast, and removes a lot of the nociceptive input. Total local anesthetic dose adds up quickly in a small rabbit, so I calculate it before anything is drawn up.
  2. Ketamine. I use ketamine in premedication or co-induction in most rabbits, and a lidocaine or ketamine CRI is worth considering for longer or more invasive cases.
  3. An opioid with a purpose. Buprenorphine IV or IM is my usual choice in rabbits. This paper doesn't tell us how it would have compared.
  4. NSAID timing. In my practice, I give the NSAID after surgery once the rabbit is warm and blood pressure is stable. In this study, every rabbit received IV meloxicam as part of the anesthetic protocol. Both approaches are used. I prefer to wait because an NSAID on board during an episode of intraoperative hypotension is hard on the kidneys.
  5. Score pain, and score sedation separately. If you can't tell whether a rabbit is sedated or painful, assume pain and reassess after treatment.

The measure of success is still the return of normal behavior. A rabbit that is eating, grooming, and moving around the cage within a few hours of surgery is the outcome to aim for.

Discussion questions

  1. Does your rabbit spay protocol include a local or regional block every time?
  2. How do you tell sedation from pain on a behavior scale in the first few hours after recovery?
  3. Would buprenorphine have done better than morphine here, or would sedation have muddied the scores in the same way?

Citation

Surita LE, Monteiro ER, Correia BS, Queiroga LB, et al. Effects of an opioid-free versus two opioid-based anesthetic protocols on intraoperative cardiovascular variables and postoperative pain in rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) undergoing elective ovariohysterectomy: a prospective, randomized, blinded clinical study. Vet J. 2026 (epub ahead of print):106942. doi:10.1016/j.tvjl.2026.106942
