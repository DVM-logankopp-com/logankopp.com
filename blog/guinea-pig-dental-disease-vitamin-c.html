Guinea Pig Dental Disease and Vitamin C: What Owners Need to Know

October 2026

Guinea pigs are cheerful, chatty companions, but two health topics come up again and again: their teeth and their vitamin C. The two are connected, and both depend heavily on diet. Here's what every guinea pig owner should know.

Guinea pig teeth never stop growing

All of a guinea pig's teeth, front and back, grow throughout life. They stay the right length only because constant chewing, especially on hay, wears them down. When that balance is off, the teeth can overgrow, form sharp points, or become misaligned (called malocclusion).

The back teeth (cheek teeth) are the usual problem. Overgrown cheek teeth can trap the tongue, cut the cheeks, and make chewing painful. Because these teeth sit far back in a small mouth, problems are hard to see without special tools and imaging. Dental disease is one of the most common problems in pet guinea pigs.

Signs of dental disease

  • Drooling or a wet chin
  • Eating less, or eating only soft foods
  • Dropping food or chewing oddly
  • Weight loss, sometimes before you notice any change in eating
  • Overgrown or crooked front teeth
  • Swelling on the jaw or face, or watery eyes
  • Fewer or smaller droppings

Weighing your guinea pig regularly at home on a kitchen scale is one of the best ways to catch problems early.

What causes dental problems?

  • Not enough hay. Hay should make up most of the diet and be available at all times.
  • Genetics and jaw shape
  • Injury to the teeth or jaw
  • Vitamin C deficiency, which weakens the tissues that hold teeth in place

Why vitamin C matters so much

Like people, guinea pigs can't make their own vitamin C, so they must get it every day from their diet. Vitamin C helps build collagen, the protein that supports skin, gums, joints, blood vessels and the ligaments around the teeth. It also supports the immune system and wound healing.

Without enough vitamin C, guinea pigs can develop scurvy. Signs include:

  • Swollen, sore or bleeding gums
  • Loose teeth
  • Limping, swollen joints or reluctance to move
  • A rough coat and poor wound healing
  • Low energy and a poor appetite

How to provide vitamin C

  • Fresh vegetables every day. Bell peppers (red are a favorite), leafy greens and herbs such as cilantro and parsley are good choices.
  • A quality guinea pig pellet fortified with vitamin C, choosing an adult or young formula as appropriate.
  • Fresh food matters. Vitamin C breaks down over time with exposure to light and air, so store pellets properly and use them before they get old.
  • Supplements may be recommended for some guinea pigs. Ask your vet before adding drops to water, since they can degrade quickly and some guinea pigs then drink less.
  • Fruit only as an occasional treat, because of the sugar.

How dental disease is treated

Your veterinarian will examine the mouth and may recommend sedation or anesthesia for a full look at the cheek teeth, along with skull or dental X-rays or CT. Treatment may include trimming and reshaping overgrown teeth, treating infection, extracting diseased teeth, pain relief and nutritional support. Many guinea pigs need repeat dental care over their lifetime, so dental disease is often managed rather than cured.

When to call your vet

Call your veterinarian if your guinea pig:

  • Is drooling or has a wet chin
  • Is eating less, losing weight, or producing fewer droppings
  • Has swelling on the face or jaw
  • Is limping, has bleeding gums, or seems painful
  • Hasn't eaten at all. Guinea pigs that stop eating need to be seen quickly.
