Macrodontic Cheek Teeth in Guinea Pigs: What Cone-Beam CT Adds

October 2026

Guinea pig cheek teeth are hard to image well. The skull is small, the arcades overlap, and the teeth curve in ways that make a lateral radiograph look tidier than the mouth really is. Most of us have trimmed a guinea pig's cheek teeth, sent them home eating, and seen them back a few weeks later with the same problem.

Macrodontia is one of the reasons. The affected tooth is enlarged and structurally abnormal, and it is a common driver of malocclusion. Ignaszak-Dziech and Piasecki used cone-beam CT (CBCT) to describe what these teeth look like in three dimensions.

What they did

This was a retrospective review of CBCT datasets from 62 guinea pigs imaged for clinical reasons. Forty-two had at least one macrodontic cheek tooth and were included. In total, 666 premolars and molars were evaluated, and 104 of those had structural changes typical of macrodontia.

The authors measured tooth circumference, scored premolar and molar alveoli on a four-grade scale, and classified structural changes as isodense or heterodense.

What they found

  • Macrodontic teeth were bigger. Mean circumference was 17.70 mm compared with 15.13 mm for non-macrodontic teeth (P < .001).
  • Advanced alveolar remodeling (grades 2 to 3) was present around 84.6% of macrodontic teeth, compared with 23.8% of non-macrodontic teeth.
  • Loss of dental substance at the occlusal margin occurred mostly in macrodontic teeth, together with widening of the periodontal and interdental spaces.

The authors conclude that CBCT shows tooth structure, size, and periapical change in a way conventional radiography cannot.

Why the alveolar finding matters

The number I would pay attention to is the remodeling. Most macrodontic teeth sat in bone that was already significantly changed. That fits with what we see clinically: these are not just crowns that need shortening. They are teeth with apical and alveolar disease underneath, and the occlusal problem is only the visible part.

It also explains some of the frustration. Widened periodontal and interdental spaces give food a place to pack, and a tooth losing substance at the occlusal margin does not wear evenly against its partner. Trimming the crown treats the symptom.

Strengths and limitations

The dataset is a reasonable size for guinea pig dentistry, and tooth-level analysis across 666 teeth gives the comparisons some weight. Having a scoring system for alveolar change is useful for anyone trying to stage cases consistently.

It is retrospective and based only on animals that were scanned for a clinical reason, so it tells us about guinea pigs already sick enough to image. There is no histology or outcome data, so we can't say which CBCT features predict treatment failure or extraction need. It also doesn't compare CBCT directly with radiographs in the same animals.

What it means in practice

Radiographs still have a place. In my practice we take skull and dental radiographs in house when cheek tooth disease is suspected, and they answer a lot of questions. But a guinea pig that keeps coming back, has a swelling or ocular change, or is being considered for extraction deserves a better look at the apices and alveolar bone. That is where CT earns its cost.

Some practical points I would take from this paper:

  • Think of macrodontia as an alveolar disease too. Talk with families about long-term management early, not after the third trim.
  • Use CT for planning, not as a first screen. It is most useful when the result will change what you do, such as extraction or a decision about prognosis.
  • Look at the whole arcade. Macrodontic teeth were mixed in with normal teeth, and a single abnormal tooth can drive the whole occlusion.
  • Manage pain properly. Remodeled alveolar bone and periodontal widening are painful. Anorexia in these guinea pigs is often pain, and analgesia belongs in the plan alongside the dental work and assisted feeding.

Discussion questions

  1. At what point do you move a guinea pig with recurrent cheek tooth overgrowth from radiographs to CT?
  2. How do you explain the difference between a crown problem and an alveolar problem to a family?
  3. Would a grading system like this one help you decide between repeated trims and extraction?

Citation
