Non-Obstructive Dystocia in a Guinea Pig: Case Rounds

November 16, 2026

When most of us think of guinea pig dystocia, we think of the sow whose pubic symphysis never separated, with a pup wedged against a closed pelvis. In this case the birth canal was open, the fetuses were alive, and labor never started.

The paper

Magalhães DA, Marques MVLSG, Sobrinho MBR, Alarcon GSL, Rahal SC. Non-obstructive dystocia in a primiparous guinea pig (Cavia porcellus) managed by caesarean section. Vet Rec Case Rep. 2026;14(4):e70696. doi:10.1002/vrc2.70696

The case

The patient was a 7- to 8-month-old primiparous pet guinea pig presented for prolonged gestation with no signs of labor. Serial ultrasonography confirmed that the fetuses were viable throughout.

Imaging showed advanced pregnancy with two oversized, viable fetuses and widening of the pubic symphysis, so the pelvis was open. The authors attempted medical induction with calcium gluconate and oxytocin, and it was unsuccessful. They interpreted the failure as support for a diagnosis of primary uterine inertia.

An emergency cesarean section followed by ovariohysterectomy delivered two live neonates. The sow initially stabilized after surgery but died within 24 hours. Postmortem findings were suggestive of thromboembolic cardiovascular collapse.

What makes this case useful

The diagnosis rests on two findings that are easy to check in practice: an open pubic symphysis and live fetuses that are not being delivered. That combination points away from obstruction at the pelvis and toward a problem with the uterus or with fetal size. Here both may have played a role, since the fetuses were described as oversized and only two were present.

The case also follows a sensible decision path. Ultrasound confirmed viability, induction was tried, and surgery followed when induction failed. The authors' own emphasis is on early reproductive counseling, serial ultrasound monitoring, timely surgery, and intensive perioperative support.

Limitations

This is a single case, so it can describe what happened but not how often it happens. The cause of death was not confirmed. The postmortem findings were suggestive of thromboembolic collapse, and the authors present it that way. A single case also can't tell us whether surgery at an earlier point, or a different perioperative plan, would have changed the sow's outcome.

How I'd use this

Guinea pig obstetric emergencies are uncommon for most of us, so I want the decision points set out before the phone call. A few points I take from this case:

Ultrasound early and more than once. Fetal viability changes the whole conversation. Live fetuses with an open pelvis may justify a trial of medical management. Dead fetuses or a closed symphysis change the plan.

Decide in advance what "failed induction" means. Agree on a time frame with the family before you start medical management, so that the decision to go to surgery isn't made at midnight from scratch.

Prepare the family for the sow, not just the pups. In this case both neonates survived and the sow did not. That outcome needs to be part of the consent conversation for any guinea pig cesarean, along with the plan for the pups if the sow can't nurse.

Plan for the first day after surgery. Warmth, fluid support, analgesia, early return to eating, and close monitoring after surgery all matter. This sow stabilized initially and still died within a day, so stable at extubation doesn't mean the risk has passed.

Talk about breeding before it happens. Many pregnancies in pet guinea pigs aren't planned. Sexing new guinea pigs correctly and keeping males and females apart, or neutering the male, prevents many of these emergencies.

Questions for rounds

  2. How would you counsel a family who wants to breed their guinea pig once "just to have babies"?
