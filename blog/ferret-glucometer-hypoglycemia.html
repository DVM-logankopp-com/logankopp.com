Glucometers and Hypoglycemia in Ferrets: What Our AJVR Study Found

October 2026

Ferrets with insulinoma are something most of us see regularly, and a glucometer is often the first thing we reach for. It's fast, it needs one drop of blood, and many owners already have one at home. The practical question is how far we can trust that number when the patient is near the hypoglycemia cutoff. My coauthors Rollins, Rishniw and Donnelly and I looked at exactly that, and the paper is now out in the American Journal of Veterinary Research. Here is a practical summary from Dr. Logan Kopp, Nashville's ferret specialist, with some options to consider in your own practice.

What we looked at

We compared portable glucose meters with a point-of-care chemistry analyzer (VetScan VS2) in 24 client-owned ferrets:

  • AlphaTrak3, a veterinary meter, read on its canine, feline and equine settings
  • FreeStyle Precision Neo, a human glucometer

We assessed two things. First, how closely each meter agreed with the analyzer. Second, whether each meter placed a ferret on the same side of a ≤60 mg/dL hypoglycemia cutoff as the analyzer did.

Key findings

  • The average bias was small, but agreement on individual readings was not. The AlphaTrak3 canine and feline settings showed minimal bias. Their limits of agreement were wide, though, so any single reading could land well away from the analyzer value.
  • No device classified hypoglycemia perfectly at ≤60 mg/dL. Agreement with the analyzer was:
  • AlphaTrak3, canine and feline settings: 79%
  • AlphaTrak3, equine setting: 75%
  • Human meter (FreeStyle Precision Neo): 58%

Even with the best settings, about one ferret in five ended up in a different category (hypoglycemic or not) on the meter than on the analyzer.

What this means in practice

A small average bias is reassuring for comparisons across a group of patients. Day to day, though, we make decisions one reading at a time, and wide limits of agreement mean an individual reading can cross the cutoff in either direction. The places where that matters most are the borderline values we use to decide on a diagnosis, to start or adjust prednisolone, or to call an owner back in.

Some options we've found useful to think about:

  • Use the meter for screening and trends, not as the final word. When a reading is low or borderline, confirming it on a chemistry analyzer before committing to a diagnosis or a management change is a reasonable step if one is available.
  • Watch which setting you use. In this study the AlphaTrak3 canine and feline settings agreed best with the analyzer. The human meter agreed least, so it may be worth avoiding for ferrets.
  • Stay consistent. If an owner monitors at home, it helps to record which device and setting they use and keep it the same, so trends can be compared over time.
  • Set expectations with owners. A home reading is a useful guide. Clinical signs such as lethargy, staring or weakness still matter, and so does a confirmatory test in the clinic.

None of this means glucometers have no place with ferrets. It means a single number near 60 mg/dL deserves some caution, and a confirmatory measurement is worth considering when the decision depends on it.

Citation

Rollins K, Rishniw M, Donnelly TM, Kopp L. Portable blood glucose meters do not reliably detect hypoglycemia in ferrets (Mustela putorius furo) compared with a point-of-care chemistry analyzer. Am J Vet Res. 2026:1-8. doi:10.2460/ajvr.26.05.0224
