Glucometers and Hypoglycemia in Ferrets: What Our AJVR Study Found

October 2026

A ferret comes in a little wobbly, the glucometer reads 62, and now you have to decide what that number means. Insulinoma is something most of us see regularly, and a glucometer is usually the first thing we reach for. It's fast, it needs a single drop of blood, and many families already have one at home. The practical question is how far we can trust it when the patient is close to the cutoff. My coauthors Rollins, Rishniw and Donnelly and I looked at exactly that, and the paper is now out in the American Journal of Veterinary Research. Here's a practical summary, with some options to consider.

What we looked at

We compared portable glucose meters with a point-of-care chemistry analyzer (VetScan VS2) in 24 client-owned ferrets. The meters were the AlphaTrak3, a veterinary meter read on its canine, feline and equine settings, and the FreeStyle Precision Neo, a human glucometer. We asked two questions: how closely each meter agreed with the analyzer, and whether each placed a ferret on the same side of a 60 mg/dL hypoglycemia cutoff.

What we found

The average bias was small, but agreement on individual readings was not. The AlphaTrak3 canine and feline settings showed minimal bias, yet their limits of agreement were wide, so any single reading could land well away from the analyzer value.

No device classified hypoglycemia perfectly. Agreement with the analyzer at the cutoff was 79% for the AlphaTrak3 canine and feline settings, 75% for the equine setting, and 58% for the human meter. Even with the best settings, roughly one ferret in five ended up in a different category on the meter than on the analyzer.

What this means in practice

A small average bias is reassuring when comparing groups of patients. Day to day, though, we make decisions one reading at a time, and wide limits of agreement mean an individual reading can cross the cutoff in either direction. That matters most at the borderline values we use to make a diagnosis, adjust medication or call a family back in.

So I'd suggest using the meter for screening and trends rather than as the final word. When a reading is low or borderline, confirming it on a chemistry analyzer before committing to a diagnosis or management change is a reasonable step. Pay attention to the setting. In this study the AlphaTrak3 canine and feline settings agreed best, and the human meter agreed least, so I'd avoid human meters for ferrets. If a family monitors at home, record which device and setting they use and keep it consistent so trends stay comparable. And set expectations: a home reading is a useful guide, but clinical signs such as lethargy, staring, hind-limb weakness or ptyalism still matter, and so does a confirmatory test in the clinic.

Don't let the glucose number end the workup

Insulinoma signs overlap with GI disease, which is also very common in ferrets. Hyporexia, lethargy and bruxism are just as typical of Helicobacter mustelae gastritis and GI foreign bodies, especially in young ferrets, and the ptyalism of a hypoglycemic episode can look a lot like nausea. A ferret can have both problems at once. Every anorectic ferret deserves a minimum database (CBC, biochemistry and radiographs) rather than a glucometer reading alone, with fluids, nutritional support, analgesia and gastroprotection while you sort it out.

A pharmacology footnote

Diazoxide, used for insulinoma in ferrets, is a thiazide-class drug without diuretic action. It reduces insulin secretion by hyperpolarizing pancreatic beta cells. The thiazide diuretics such as hydrochlorothiazide have hyperglycemia as a recognized effect in people, which is worth keeping in mind if you ever manage a ferret on a thiazide for another reason.

None of this means glucometers have no place with ferrets. It means a single number near 60 mg/dL deserves some caution, and a confirmatory measurement is worth considering when the decision depends on it.

Citation

Rollins K, Rishniw M, Donnelly TM, Kopp L. Portable blood glucose meters do not reliably detect hypoglycemia in ferrets (Mustela putorius furo) compared with a point-of-care chemistry analyzer. Am J Vet Res. 2026:1-8. doi:10.2460/ajvr.26.05.0224
