Why I Want You to Weigh Your Small Pet at Home

November 9, 2026

If I could hand every family one piece of equipment, it wouldn't be anything fancy. It would be a digital kitchen scale.

Rabbits, guinea pigs, chinchillas, and most other small mammals are prey animals. They're built to hide weakness, and they're very good at it. By the time a guinea pig looks sick, it has often been sick for a while. Weight is one of the few things they can't hide.

A visit is a snapshot

When you come in, I weigh your pet and compare that number to the last visit. That helps, but visits may be months apart. A slow loss over the summer looks the same on my scale as a quick loss over the past two weeks, and those can mean very different things.

Weights you take at home fill in the gaps. A list of numbers over several weeks tells me whether something is changing, how fast, and when it started. That's often more useful than any single test.

What you'll need

  • A digital kitchen scale that reads in grams
  • A small box, bowl, or carrier your pet can sit in calmly
  • A notebook or a note on your phone

Grams matter. Small pets are small, and a change that disappears when you round to the nearest pound can be a big deal for an animal as small as a chinchilla.

How to weigh your pet

  1. Put the empty box or bowl on the scale and press the "tare" or "zero" button.
  2. Place your pet in the container. A favorite treat can help them settle.
  3. Wait for the number to hold steady, then write it down with the date.

Try to weigh at about the same time of day each time, ideally before breakfast. Food and droppings can shift the number a little, so consistency helps.

For most healthy adult pets, once a week is plenty. If your pet is older, recovering from surgery, or being treated for an illness, your vet may ask you to weigh more often.

What the numbers can tell you

Slow, steady loss over a few weeks is the pattern I worry about most, because it's easy to miss by eye. In rabbits, guinea pigs, and chinchillas, dental disease is one of the most common reasons. Kidney disease, other chronic illness, and pain are also on the list. A recent veterinary case report described a senior chinchilla that lost weight for months before kidney disease was found, and that kind of slow slide is exactly what a home scale can catch.

A sudden drop over a few days can mean your pet isn't eating, which is an emergency in rabbits, guinea pigs, and chinchillas.

Gain matters too. Extra weight in rabbits and guinea pigs makes it hard to groom and puts pressure on joints and feet.

A few species notes

  • Chinchillas have such thick fur that you often can't see weight loss at all. The scale is your best friend here.
  • Ferrets often gain a little weight in fall and lose it again in spring. That can be normal, but steady loss outside that pattern isn't.
  • Hedgehogs tend to gain weight easily, so watching the trend helps you adjust portions before it becomes a problem.
  • Rats, mice, and hamsters are tiny, so even small changes matter. A scale that reads to the single gram is ideal.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Stops eating or stops making droppings
  • Loses weight quickly over a few days
  • Seems weak, hunched, or less interested in favorite foods

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Weight going down steadily over two or more weeks
  • Weight going up steadily and you're not sure why
  • Dropping food, drooling, or chewing differently, which can point to dental problems
  • Any weight change in an older pet

Bring your weight log to the appointment. It helps me more than you might think.
