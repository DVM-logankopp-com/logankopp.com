Keeping Your Small Pet Safe Through the Holidays

November 3, 2026

From Thanksgiving through New Year's, most homes in Nashville change a lot. There is more food on the counters, a tree in the corner, new cords along the baseboards, and people coming and going. Your rabbit, ferret, or guinea pig notices all of it, because to them the whole house has been rearranged overnight.

Most of the holiday problems I see in small pets are predictable, which means you can head off a lot of them.

The holiday table

It is tempting to share. A bite of sweet potato, a piece of roll, a little pie crust. For a rabbit, guinea pig, or chinchilla, those starchy and sugary foods can upset the bacteria that keep their gut working, and that can lead to diarrhea, bloating, or a gut that slows down and stops.

Ferrets have the opposite kind of gut. They are built for meat and don't handle sugar, dairy, or produce well. Sweets are a particular problem for ferrets, and chocolate and anything sweetened with xylitol should never be within reach of any pet.

What I usually suggest:

  • Keep your pet's regular diet exactly the same through the holidays. For rabbits and guinea pigs, that means plenty of hay and their usual greens.
  • If you want them to feel included, set aside a few of their normal leafy greens as a special plate.
  • Ask guests not to feed your pet, and keep plates and trash cans up high.

The tree, the cords, and the decorations

Rabbits chew. Ferrets explore everything with their mouths. Rodents will sample anything at their level. Holiday decorations hand all of them a buffet.

Electrical cords are my biggest worry. A rabbit that bites through a light strand can burn its mouth badly, and the injury can be more serious than it looks. Run cords up high, use cord protectors, or block the area around the tree so your pet can't reach it.

Tinsel, ribbon, and string can be swallowed and cause a blockage in the stomach or intestines. Ferrets are especially good at finding small rubbery or soft items. Getting down at their level and ferret-proofing the room before playtime catches most of it.

Plants. Holly, mistletoe, and amaryllis are commonly listed as toxic to pets. Research on which holiday plants are safe for rabbits and rodents is limited, so I tell families to treat every holiday plant as off limits.

The tree itself. Needles and tree water with preservatives aren't good for nibbling or drinking. A pen or gate around the tree solves most of this.

Guests, noise, and changes in routine

Prey animals like rabbits, guinea pigs, and chinchillas feel safest when their day is predictable. A crowded house, loud music, kids who want to hold them, or fireworks around New Year's can all be stressful. Stress isn't just a feeling for these animals. It slows the gut and can be the start of a medical problem.

A few things help:

  • Give your pet a quiet room or a covered corner of their enclosure where they can hide.
  • Keep feeding times and playtime on their usual schedule.
  • Supervise children closely, and have them sit on the floor if they want to hold a small pet. Rabbits in particular can be badly hurt if they kick and fall.
  • Remind guests to close doors. Ferrets and rabbits are quick, and an open front door in December is a real risk.

Traveling or boarding

If you're leaving town, plan ahead. A trusted pet sitter who already knows your animal is often less stressful than moving them. If your pet will go to a boarding facility or a friend's house, send their own food, hay, and bedding so nothing changes at once.

Write down what normal looks like: how much they eat, how many droppings you expect, and what their usual energy is like. Ask your sitter to call if any of that changes. Leave the name and phone number of your exotic vet, and find out ahead of time which emergency hospital near you sees exotic pets after hours.

If your pet is on daily medication or has a chronic condition, check in with your vet before you leave.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Stops eating or stops passing droppings
  • Chewed through a cord, or has burns or drooling around the mouth
  • May have swallowed tinsel, ribbon, foam, or rubber
  • Has diarrhea, a swollen belly, or seems weak or painful
  • Got into chocolate, xylitol, or a holiday plant

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Softer droppings or a smaller appetite that lasts more than a day after a diet slip
  • Hiding more than usual or seeming jumpy after the holidays
  • Weight gain from extra treats
