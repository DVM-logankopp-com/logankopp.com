Mammary Tumors in Pet Rats: What Every Rat Owner Should Know

October 2026

If you've found a lump on your pet rat, you're not alone. Mammary tumors are one of the most common health problems in pet rats, especially in females. The good news is that most of them are benign, and surgery often goes very well when the lump is removed early.

What are mammary tumors?

Mammary tumors are growths that start in the mammary (breast) tissue. Rats have mammary tissue spread across a large area of the body, from the neck and shoulders to the belly and groin. A mammary tumor can show up in places you might not expect, such as the armpit, the side of the chest or near the tail base.

Most mammary tumors in rats are benign, meaning they don't spread to other organs. Even so, they can grow quickly and become very large. A big tumor can make it hard for your rat to walk, groom or eat. It can also develop sores on the surface.

Male rats can get mammary tumors too, but this is much less common.

Signs to watch for

  • A soft or firm lump under the skin, often movable
  • A lump that is growing
  • Difficulty walking, climbing or grooming
  • A scab, sore or ulcer on the lump
  • Less activity, hiding or a reduced appetite

Check your rats regularly by gently running your hands over their whole body during handling time. Finding a lump while it's small makes surgery simpler and recovery faster.

How mammary tumors are treated

Surgical removal is the most common and effective treatment. Your rat is placed under general anesthesia and the lump is carefully removed. Many rats recover quickly and are back to normal activity within a few days.

Removed tumors are often sent to a lab to confirm what kind of tumor it is.

Because rats have so much mammary tissue, new tumors can develop later, even after a successful surgery. Your veterinarian may discuss spaying at the time of lump removal, or a medication that may help reduce the chance of new tumors forming. Dr. Kopp will talk through these options with you based on your rat's age and health.

Prevention: the role of spaying

The best way to lower the risk of mammary tumors in female rats is spaying. Spaying a young female before she reaches maturity greatly reduces her chance of developing mammary tumors later in life. It also prevents uterine disease and unplanned litters. Ask your veterinarian about the best timing for your rat.

Caring for your rat after surgery

  • Keep your rat in a clean, simple cage with soft bedding such as paper towels or fleece for a few days.
  • Remove tall shelves and hammocks temporarily so they don't strain the incision.
  • Give pain medication exactly as prescribed.
  • Offer favorite foods to encourage eating.
  • Check the incision daily, and stop cage mates from grooming or chewing it.

When to call your vet

Contact your veterinarian if your rat:

  • Has a new lump anywhere on the body
  • Has a lump that is growing quickly, has opened or is bleeding
  • Is struggling to move, eat or groom
  • Is breathing hard or making noisy breathing sounds (rats are also prone to respiratory disease)
  • Is not eating, seems lethargic, or has a problem with an incision after surgery
