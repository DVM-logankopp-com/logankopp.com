Preparing Your Exotic Pet for Surgery and Anesthesia

October 2026

Hearing that your rabbit, guinea pig, ferret or rat needs surgery can be scary. Many owners have heard that anesthesia is "risky" for small animals. Modern monitoring, species-specific anesthetic plans and good pain control have made surgery much safer than it used to be, and good preparation at home helps too.

Why small mammals need a different approach

Exotic companion mammals have a fast metabolism, lose body heat quickly and can hide pain very well. Rabbits and guinea pigs also depend on eating almost constantly to keep their digestive system moving. Because of this, a veterinarian experienced with these species plans anesthesia around:

  • Keeping your pet warm before, during and after the procedure
  • Close monitoring of heart rate, breathing and oxygen levels
  • Pain relief before, during and after surgery
  • Getting your pet eating again as soon as possible

Before surgery: what to expect

A pre-surgical exam. Your vet will examine your pet and may recommend bloodwork to check organ function before anesthesia. In older pets, X-rays or other tests may also be suggested.

Food and water instructions. This is where exotic pets differ a lot from dogs and cats. Rabbits and guinea pigs usually should not be fasted, because they can't vomit and their gut needs a steady supply of food. Ferrets and some other species may get specific instructions. Always follow the instructions your veterinarian gives you for your individual pet rather than general advice you find online.

Bring their favorite foods. Pack a small bag of your pet's usual hay, pellets and fresh greens. Familiar food helps them start eating sooner after surgery.

Bring a bonded buddy if asked. Some bonded rabbits and guinea pigs do better with their companion nearby. Ask your clinic whether this is a good idea for your pet's procedure.

On the day of surgery

Plan a few extra minutes at drop-off to go over paperwork, confirm your contact number and ask questions. Let the team know about any medications or supplements your pet takes, and any changes in appetite or droppings over the last day or two.

During the procedure, your pet will be monitored continuously. Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital uses IV fluids and monitors such as EKG, blood pressure and pulse oximetry, and provides pain management during and after surgery.

Common surgeries in exotic companion mammals

Dr. Kopp performs a wide range of procedures, including:

  • Spays and neuters (depending on species and size)
  • Mass and tumor removal, including mammary tumors in rats
  • Dental procedures and extractions
  • Abscess surgery
  • Abdominal surgery, such as for intestinal obstruction or liver lobe torsion in rabbits
  • Bladder stone removal
  • Adrenal surgery in ferrets
  • Nasal and ear surgery in rabbits

Recovery at home

  • Keep them warm and quiet. A calm, draft-free room helps.
  • Offer food right away. Fresh hay, greens and their usual diet. Your vet may send home a critical care food to syringe-feed if appetite is slow.
  • Watch the droppings. Normal-sized droppings are one of the best signs of recovery in rabbits and guinea pigs.
  • Give pain medication exactly as directed. Don't stop early just because your pet seems fine. These species hide pain.
  • Check the incision daily for redness, swelling or discharge, and prevent chewing at it.
  • Use soft, clean bedding like towels or fleece for the first several days.

When to call your vet

Call right away if, after surgery, your pet:

  • Hasn't started eating by the time your vet said to expect it
  • Is producing few or no droppings
  • Seems painful, hunched, or is grinding their teeth
  • Has bleeding, swelling or discharge at the incision, or has opened the incision
  • Is breathing hard, weak or unusually cold to the touch
