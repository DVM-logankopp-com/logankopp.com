Epidurals in Exotic Companion Mammals: A Skunk Case Report and What the Rabbit and Ferret Data Show

November 23, 2026

Campoli and Dutton recently reported a lumbosacral epidural in a 6-year-old pet skunk undergoing right anal sacculectomy. The skunk received 0.25% bupivacaine through a 22-gauge spinal needle, needed no rescue analgesia, and was eating 1 hour after surgery. It is a single case, but it makes a point I want to make to colleagues: the epidural is a technique most of us learned on dogs and cats, and it has a place in exotic companion mammals too. It is easy to forget about when the patient weighs 1 kg instead of 20 kg.

Why an epidural in a small mammal

According to Campoli and Dutton, epidurally administered drugs can provide substantial analgesia with fewer systemic effects than drugs given IM or IV, which matters in small mammals, where systemic anesthetic and analgesic drugs can cause cardiovascular and respiratory depression. Epidural administration can also reduce inhalant requirements and may shorten recovery. The analgesia is directed at the hind limbs and perineum, which makes the technique a good fit for perineal surgery such as anal sacculectomy. They also note that epidurals are commonly used in veterinary medicine for abdominal procedures and surgery on the caudal part of the body.

In dogs and cats, the lumbosacral epidural is routine. The technique Campoli and Dutton used in the skunk was the one routinely applied in cats and dogs, and Ong and colleagues note that sacrococcygeal epidural injection has been performed in some species, especially cats. What differs in exotic companion mammals is the anatomy, the margin for error with volume and dose, and how little species-specific dosing data exist.

The skunk case

The patient was a 6-year 3-month-old neutered male skunk (Mephitis mephitis) weighing 3.89 kg, with a body condition score of 7/9. It presented for removal of the right anal sac after 2 months of unsuccessful medical management of chronic sacculitis, which had included anal sac lavage, intrasaccular clindamycin, oral amoxicillin-clavulanate, and oral meloxicam. An irregular tachycardia had been noted previously, with no abnormalities on cardiac ultrasound.

Anesthesia and the epidural. The skunk was premedicated IM with dexmedetomidine 0.04 mg/kg and methadone 0.2 mg/kg, which produced profound sedation. A 22-gauge cephalic catheter was placed, alfaxalone 1.29 mg/kg IV allowed intubation with a size 3 cuffed tube, and anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane in oxygen on a T-piece. With the skunk in sternal recumbency and the hind limbs extended cranially, the L7 to S1 space was located by palpating the highest points of the iliac wings and the dorsal spinous process of L7. The authors chose sternal over lateral recumbency because the "pop" through the ligamentum flavum is more apparent in that position.

First, a small skin incision was made with a No. 11 blade before the needle went in, to keep a skin plug out of the epidural space and out of the needle. A plug can act as a nidus for inflammation and infection, and if it occludes the needle, you lose the ability to check the hub for CSF. Second, the pop was not clearly felt in this case. Placement was supported by the hanging drop technique and the lack of resistance to injection of sterile saline, and no fluid compatible with CSF was seen in the hub.

Bupivacaine 0.25% was given at 0.15 mL/kg (0.39 mg/kg), a total of 0.6 mL, injected over 40 seconds without resistance.

Intraoperative course. Heart rate fell from 95 to 80 beats per minute after the injection, with stable blood pressure. Mean isoflurane concentration was 2.7% during the first 15 minutes of anesthesia and 1.5% for the 85 minutes after the epidural, with no evident lightening of the plane or nociceptive responses. Mean arterial pressure went from 105 mmHg at the start to 80 mmHg after 1 hour of anesthesia.

Recovery. The skunk was extubated 10 minutes after isoflurane was discontinued and was hypothermic at 36.3 °C, so it went to a heated cage. It ate 1 hour after surgery. At 4 hours after the epidural, it could stand but lacked proprioception in the tarsi. Motor function was normal at 6 hours. Postoperative analgesia was one IV dose of methadone 0.1 mg/kg, IV buprenorphine 0.02 mg/kg 4 hours later, oral meloxicam 0.1 mg/kg once daily, and oral tramadol 10 mg/kg three times daily. Tramadol was stopped at 7 days, when behavior was normal, and meloxicam at 9 days.

What the case adds, and its limits

The authors describe this, to their knowledge, as the first report of successful epidural administration in skunks, while also noting that epidural anesthesia in skunks has been mentioned before. What makes the case useful to me is less the priority claim than the practical detail: a lumbosacral epidural was technically feasible in a 3.9 kg skunk using dog and cat landmarks, the pop was not reliable, and the motor block outlasted the surgery by several hours.

The limitations are specific. This is one animal. The epidural was one part of a multimodal protocol that included dexmedetomidine, methadone, meloxicam, and postoperative opioids, so the contribution of the epidural itself cannot be separated out. The drop in isoflurane after the epidural was not part of a controlled comparison. Postoperative assessment was limited to observation because the skunk was uncooperative. No anatomical description of the epidural space or the end of the dural sac exists for skunks, so the authors could not say how close the needle was to the dural sac. They also point out that species-specific local anesthetic dosing guidance is lacking and that extrapolating from other species can lead to underdosing or overdosing. Because recovery of motor function was slow, they suggest a lower bupivacaine concentration or a smaller volume could be considered to shorten the block, since a dilute concentration may provide analgesia with less motor blockade.

The hypothermia and the hind limb ataxia at 4 hours are worth planning for. Neither is a reason not to do an epidural, but both affect recovery and what families should be told to expect.

Needle placement and confirmation

The skunk case and the rabbit literature agree on one practical problem: the tactile pop through the ligamentum flavum is often minimal or absent in small mammals. Campoli and Dutton cite Lichtenberger's observation that in ferrets the pop may be minimal or hard to detect, particularly with a sharp 25-gauge hypodermic needle, and Salem and colleagues state that the definitive pop is rarely detected in ferrets and rabbits.

Campoli and Dutton discuss needle choice in some detail. Compared with a spinal needle, a hypodermic needle makes the pop harder to feel, and its longer cutting bevel may increase tissue trauma. A stylet helps when the needle is small or at risk of blocking. They used a 22-gauge 1.5-inch Quincke spinal needle and would otherwise have used a Tuohy needle. Because the pop was not felt, they relied on the hanging drop, loss of resistance, and the absence of CSF in the hub. Salem and colleagues also aspirated before injection to check for blood.

Where the spinal cord ends

The site you choose depends on where the spinal cord and dural sac end, and this differs among species.

  • Ferrets. Campoli and Dutton state that the dural sac ends cranial to the L7 to S1 junction, so the lumbosacral space is considered relatively safe from accidental dural puncture.
  • Rabbits. The spinal cord extends into the sacrum. Santos and colleagues note that it extends as far as the second sacral vertebra, and that the number of lumbar vertebrae can vary even among individuals. Ong and colleagues cite an earlier study in which contrast injected at the lumbosacral junction, with epidural injection intended, entered the subarachnoid space in all 7 rabbits.
  • Skunks. No anatomical description of the end of the dural sac has been published, according to Campoli and Dutton.

For rabbits, that anatomy is the argument for moving caudally.

Rabbits: coccygeal epidural with bupivacaine

Ong and colleagues (2020) tested a coccygeal epidural in six healthy male Japanese White rabbits (6 to 33 months old, 3.6 ± 0.2 kg) in a blinded crossover design, with each rabbit receiving 0.5% bupivacaine 0.3 mL/kg (1.5 mg/kg) in one trial and the same volume of saline in the other, 14 days apart. Under propofol induction and isoflurane by face mask, a 27-gauge 70 mm spinal needle was inserted at the intercoccygeal space at the tail base, at about 30 degrees to the skin. The authors found the intervertebral space easier to palpate at the tail than at the sacrococcygeal junction. Needle position was checked with iohexol 0.1 mL/kg under fluoroscopy before the drug was given.

Distribution. No contrast entered the subarachnoid space in any rabbit. On the first contrast injection, contrast was in the epidural space in all 12 injections, but 3 also showed a linear contrast line ventral to the vertebrae that the authors considered possibly a blood vessel, and 1 showed contrast in muscle. The needle was repositioned until contrast was epidural only, which took a third attempt in 2 cases. Across all 18 attempts, the possible vascular line appeared 4 times. The authors did not aspirate before injecting contrast and suggest aspiration may help avoid inadvertent intravascular injection.

Block characteristics. Compared with saline, bupivacaine prolonged the time to recovery of the anal reflex (median 90 minutes, range 40 to 180), hind limb movement during walking (median 60 and 80 minutes for left and right), hind limb conscious proprioception, and pain sensation in the tail (median 110 minutes, range 100 to 200) and left hind limb (median 90 minutes, range 40 to 220). The right hind limb showed the same trend (median 90 minutes, range 20 to 220) without reaching statistical significance (P = 0.059). Forelimbs and mentation were unaffected. Three of the six rabbits had a 60 to 80 minute difference in recovery of pain sensation between the left and right hind limbs, which the authors describe as a unilateral block-like phenomenon. Blood gases and oscillometric blood pressure did not change significantly, and all rabbits were neurologically normal 1 week later.

What the study did not show. These were healthy rabbits that did not have surgery, so the study describes the sensory and motor block, not clinical analgesic efficacy. Neurologic examinations were done every 20 minutes after isoflurane was discontinued, so residual anesthetic may have affected the early scores, and durations are approximate. Plasma bupivacaine concentrations were not measured. The fluoroscopic confirmation step is also not something most practices will do for every case, and without it, the vascular uptake and muscle injections would not have been recognized.

Rabbits: lumbosacral epidural lidocaine, tramadol, or both

Salem and colleagues (2022) studied 24 male New Zealand White rabbits (2.8 to 3.0 kg) undergoing an experimental knee procedure, an osteochondral defect drilled in the trochlear groove, which took about 15 minutes. Rabbits were anesthetized with IM xylazine 5 mg/kg and ketamine. Lumbosacral epidurals were placed with a 20-gauge 50 mm needle, with aspiration, the hanging drop, and loss of resistance used to confirm placement. Groups of eight received epidural lidocaine 2% at 4 mg/kg, tramadol 5% at 4 mg/kg, or both drugs at 4 mg/kg each.

Mean duration of analgesia to pinprick was 23.3 ± 1.1 minutes with lidocaine, 31.5 ± 2.3 minutes with tramadol, and 45.8 ± 3.3 minutes with the combination, and onset was fastest with the combination (46.5 ± 1.4 seconds). Duration of flaccid paralysis was also longest with the combination (35.5 ± 2.9 minutes, compared with 17.6 ± 1.4 for lidocaine and 21.8 ± 3.6 for tramadol). Heart rate, respiratory rate, and rectal temperature fell significantly from baseline in all groups, most in the combination group, where mean heart rate reached 103.7 beats per minute at 60 minutes (baseline 145), respiratory rate reached 34.3 breaths per minute at 75 minutes, and rectal temperature reached 37.2 °C. The authors concluded that the combination could be a better choice than either drug alone.

I would read this study carefully before applying it. There was no group without an epidural, so the study compares epidural drugs with each other, not epidural analgesia with none. The paper is internally inconsistent in places: the ketamine dose is given as 35 mg/kg in the abstract and 15 mg/kg in the methods, butorphanol appears only in the abstract, and the abstract describes the duration of flaccid paralysis as lower in the combination group when the reported values show it was longer. The injected volume was not reported. The cardiorespiratory and temperature decreases were measured under xylazine and ketamine, so they cannot be attributed to the epidural alone.

Rabbits: epidural dexmedetomidine and lidocaine, and a safety signal

Santos and colleagues (2021) gave epidural drugs through a catheter to eight 6-month-old male New Zealand White rabbits (3.1 ± 0.1 kg) in a randomized crossover study, with at least 1 week between treatments. Under isoflurane, an 18-gauge Tuohy needle was used to thread a 20-gauge catheter 5 cm cranially in the epidural space, position was confirmed with contrast radiography, and the rabbits were allowed to recover from anesthesia before the drugs were injected. Treatments were dexmedetomidine 2.5 µg/kg, lidocaine 2 mg/kg, or both, in a final volume of 0.2 mL/kg. Evaluators were blinded. Two rabbits were withdrawn (see below), so results are reported for six.

Antinociception to pinprick was limited to the perineal region with dexmedetomidine alone (mean 11.6 ± 7.6 minutes) and lidocaine alone (mean 15.2 ± 11.0 minutes in the perineal region). The combination produced longer antinociception and extended it to the sacral and lumbar dermatomes, with a mean of 75.5 ± 29.4 minutes in the perineal region, 40.2 ± 26.5 minutes in the sacral region, and 30.7 ± 20.5 minutes in the lumbar region. (The abstract summarizes the combination as extending the block by up to 45 minutes.) Mean arterial pressure decreased with lidocaine and with the combination, by less than 15% from baseline, and respiratory rate decreased with the combination.

The safety finding matters more than the durations. Two of the eight rabbits developed permanent pelvic limb paralysis after the dexmedetomidine and lidocaine combination and were euthanized. Necropsy found spinal cord hemorrhage, congestion, malacia, Wallerian degeneration, and neuronal necrosis. The authors considered two explanations without settling on either. The commercial dexmedetomidine product they used contained methylparaben-derived preservatives and has a pH of 4.0 to 6.0, and they could not exclude preservative toxicity. They also could not exclude trauma from repeated puncture, since each rabbit had a catheter placed for each treatment, with a mean placement time of 43 to 46 minutes. They cite earlier work describing meningeal changes after epidural dexmedetomidine at 10 µg/kg in rabbits.

Eight healthy young male laboratory rabbits with repeated catheterization are not the same as a single-shot epidural in a pet rabbit. However, two cases of permanent paralysis in eight animals is a serious adverse event. That is a reason to check the formulation of anything that goes into the epidural space and not to assume an adjuvant that is well tolerated in dogs will behave the same way in a rabbit.

PK, efficacy, and safety: what these rabbit studies measured

None of these three rabbit studies measured plasma drug concentrations, so none of them provides pharmacokinetic data. None evaluated postoperative analgesia in clinical patients. Ong and colleagues measured sensory and motor block in rabbits that did not have surgery. Salem and colleagues measured intraoperative and short postoperative responses to pinprick during an experimental procedure, without a no-epidural control. Santos and colleagues measured antinociception in conscious rabbits without surgery. The safety information is also limited to small numbers of healthy young males, followed for about 1 week in the case of Ong and colleagues. These are useful technique and block studies, but they do not establish a dose, a volume, or a safety profile for clinical rabbits.

Ferrets: a sacrococcygeal epidural case

Daly and colleagues reported a nerve stimulator-guided sacrococcygeal epidural in a 3-year-old female ferret undergoing bilateral anal sacculectomy, published as a one-page conference abstract. The ferret was premedicated with methadone 0.2 mg/kg and midazolam 0.2 mg/kg SC, then dexmedetomidine 3 µg/kg IM when sedation was not sufficient for IV catheterization, and anesthesia was induced with alfaxalone 2 mg/kg IV and maintained with isoflurane. The epidural space was located with a nerve stimulator set to 0.5 mA and 1 Hz, with a visible tail twitch confirming position. Bupivacaine 0.5% at 0.5 mg/kg and preservative-free morphine 0.1 mg/kg (total volume 0.16 mL) were injected without resistance. No rescue analgesia was needed during surgery, and the ferret was comfortable on postoperative grimace scoring.

The authors state that lumbosacral epidurals have been described in ferrets but sacrococcygeal epidurals had not. This is one animal, described in an abstract rather than a full paper, with the epidural given as part of a multimodal protocol. What it shows is that the technique was feasible in this ferret, with a tail twitch on nerve stimulation used to confirm placement.

How I use epidurals and sacrococcygeal blocks

This section is my clinical practice, not published evidence.

Epidurals are a tool I use in exotic companion mammals, and I think they are something many of us forget about. In guinea pigs, I use sacrococcygeal blocks for ureteral stones. I use sacrococcygeal blocks in ferrets as well. In dogs and cats, epidurals are standard, and that is where most of us learned the technique.

Published neuraxial data in guinea pigs are limited. In the literature I reviewed, the guinea pig work was older laboratory research rather than clinical reports, so my use of these blocks in guinea pigs rests on clinical experience, not on species-specific clinical studies.

A few points from the published work are directly relevant to these blocks. In rabbits, the spinal cord extends into the sacrum, and the Ong contrast study found no subarachnoid spread with a coccygeal approach. In both the skunk and the Ong rabbits, the bupivacaine motor block outlasted the procedure (median 60 to 80 minutes in the rabbits, resolving between 4 and 6 hours in the skunk), which matters for recovery. The Santos results are a reason to be deliberate about what goes into the epidural space.

Other regional blocks in rabbits

The epidural is not the only regional technique with rabbit data, but the other blocks in this group of papers are cadaver studies. They describe anatomy and dye spread, not analgesia.

  • Transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block. Di Bella and colleagues (2021) performed an ultrasound-guided lateral TAP block in 17 New Zealand White rabbit cadavers with 1 mL/kg of 2% lidocaine and methylene blue. T11 was stained in 52% of hemiabdomens and T12, L1, and L2 in 75%, 95%, and 100%, so a single lateral injection did not reliably reach the cranial abdomen. Serino and colleagues (2024) compared one-point (preiliac) and two-point (preiliac plus retrocostal) injections in 30 cadavers. With two points, T10, T11, and T12 were stained in 68.1%, 100%, and 98% of cases, compared with 18%, 52%, and 75% with one point. Both TAP groups used 1 mL/kg of 2% lidocaine in cadavers and note that the dose would need to be considered against the toxic dose of lidocaine, and reduced, in live rabbits.
  • Saphenous nerve block. Felisberto and colleagues (2022) described an ultrasound-guided saphenous block in 12 hind limbs from six cadavers. Both 0.05 and 0.1 mL/kg stained the saphenous nerve consistently without staining the motor branch of the femoral nerve, which suggests the block could provide analgesia for mid to distal hind limb surgery while preserving femoral motor function.
  • Mandibular alveolar nerve block. Serpieri and colleagues (2026) compared in-plane and out-of-plane ultrasound-guided approaches in 12 New Zealand White rabbit cadavers using 0.1 mL/kg of lidocaine and methylene blue, and found no statistically significant differences in image quality, staining, or dye spread between the two approaches.

All four groups of authors call for in vivo studies of efficacy and safety. I've written separately about local and regional anesthesia in companion mammals and about epidurals in ferret anesthesia.

What is still missing

What is missing is clinical data: plasma concentrations after epidural injection in these species, studies of postoperative analgesia in pet rabbits, ferrets, and guinea pigs having real procedures, and anatomical work on the end of the dural sac in skunks. Until those exist, doses and volumes come from small experimental studies and single cases and should be presented that way.
