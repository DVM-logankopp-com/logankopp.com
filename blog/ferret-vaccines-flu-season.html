Ferret Vaccines, Vaccine Reactions, and Flu Season

October 2026

Flu season is starting, and it is a good time to make sure your ferret's vaccines are current. Those two things are more connected for ferrets than for most pets. Ferrets can catch human influenza, and they need a couple of vaccines that many people don't expect a small indoor pet to need.

Here is what I talk through with families at a ferret wellness visit.

Distemper: the one you don't want to skip

Canine distemper is a virus best known in dogs, but ferrets are extremely susceptible to it. In ferrets it is almost always fatal. There is no reliable treatment once a ferret is sick, which is why prevention matters so much.

Indoor ferrets are not completely out of reach. The virus circulates in dogs and in wildlife such as raccoons, foxes, and skunks, all of which live around Middle Tennessee. Contact with an unvaccinated dog, a trip outside, or a new ferret joining the household can all bring it close.

Young ferrets get a series of distemper vaccines, then boosters on a schedule your veterinarian recommends. Use a vaccine your vet has chosen for ferrets. Not every distemper vaccine made for dogs is appropriate.

Rabies: rare, but serious

Rabies in pet ferrets is rare. When it does happen, the consequences are severe, both for the ferret and for any person who was bitten. Rabies is present in wildlife in Tennessee, and a ferret that slips outside or meets a bat in the house is at risk.

A current rabies vaccine also protects your ferret if they ever nip someone. An unvaccinated ferret that bites a person can face a far more difficult situation than a vaccinated one. Your veterinarian can explain how this works locally.

Vaccine reactions: what to watch for

Ferrets are more likely than many species to have an allergic reaction after a vaccine. Most ferrets have no problem at all, but it is worth knowing the signs.

A reaction usually shows up soon after the injection. It can look like:

  • Vomiting or diarrhea
  • Red, blotchy skin or swelling of the face
  • Drooling
  • Weakness, collapse, or trouble breathing

At Priest Lake, we always give the distemper and rabies vaccines on separate days. If there is a reaction, we know which vaccine caused it, and your ferret's immune system only deals with one at a time. We also ask you to stay at the hospital for a little while after the shot so we can watch your ferret.

If your ferret has had a reaction before, tell us. We can plan around it, which may mean pretreating with medication or changing the vaccine schedule. Don't simply skip the next vaccine without talking it over.

Flu season: protect your ferret from you

Ferrets catch human influenza. They get many of the same signs we do: sneezing, a runny nose, watery eyes, fever, tiredness, and a poor appetite. Most healthy adult ferrets recover with supportive care, but very young, older, or already sick ferrets can become seriously ill.

There is no flu vaccine for ferrets. Protection comes from the people around them.

  • If you are sick, limit close contact. Let someone else handle feeding and playtime if you can.
  • Wash your hands before handling your ferret or their food.
  • Don't snuggle face to face while you have symptoms.
  • Get your own flu shot. It helps protect your household, and your ferret is part of it.

Ferrets may be able to pass the flu back to people as well, so if your ferret is sneezing, wash your hands after handling them.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your ferret:

  • Vomits, collapses, swells, or has trouble breathing after a vaccine
  • Stops eating, or seems weak or wobbly
  • Seems feverish and lethargic, has thick nasal discharge, or is breathing hard
  • Was exposed to a sick dog or wildlife and isn't vaccinated

Book a visit soon if:

  • Your ferret is due or overdue for vaccines
  • Flu-like signs are mild but not improving after a few days
  • You have a new ferret and aren't sure of their vaccine history
