Common Tumors in Ferrets and Rodents: A Clinical Update

November 28, 2026

As ferrets and pet rodents live longer with better care, more of them develop neoplasia. Chung and Lennox recently updated the Veterinary Clinics of North America: Exotic Animal Practice review of ferret and rodent oncology, last covered in that series in 2017. Most new publications since then describe new tumor types rather than new treatments or outcomes, so a lot of the guidance remains familiar. The review is still a useful organized reference, and it includes several practical observations from the authors' own caseload.

A note on the data: for mice and rats, most of what we know comes from laboratory animal medicine, where tumor prevalence varies with age, diet, genetic strain, and environment.

General principles

The workup follows the same logic as in other species: imaging (radiographs, CT, ultrasound), fine needle aspiration and cytology, and ideally biopsy with histopathology. The authors recommend excisional biopsy where possible, since it serves as both diagnosis and treatment.

  • Surgery remains the treatment of choice for any tumor with a reasonable chance of complete excision, even in very small patients.
  • Incomplete excision can still be worthwhile. Previous studies in exotic species have shown improved quality of life even with incomplete removal, and it can be offered as palliative care.
  • Chemotherapy and radiation are being reported more often. Published accounts are limited, but the authors encourage offering diagnostics and treatment to all ferret and rodent cancer patients.
  • GnRH agonists may have a role because some reproductive tumors may be sex hormone responsive.

Ferrets

Spontaneous tumors are often seen in ferrets after 3 years of age, and more than one tumor type may be present at the same time. Ferret tumors also behave differently than the same tumor types in dogs and cats, which changes treatment.

Insulinoma

Insulinoma is the most common tumor in ferrets. The median age is 5 years, with a slightly higher incidence reported in males. Unlike the highly malignant insulinomas of dogs and cats, ferret insulinomas are generally only locally metastatic and infiltrative. High-carbohydrate diets and genetics are thought to increase risk.

Signs include lethargy, stargazing, hind limb weakness, ptyalism, pawing at the mouth, seizures, and coma. They often wax and wane and improve after eating.

  • Diagnosis. A blood glucose below 60 mg/dL (3.3 mmol/L) after a 4-hour fast is suggestive but not diagnostic. Repeat measurements should be run by the laboratory or on a benchtop analyzer. Handheld glucometers can be used for quick assessment and trends, but the authors note they're often inaccurate for diagnosis. (I've written about portable glucometers in ferrets separately.) Plasma insulin is elevated in 83% of cases. Ultrasound and CT may identify pancreatic nodules, which are generally hypoechoic and smooth-margined.
  • Medical management. Prednisolone, which many clinicians use first line, and diazoxide, alone or together, with frequent glucose monitoring.
  • Surgery. Partial pancreatectomy or nodulectomy removes tumor burden, but recurrence is common, and removing too much pancreas risks iatrogenic diabetes mellitus. Comparisons of medical and surgical survival are conflicting.
  • Long-term feeding. Encourage eating every 4 to 6 hours on a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet.

Adrenocortical disease

ACD is the second most common neoplasia of ferrets, with no sex predilection and an average age of 4.5 years. Lesions range from hyperplasia to adenocarcinoma, and all can overproduce sex hormones. Early neutering, irregular day length, and genetics are proposed contributors.

Classic signs are symmetrical alopecia starting at the tail base, pruritus, increased odor, sexual behavior in males, and vulvar swelling in up to 90% of females. In males, periprostatic or periurethral cysts and prostatic enlargement can cause urinary obstruction.

  • Diagnosis is often based on classic signs, supported by adrenal imaging (ultrasound, contrast CT) and sex hormone measurement. Estradiol, 17α-hydroxyprogesterone, and androstenedione also rise in estrus, and UCCR can't distinguish ACD from an intact ferret. In the US, only the University of Tennessee Veterinary Medical Center Endocrinology Laboratory offers the hormone panel. Adrenal invasion of the vena cava carries a poorer prognosis.
  • Medical treatment. Deslorelin acetate is conditionally licensed in the US for ACD in ferrets, and its effect can last up to a year or more. Leuprolide may last up to 30 days. Anecdotally, these reduce hormone production and signs but don't affect tumor development. Melatonin helps temporarily. Ketoconazole and mitotane are not recommended.
  • Surgery. Left adrenalectomy is straightforward. The right gland's relationship to the vena cava makes complete resection difficult, and many surgeons perform complete left and subtotal right adrenalectomy. In one study, surgery gave an average disease-free period of 13.6 months (range 0 to 38). Survival with medical and surgical treatment is described as nearly identical, which is why many ferrets are now managed medically.

Lymphoma

Lymphoma is the third most common tumor type, at 10% to 15% of ferret neoplasia. It occurs at any age and in either sex. Contrary to older teaching, younger ferrets don't develop more aggressive forms. Presentations include multicentric, gastrointestinal, mediastinal, and cutaneous disease, with respiratory or cardiac signs if there's a mediastinal mass or pleural effusion.

Epitheliotropic lymphoma in ferrets usually doesn't progress to systemic disease, unlike in dogs and people. Several chemotherapy protocols have been published with successful outcomes. Dose by body surface area rather than weight. Radiation has been described and can be curative, and prednisolone is an option for palliation.

Skin tumors

The skin is the third most common site of neoplasia in ferrets. Most skin tumors are benign and malignant ones tend to be slow-growing, so early excision with wide margins is recommended.

  • Mast cell tumors are common in males and older ferrets (average 4.5 to 5 years), benign, usually pruritic, and sometimes hemorrhagic. About 30% present with more than one tumor. Recurrence can indicate a poorer prognosis.
  • Sebaceous epitheliomas look warty and aggressive and can grow large, but they're benign. Multiple tumors and recurrence are common.
  • Preputial gland tumors are the exception. They're malignant and metastasize locally, so early identification and excision matter. Penile amputation with urethrostomy and radiation are options for advanced tumors.
  • Electrochemotherapy with bleomycin was recently reported to produce complete responses in mast cell tumors, a squamous papilloma, and a sebaceous adenoma in ferrets, without surgery.

Chordoma

Chordomas are more common in males and are usually low-grade, slow-growing, non-metastatic masses at the tail tip. Those are firm, white, club-like, and easily excised. They can also occur along the vertebral column, and radiographs help with surgical planning when a tumor looks locally aggressive.

Guinea pigs

Skin and subcutaneous masses

Skin tumors make up 15% of guinea pig neoplasms. In a retrospective study of 619 biopsies of externally palpable masses, 88.9% came from the skin and subcutis, and lipoma was the most common diagnosis (286 of 619). Other reports find trichofolliculomas more common. Trichofolliculomas are benign hair follicle tumors, most common in males and on the dorsum, and can involve the coccygeal gland. Both are generally cured by complete excision. The authors commonly remove smaller skin tumors in guinea pigs under sedation with local analgesia.

Mammary tumors

Mammary tumors occur in both sexes but are more prevalent in males. Reported malignancy rates vary from 30% to 50% up to 75%. In one series of 54 mammary masses, 26 were malignant, 20 benign, and 8 hyperplastic. Metastasis is reported as low, but because some tumors spread to the lungs, thoracic imaging is recommended. Treatment is excision with wide margins and local lymph node removal. Masses left in place can grow large, ulcerate, and become infected.

Uterine tumors

In a study of 83 pet guinea pigs, uterine tumors were more often benign than malignant, with leiomyoma the most common. Size didn't correlate with malignancy. Leiomyomas often occur alongside cystic ovaries: all 7 guinea pigs with leiomyomas in one study had cystic rete ovarii, and in another, 64.5% of guinea pigs with ovarian cysts had concurrent uterine neoplasia. Signs include abdominal distension, a palpable mass, hemorrhagic vaginal discharge, and pain. Surgical excision is recommended. In the authors' experience, very large tumors of the short uterine body and cervix were often nonresectable but regressed after ovariectomy and partial hysterectomy. I've covered the ovarian side in my post on ovarian cysts and spaying in guinea pigs.

Endocrine tumors

  • Thyroid. Thyroid tumors made up 2.4% of submitted tumors in one review and 3.6% of tissue submissions at another laboratory. They may be functional or not. Signs include a ventral neck mass (not always palpable), weight loss despite a normal appetite, polyphagia, hyperactivity, murmurs or arrhythmias, and PU/PD in guinea pigs over 3 years old. The authors have also observed hyperthermia in hyperthyroid guinea pigs. Treatment options include thyroidectomy, I-131, and methimazole.
  • Pituitary and adrenal. These can cause hyperadrenocorticism in middle-aged to older guinea pigs, with bilateral symmetric alopecia, thin skin, PU/PD, and weakness. Endogenous ACTH and ACTH stimulation testing look promising with recently established reference intervals. Trilostane titrated to effect has been described.
  • Insulinoma. Reported cases have all been in guinea pigs older than 5 years, presenting with acute neurologic signs from hypoglycemia.

Lymphoma and other tumors

Lymphoma and leukemia in guinea pigs present with a poor coat, lethargy, anorexia, and lymphadenopathy, and they tend to be high stage with a poor prognosis. Epitheliotropic T-cell lymphoma causes pruritic alopecia and scaling. Splenic hemangiosarcoma is rarely reported, with surgery the treatment of choice. In one study, pulmonary tumors were found in 4.7% of cases (116 of 2474), split almost evenly between adenocarcinomas and adenomas.

Rats

  • Mammary tumors. Fibroadenomas are the most common tumor in female rats and also occur in males. One study found 80% to 90% of mammary tumors were fibroadenomas, and another found 53% benign fibroadenomas and 12% carcinomas. Masses can appear anywhere from neck to tail, grow quickly, impede movement, ulcerate, and recur at new sites. In the authors' experience, adenomas tend to separate easily from surrounding tissue, while malignant tumors don't and may invade muscle. Radiation didn't appear to slow fibroadenoma progression. Ovariectomy before 90 days of age reduces mammary tumor prevalence. In one study of rats with fibroadenomas, 40% had concurrent reproductive tract abnormalities and 75% had pituitary tumors. My post on mammary tumors in pet rats covers this from the family side.
  • Pituitary adenomas are common and increase with age. They cause ventral vestibular signs, hind limb paresis, vision changes, and sometimes spontaneous lactation from prolactin-secreting tumors. MRI or CT helps diagnose them, and an ultrasound technique has been described. Cabergoline may shrink the mass and improve signs but isn't curative.
  • Zymbal gland tumors are usually malignant carcinomas that appear as firm, often ulcerated masses below the ear. They can be mistaken for squamous cell carcinoma, and their location makes surgery difficult.

Mice

Mammary tumors in mice are typically malignant, usually metastasize, and recur often after excision, so the prognosis is poor. Pulmonary tumors are common in mice, unlike other rodents. Patients are usually subclinical until late, and no treatments have been reported in pet mice.

Hamsters and gerbils

  • Hamster lymphoma is the most common hamster neoplasm, especially in Syrian hamsters. There are three forms: a polyomavirus-induced epizootic form in young hamsters, multicentric lymphoma in older hamsters, and a cutaneous form resembling epitheliotropic T-cell lymphoma that doesn't respond to standard skin treatments. Chemotherapy has been used in Syrian hamsters.
  • Djungarian hamsters have a neoplasia prevalence 5 times that of Syrian hamsters, and skin tumors are common in them.
  • Syrian hamsters can develop cutaneous melanomas of the flank organs, and males are 10 times more likely to develop them than females.
  • Hamster hyperadrenocorticism has been reported in only 4 pet hamsters. Metyrapone was successful in one case where ketoconazole and mitotane were not.
  • Gerbils. Ovarian granulosa cell tumors are the most common gerbil tumor, usually in young females and more often in unbred ones. Ventral scent gland tumors in older gerbils have a high metastatic rate, and castration is recommended at the time of removal because the glands are androgen dependent.

Chinchillas

Chinchillas have lower reported rates of neoplasia than guinea pigs or rats.
