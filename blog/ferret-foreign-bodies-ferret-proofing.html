Ferret-Proofing Your Home and What to Do if Your Ferret Swallows Something

October 2026

If you've ever watched a ferret dive into a pile of laundry and come out with a sock, you know they explore the world with their mouths. That curiosity is a big part of their charm. It's also why swallowed objects are one of the most common reasons ferrets need surgery, especially young ferrets under about two years old.

Why a small bite becomes a big problem

Ferrets have a short, fast digestive tract built for a meat-based diet. Food passes through in just a few hours, which leaves little room for anything that isn't food. A small piece of rubber or foam can lodge at the outlet of the stomach or in the small intestine and block it.

Rubber and foam are the usual culprits in my practice: rubber toys, foam padding and door stops. In general, anything soft and chewy is a risk, including dog and cat toys, furniture foam, earplugs, shoe insoles, erasers, balloons and elastic bands.

Ferret-proofing, from the floor up

The best way to ferret-proof is to get down at ferret level and look around. If your ferret can reach something rubbery or spongy, assume they will chew it. Choose hard, durable toys made for ferrets, and throw out any toy that's starting to break apart.

Furniture deserves a close look. Ferrets love to burrow into couches and recliners, where they can chew the foam inside and be hurt by reclining mechanisms. Block the gaps behind appliances and under cabinets, keep shoes, bags and laundry off the floor, and do a quick sweep of the room before playtime. Supervised play outside the cage is safest.

Signs your ferret may have swallowed something

Signs can be dramatic or surprisingly mild, and they may come and go over days. A ferret with a foreign body may eat less or stop eating, sleep more, grind their teeth (often a sign of pain), vomit, paw at the mouth or drool. Black, tarry stools can mean bleeding in the stomach or intestines. Some ferrets have diarrhea, while others pass very little at all.

None of these signs is specific to a swallowed object. Stomach ulcers, infections, inflammation and low blood sugar can all look similar, which is why an exam matters rather than waiting it out.

How it's diagnosed

I start with a careful exam, and sometimes an object can be felt in the belly. Bloodwork and X-rays come next. Here's the tricky part: rubber often doesn't show up on X-rays. Ultrasound helps, and sometimes a contrast X-ray study is needed.

What treatment involves

If an object is causing a blockage, surgery is usually the answer. During surgery I examine the whole abdomen systematically.

I'll also be honest with you up front. When the diagnosis isn't certain, there's a chance surgery won't find an object. If that happens, I take small tissue samples from the stomach and intestines so we can still learn what's causing the problem, such as inflammation or infection. Exploring with a plan is far better than waiting while a ferret gets sicker.

While we work things out, your ferret will receive supportive care: fluids, pain relief, stomach protection and a special carnivore recovery food. When surgery is done promptly, most ferrets bounce back well.

A word about diet

Ferrets do best on a high-quality diet built on animal protein and fat, with very little fiber or sugar. I generally don't recommend raw diets because of the risk of bacterial infections, including Salmonella, which can also spread to people.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your ferret has stopped eating, is very lethargic, is vomiting repeatedly, has black, tarry stools, or is weak or collapsed. For a weak or collapsed ferret, please call before you put them in the car.

Book a prompt visit if your ferret is eating less than usual, grinding their teeth, losing weight, or has diarrhea or green, slimy stools, especially after a new ferret has joined the household.
