Analgesia in Rabbits: What the Evidence Supports and Where It Runs Thin

November 10, 2026

The paper opens with an uncomfortable finding. In the meta-analysis Ozawa and colleagues cite, the proportion of rabbits that received analgesics after surgery didn't change between two time periods several years apart. The authors suspect rabbits are still undertreated, partly from worry about adverse effects and partly from unfamiliarity with drugs and doses.

Untreated pain has its own costs. Sympathetic activation affects perfusion, appetite, GI motility, immune response, and wound healing, and all of those can raise morbidity and mortality in rabbits.

The article

Ozawa S, Cenani A, Sanchez-Migallon Guzman D. Treatment of pain in rabbits. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2023;26:201-227. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2022.09.001

This is a narrative review. Most of the studies it summarizes were done in healthy, young New Zealand White rabbits, and many measured plasma concentrations or MAC reduction rather than analgesia. Plasma targets are often borrowed from other species. Doses below are as the authors state them. Check them against a current formulary.

Two principles: multimodal and preemptive

Multimodal. Rabbits that received buprenorphine and meloxicam together gained body weight, while those that received either drug alone didn't. Combining drugs also lets you use lower doses of each.

Preemptive. Buprenorphine given before colorectal distension blunted the cardiovascular response, but the same drug given during distension didn't. Analgesia works better when it's on board before the stimulus.

Buprenorphine: shorter than we thought

Buprenorphine is the most commonly used analgesic in rabbits, and longer duration comes with higher doses. The review lists 0.03 to 0.1 mg/kg IV, SC, or IM every 4 to 6 hours. Onset is about 30 minutes after IV administration.

The data on duration are worth knowing:

  • After 0.05 mg/kg SC of buprenorphine HCl, plasma concentrations stayed above levels considered analgesic in other species for only 4 hours.
  • SC bioavailability was about 50% of IM.
  • Rabbits given buprenorphine HCl at 0.2 mg/kg SC every 12 hours after tibial implant surgery had pain scores indicating moderate to severe pain at 12 hours, before the next dose.
  • Oral transmucosal buprenorphine needed large volumes, gave lower concentrations than SC, and is not recommended in rabbits.

In a 2016 degree project at the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, Freijs compared oral transmucosal buprenorphine at 0.15 mg/kg with subcutaneous buprenorphine at 0.05 mg/kg, each given every 8 hours, in 18 female New Zealand White rabbits after a segment of radius was removed. Median peak plasma concentration was 0.74 ng/mL after transmucosal dosing and 1.16 ng/mL after subcutaneous dosing, and the median area under the curve was 41 ng·h/mL versus 337 ng·h/mL. The transmucosal dose took a large volume and was difficult to give, and plasma concentrations varied widely with both routes.

Freijs E. Comparison of plasma levels and analgesic effect between oral transmucosal and subcutaneous administration of buprenorphine in rabbits. Second cycle, A2E. Uppsala: Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, Dept. of Clinical Sciences; 2016. Examensarbete 2016:46. https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/9518/

Longer-acting formulations. High-concentration buprenorphine (Simbadol) at 0.24 mg/kg SC kept plasma concentrations above effective levels in other species for up to 42 hours, and sustained-release buprenorphine at 0.15 mg/kg SC did so for up to 77 hours. The SR formulation took 15 hours to reach effective levels, so it can't provide immediate analgesia. Neither study measured analgesia.

After the 0.24 mg/kg high-concentration dose, two rabbits developed depression, nystagmus, and ataxia. One seized and was euthanized. The cause of the seizure wasn't determined.

Other opioids

  • Butorphanol has a half-life of only 1.64 hours after IV administration, and rabbit analgesia data are lacking. The authors don't recommend it for overtly painful conditions.
  • Hydromorphone has a clinical dose of 0.1 to 0.4 mg/kg in the review's table. Oral bioavailability was only 20.4%, so it isn't recommended PO.
  • Methadone at 0.2 mg/kg SC didn't reduce food intake or fecal output compared with buprenorphine or hydromorphone.
  • Fentanyl patches produced variable plasma concentrations depending on the site and whether the skin was shaved or depilated. Analgesia wasn't measured, and in one postoperative study a fentanyl patch made no difference in activity, weight, or fecal output compared with no analgesia. Rabbits groom, so eating the patch is a possible risk.

Opioids and the gut

This is the question I hear most. The data conflict:

  • One study found more pyloric and duodenal contractions and no change in feces after a single buprenorphine dose.
  • Others found delayed and reduced fecal output with repeated buprenorphine, likely from reduced food intake. SR buprenorphine also reduced fecal output.
  • Morphine and butorphanol at high doses slowed GI transit on ultrasound, but those doses are higher than we use clinically.

The authors' key point is that nearly all of these studies were done in healthy rabbits. Pain itself may cause ileus. Any opioid effect on motility usually resolves once the drug is stopped, for example after 2 to 3 days of postoperative use. Their conclusion is that opioids are warranted in painful rabbits, with nutritional support and monitoring of food intake and fecal output.

Opioids can also depress breathing. Buprenorphine at 0.03 or 0.06 mg/kg in healthy awake rabbits lowered respiratory rate and caused mild hypoxemia. This is more of a concern in sick rabbits.

Tramadol: mixed evidence

Tramadol is widely used for mild to moderate pain in rabbits, but:

  • Oral tramadol at 11 mg/kg didn't reach plasma concentrations considered analgesic in other species. The active M1 metabolite reached them for only 45 minutes, in 2 of 6 rabbits.
  • At 4.4 mg/kg IV, the reduction in isoflurane MAC was statistically significant but clinically unimportant.
  • At 10 to 15 mg/kg IM, rabbits had higher creatinine and liver enzymes than controls, with renal tubular and hepatic fatty degeneration on histopathology. The authors suggest monitoring kidney and liver function with long-term use.
  • It's bitter, so palatability can be a problem.

NSAIDs: dose matters

Meloxicam and carprofen are the most used NSAIDs in rabbits.

  • Meloxicam at 1 mg/kg PO once daily maintained plasma concentrations effective in other species, with minimal accumulation and no adverse effects for up to 29 days.
  • Lower doses of 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg PO gave inadequate plasma concentrations.
  • After ovariohysterectomy, only the group given 1 mg/kg PO on day one and then 0.5 mg/kg PO once daily showed some pain control. Even that group still showed some pain behaviors, which suggests additional analgesia is needed for this surgery.
  • At 1.5 mg/kg daily for 7 days, rabbits had mild hepatic and renal changes that resolved by day 10. At 3 mg/kg, they had severe hepatic necrosis and glomerular damage that persisted.

Sarvi and colleagues gave 6 New Zealand White rabbits one oral dose of meloxicam at 1 mg/kg and measured plasma prostaglandin E2 and thromboxane B2 as markers of COX-1 and COX-2. Both fell below baseline, with the largest mean reductions to 80% of baseline for thromboxane B2 and 60% of baseline for prostaglandin E2 at 8 hours, and prostaglandin E2 then rose toward baseline between 8 and 24 hours. Lethargy, inappetence, and changes in fecal production were not seen, and the authors concluded that meloxicam appeared to inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2 and that 1 mg/kg orally could provide analgesia, though dosing more often than once daily may be needed to maintain the effect.

Sarvi JY, Gardhouse SM, Kleinhenz MD, Hocker SE, Weeder MM, Montgomery SR, Rooney TA. Measurement of cyclooxygenase products in plasma as markers for inhibition of cyclooxygenase isoforms by oral meloxicam in New Zealand White rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus). J Am Assoc Lab Anim Sci. 2023;62(3):254-259. doi:10.30802/AALAS-JAALAS-22-000109

Carprofen has been studied less. Carprofen plus buprenorphine after maxillary surgery made no difference in facial pain expression compared with saline.

Grapiprant, an EP4 receptor antagonist, at 2 mg/kg IV increased thermal withdrawal latency for up to 10 hours in an inflammatory pain model, similar to meloxicam 0.5 mg/kg SC. Oral dosing hasn't been established.

The authors call adverse NSAID events rare at appropriate doses, but recommend monitoring renal, hepatic, and GI function.

Adjuncts

  • Gabapentin at 25 mg/kg SC reached plasma concentrations analgesic in humans. Its effect on acute pain in rabbits is unknown. Clinical dosing every 8 to 12 hours is extrapolated from other species, and sedation is the most common side effect.
  • Ketamine as a CRI at 40 µg/kg/min reduced isoflurane MAC. Little to no research exists on its analgesic properties in rabbits.
  • Alpha-2 agonists provide sedation and analgesia but have cardiovascular effects. Myocardial necrosis has been reported after xylazine-ketamine anesthesia.
  • Maropitant at 1 mg/kg reached antiemetic concentrations in other species, but lower concentrations than are associated with visceral analgesia in dogs. Its efficacy as a visceral analgesic in rabbits hasn't been evaluated. SC injection caused skin reactions in several rabbits.
  • Lidocaine CRI. After ovariohysterectomy, lidocaine (2 mg/kg bolus, then 100 µg/kg/min for 2 days) led to better GI motility, food intake, and fecal output than buprenorphine at 0.06 mg/kg IV every 8 hours. Pain scores didn't differ, but the lidocaine group showed more normal behaviors.

Locoregional techniques

Limitations

This is a narrative review. Most of the underlying studies used healthy New Zealand White rabbits, measured plasma concentrations or MAC reduction rather than analgesia, and used plasma targets from other species. Several doses in the review's table are based on clinical experience and anecdotal reports. Opioid GI data come from healthy rabbits, not painful ones.

How I'd use this

  1. Give buprenorphine IV or IM when you can. That's my usual choice in rabbits. Base the redosing interval on these duration data rather than on habit.
  2. Don't count on SR buprenorphine for the first 15 hours. Give something fast-acting with it.
  3. Treat the pain rather than avoiding opioids for the gut's sake. Support nutrition and track fecal output.
  4. Use meloxicam at doses with data behind them. I give the NSAID after surgery, once the rabbit is warm and blood pressure is stable.
  5. Run a lidocaine or ketamine CRI for longer or GI cases. In my practice, ketamine also goes in with the premedication or at co-induction for most rabbits.
  6. If you can't tell sedation from pain, assume pain and reassess.
