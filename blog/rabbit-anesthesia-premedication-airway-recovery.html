Rabbit Anesthesia: Premedication, Airway Options, and Recovery

November 10, 2026

Rabbits have a reputation as risky anesthetic patients, and the numbers back it up. In the studies Gardhouse and Sanchez cite, overall anesthetic mortality in rabbits ranges from 1.39% to 4.8%. In the larger of those studies, it was 0.73% in healthy rabbits and 7.37% in systemically ill rabbits. The detail I bring up most with colleagues is when those deaths happen. In that same study, rabbits were as likely to die in the 3 hours after anesthesia as during it, and deaths occurred up to 48 hours later.

So rabbit anesthesia is not just the time on the table. It starts with the history and ends when the rabbit is eating and passing feces at home.

The article

Gardhouse S, Sanchez A. Rabbit sedation and anesthesia. Vet Clin North Am Exot Anim Pract. 2022;25:181-210. doi:10.1016/j.cvex.2021.08.012

This is a narrative review written by an exotic companion mammal clinician and a veterinary anesthesiologist. The doses below are the ones the authors list, which they describe as suggestions based on published data and their own experience. Check them against a current formulary before using them.

Before anesthesia

Rabbits hide illness, so the healthy-looking rabbit on the exam table may not be the anesthetic candidate it appears to be. The authors recommend a full physical exam with cardiac, respiratory, and GI assessment, an accurate body weight, and at minimum a PCV, total protein, and blood glucose. A CBC and chemistry panel is better.

The authors cite work linking hyperglycemia to stress, anorexia, and severity of disease, with severe hyperglycemia (above 20 mmol/L, or 360 mg/dL) associated with a poor prognosis. In that work, rabbits with intestinal obstruction had a mean glucose of 24.7 mmol/L (444.6 mg/dL), compared with 8.5 mmol/L (153 mg/dL) in nonobstructive ileus. Cholesterol and triglycerides are flagged as markers of sepsis, renal failure, and hepatopathy. All of this belongs in the risk conversation with the family.

If fluid deficits or ileus are suspected, stabilize first.

Fasting. Rabbits can't vomit, and long fasts are contraindicated. The authors suggest removing food 1 to 2 hours before anesthesia, after premedication, mainly so there is less food in the mouth at intubation.

Temperature. The authors cite an association between hypothermia at admission and mortality in rabbits. Warming should start at premedication and continue until the rabbit is fully recovered and normothermic.

Stress. House rabbits away from dogs, cats, ferrets, and birds of prey, give them a hide and a litter box, and keep bonded partners together when possible.

Premedication

Premedication reduces catecholamine release, makes IV access easier, and lowers induction and inhalant requirements. Given the limited data and the higher risk, the authors favor reversible drugs.

The protocols they list include:

  • Midazolam plus butorphanol plus ketamine for nonpainful procedures such as radiographs, CT, and catheter placement (midazolam 0.5 to 2 mg/kg, butorphanol 0.5 to 2 mg/kg, ketamine 3 to 5 mg/kg, SC or IM).
  • Midazolam plus hydromorphone plus ketamine for potentially painful procedures or as a surgical premed (hydromorphone 0.1 to 0.3 mg/kg SC or IM, with the midazolam and ketamine doses above).
  • Midazolam plus butorphanol or hydromorphone plus dexmedetomidine for young, healthy rabbits that need moderate to profound sedation (I usually use dexmedetomidine at 5 µg/kg, and at most 15 µg/kg, SC or IM).

A few drug-specific points from the review:

  • Midazolam alone at 2 mg/kg IM gave mild sedation for 30 to 60 minutes with minimal respiratory effect.
  • Butorphanol is suited to mild pain only and has minimal MAC-sparing effect.
  • Alpha-2 agonists cause vasoconstriction, reduced cardiac output, and bradyarrhythmias. Avoid them in rabbits with cardiovascular compromise.
  • Diazepam's propylene glycol can cause hypotension and thrombophlebitis, which is why midazolam is preferred.
  • Routine anticholinergics are not recommended. Atropine is rapidly hydrolyzed in many rabbits, and anticholinergics have negative effects on GI motility in hindgut fermenters.

Induction

The authors recommend injectable induction over mask or chamber induction when possible. Preoxygenate by face mask first. A well-fitted mask needs more than 100 mL/kg/min of oxygen, and a loose mask may need about 300 mL/kg/min.

  • Propofol, 2 to 8 mg/kg IV. Apnea and dose-dependent hypotension are common, especially with rapid injection. The authors suggest giving the calculated dose in quarter increments over 30 to 60 seconds. I generally give 0.5 mg/kg, wait 30 to 60 seconds, then give more as needed. I use ketamine 0.25 to 1 mg/kg IV as a co-induction agent to help minimize the propofol dose. I looked at propofol with or without dextroketamine in rabbits earlier this fall.
  • Alfaxalone, 1 to 4 mg/kg IV. Apnea is also common. I generally give 0.5 mg/kg, wait 30 to 60 seconds, then give more as needed. I use ketamine 0.25 to 1 mg/kg IV as a co-induction agent to help minimize the alfaxalone dose. IM alfaxalone at 4 and 6 mg/kg was tolerated in one study, but a rabbit died after 8 mg/kg IM.
  • Ketamine 2.5 to 5 mg/kg plus midazolam 0.5 mg/kg IV. Apnea is uncommon, and MAP and heart rate increase.

Airway options

Each option has a place.

Orotracheal intubation is the standard when you need to ventilate. It is also the hardest, because of the narrow oral cavity, the large tongue base, the limited jaw opening, and the risk of laryngospasm. Tubes are usually 2.0 to 3.5 mm ID and should be measured to the thoracic inlet and cut so you don't intubate a bronchus. Small tubes kink and plug with saliva, and capnography is the fastest way to catch that. Tracheal injury and stricture have been reported after both cuffed and uncuffed tubes.

The rabbit v-gel is a supraglottic device shaped for rabbit anatomy. It is not my favorite option, and a v-gel should always be used with ETCO2 (capnography) monitoring. In the study the authors cite, placement took 14 to 38 seconds, compared with up to 171 seconds for an endotracheal tube, and it allows positive pressure ventilation when seated well. Reported problems include lingual edema and cyanosis, gastric tympany, and dislodgement when the head moves. For long procedures, prolonged ventilation, or a lot of head repositioning, the authors favor an endotracheal tube.

A face mask is reasonable for a very short procedure, such as a brief incisor trim, but it leaks badly if you need to ventilate.

Maintenance and hypotension

The authors describe rabbits as more sensitive than other species to inhalant vasodilation, with hypotension at levels as low as 1 MAC. They suggest this may contribute to the higher mortality. A balanced approach reduces how much inhalant you need. Fentanyl, ketamine, and lidocaine infusions all have reported MAC-sparing effects in rabbits, while tramadol and meloxicam did not show clinically significant MAC reduction.

When hypotension does occur, the pressor choice matters. In the studies cited, dopamine was ineffective for isoflurane-induced hypotension in rabbits and phenylephrine was only minimally effective, while norepinephrine (0.5 to 1.0 µg/kg/min) looked potentially effective.

Lidocaine deserves its own mention. In the postoperative ovariohysterectomy study the authors cite, rabbits on a lidocaine CRI for 2 days had better GI motility, higher food intake, more fecal output, and more normal behaviors than rabbits on buprenorphine 0.06 mg/kg IV every 8 hours. Pain scores did not differ between the groups.

Monitoring

  • Blood pressure. Doppler is generally preferred over oscillometry in rabbits, because oscillometric units struggle with heart rates above 200 bpm, small patients, and hypothermia. In one study of 17 rabbits, Doppler at the dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery agreed well with direct pressure, and Doppler readings below 80 mm Hg reliably indicated hypotension. A smaller study of 6 rabbits found both methods poor. If you use oscillometry, use the forelimb.
  • ECG. Alligator clips tear rabbit skin. Don't shave the plantar feet for adhesive pads, because that fur protects against pododermatitis.
  • Pulse oximetry has been validated in rabbits at saturations above 85%.
  • Capnography gives a useful estimate of PaCO2 in intubated rabbits, and it is the quickest way to spot a kinked or plugged tube.
  • Temperature should be monitored continuously. Heated anesthetic gas raised body temperature by about 1 °C in one study.

Recovery

Recovery deserves the same attention as induction. Keep the rabbit on oxygen until spontaneous breathing and movement return, and keep oxygen available by mask or cage after extubation until the rabbit is fully alert. Move the rabbit to a warm, dark, quiet cage and keep checking temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and comfort. Offer food and water as soon as the rabbit is moving confidently. If intake or fecal output drops, start assisted feeding and reassess analgesia.

Limitations

This is a narrative review, not a trial. Much of the rabbit data comes from small studies in healthy New Zealand White rabbits, and some recommendations are extrapolated from dogs, cats, and people. The authors also note that breed and strain affect drug responses, and that there is little information on how age, sex, or illness change them.

How I'd use this

For rabbits in my practice, this review lines up with how I think about the anesthetic period:

  1. Premedicate with reversible drugs and an opioid that matches the procedure. Ketamine in the premed or at co-induction is part of most of my rabbit protocols.
  2. Pick the airway for the procedure, not out of habit. A v-gel is not my favorite option. A v-gel should always be used with ETCO2 (capnography) monitoring, including for a short procedure with the head still. An endotracheal tube is better when you need control for longer.
  3. Treat hypotension as expected, not as a surprise. Have Doppler on before induction, and reduce the inhalant with locoregional and infusion analgesia rather than chasing pressure with the vaporizer alone. Norepinephrine is often my drug of choice for treating hypotension.
  4. Staff recovery like it matters. The data say it does. Watch closely, and monitor and record values during recovery, including temperature and mentation, heart rate, and respiration.
