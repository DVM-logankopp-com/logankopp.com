One Dose of Oral Fluralaner for Sarcoptic Mange in Rabbits

November 9, 2026

Sarcoptes isn't a mite I see often in rabbits here in Middle Tennessee. Cheyletiella and Psoroptes keep us much busier. But when Sarcoptes does show up, it tends to arrive as a crusted, miserable rabbit that has already been through a round or two of treatment, and a family that is starting to itch themselves.

So a paper with 124 rabbits, a single dose, and a 12-week follow-up got my attention this week.

The paper

Ghadaksaz S, Rostami A, Arabkhazaeli F, et al. Efficacy of oral fluralaner for treatment and prevention of sarcoptic mange in pet rabbits: a clinical case series of 124 rabbits with risk factor assessment. Curr Res Parasitol Vector Borne Dis. 2026;10:100452. doi:10.1016/j.crpvbd.2026.100452

What they did

This was a prospective clinical case series in pet rabbits. Of the 124 rabbits enrolled, 95 had confirmed Sarcoptes scabiei infestation. Thirty-seven of those 95 came in after a previous treatment had failed. The other 29 rabbits were in-contact animals living with affected rabbits but not yet showing disease.

Every rabbit, affected or not, received one oral dose of fluralaner at 25 mg/kg. Rabbits were rechecked at weeks 1, 2, 3, and 12.

What they found

  • All 95 confirmed cases recovered completely, a 100% success rate (95% CI 96.2 to 100%).
  • That included all 37 rabbits that had failed earlier antiparasitic treatment.
  • None of the 29 in-contact rabbits developed mange during follow-up.
  • In 8 of 52 households (15.4%), families reported itchy skin lesions of their own that were consistent with scabies.

That last number is the one I would bring up with families on day one.

Strengths

For exotic companion mammal dermatology, 95 confirmed cases is a big series. It was prospective, the follow-up ran to 12 weeks rather than stopping when the crusts fell off, and it deliberately included the hard cases, rabbits that had already been treated and were still infested. Treating in-contact rabbits and following them is also closer to how we manage mites in real households.

Limitations

It's a case series, so there is no comparison group. We can say a single oral dose worked very well here, but not how it stacks up head to head against selamectin, ivermectin, or a topical isoxazoline. The in-contact rabbits were all treated, so we can't tell how many would have developed mange without it. The human lesions were reported by families, not diagnosed by a physician.

Fluralaner is also off-label in rabbits, and there are still no published pharmacokinetic data for the species. Document the discussion and the family's consent in the record.

How I'd use this

I've already moved to fluralaner for most rabbit mite cases, and I wrote about why selamectin so often fails for rabbit mites earlier this fall. This paper adds Sarcoptes to the list with real numbers behind it, and it supports a single dose rather than a long series of rechecks for retreatment.

What I usually do with a crusted, itchy rabbit:

  1. Confirm the mite. Deep scrapes in rabbits need a dull blade or a scraping spatula, because rabbit skin is thin and tears easily. Tape cytology is still worth doing for Cheyletiella and fur mites.
  2. Look for the reason the rabbit couldn't groom. Obesity, dental disease, spondylosis, and osteoarthritis all show up as parasites that won't go away. Treat the mite and you will be back in a month if the rabbit still can't reach its back.
  3. Treat every rabbit in the household, not just the one with crusts.
  4. Treat the discomfort. A heavily crusted rabbit is painful and often not eating well. Analgesia and nutritional support are part of the plan, not extras.
  5. Talk about the people. Sarcoptes is zoonotic. If anyone in the home has itchy skin lesions, they should see their own physician.

And please don't bathe the rabbit. The hypothermia risk and the difficulty of drying the coat outweigh any benefit.

Questions for rounds

  1. Would you treat in-contact rabbits that look normal, and how would you explain that cost to a family?
  2. What would you want to see in a controlled trial before calling fluralaner first-line for rabbit Sarcoptes?
  3. How do you handle zoonotic counseling without stepping into human medical advice?
