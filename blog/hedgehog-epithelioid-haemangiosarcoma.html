Not Every Hedgehog Mammary-Region Mass Is Mammary Carcinoma

October 24, 2026

When an African pygmy hedgehog presents with a subcutaneous mass over the mammary region, mammary carcinoma sits near the top of most differentials. A 2026 Veterinary Record Case Reports paper is a useful reminder that vascular tumors belong on that list too, and that aggressive histology does not always predict a short clinical course.

The case

Rassin described a 4-year-old intact female African pygmy hedgehog (Atelerix albiventris) with a chronic subcutaneous mass in the mammary region.

Preoperative ultrasound showed a hypoechoic nodule at the mass site, mild uterine thickening, and diffuse hepatic hyperechogenicity. Bloodwork showed mild hypercalcaemia, eosinophilia, and monocytosis.

The hedgehog underwent ovariohysterectomy and mass excision. Histopathology was most consistent with cutaneous epithelioid haemangiosarcoma: infiltrative growth, high mitotic activity, and narrow margins.

Despite that aggressive microscopic picture, there was no clinical or radiographic recurrence or metastasis during follow-up. The hedgehog survived about 21 months after surgery. No necropsy was performed, so occult disease at the end of life cannot be excluded.

What this means in practice

  • Location is not diagnosis. A mass over the mammary chain can be cutaneous haemangiosarcoma, mammary carcinoma, or something else. Fine-needle aspiration or biopsy before or at surgery helps set expectations with the family.
  • Stage before you cut when you can. Ultrasound here added uterine and hepatic findings that changed the overall surgical conversation. Thoracic imaging and a look at regional lymph nodes remain reasonable for any malignant-appearing mass in this species.
  • Discuss margins honestly. Narrow margins with an infiltrative, high-mitotic tumor usually mean a guarded local prognosis. This hedgehog did well anyway. That is encouraging, but it is one case. Families need both the histology and the uncertainty.
  • Concurrent OHE is often worth discussing in intact females. Mild uterine thickening was already present. Combining mass excision with ovariohysterectomy under one anesthetic avoided a second procedure and addressed reproductive risk, which is common in aging hedgehogs.
  • Follow-up matters. Twenty-one months without clinical or radiographic recurrence is a meaningful outcome. Plan rechecks and imaging based on the histology and the family's goals, even when early recovery looks smooth.

Strengths and limitations

This is a single case report with useful imaging, bloodwork, surgical, and long-term clinical follow-up. The author is appropriately cautious: the diagnosis is "most consistent with" cutaneous epithelioid haemangiosarcoma, and lack of necropsy leaves the true disease-free status unknown.

We cannot generalize survival from one hedgehog. Narrow margins and high mitotic activity usually warrant a guarded outlook; this patient's course should not soften that conversation for the next case. Mild hypercalcaemia and hepatic hyperechogenicity were noted but not fully explained in the abstract-level summary, so do not over-interpret those findings from this write-up alone.

How I would use this

In my practice, any mammary-region mass in a hedgehog gets a careful exam, imaging when the family can pursue it, and a surgical plan that assumes malignancy until proven otherwise. I talk through staging, margin goals, and whether concurrent OHE makes sense for an intact female before we induce.

After excision, I submit the mass and any reproductive tissue for histopathology. If the report comes back as a vascular sarcoma with narrow margins, I still offer structured rechecks and thoracic imaging. This case shows that a long, good-quality survival is possible, and I share that hope without promising it.

Discussion questions

  1. Would you biopsy first or go straight to wide excision for a chronic mammary-region mass in an otherwise stable hedgehog?
  2. How do you counsel families when histology is aggressive but clinical follow-up looks clean?
  3. In your hands, when does concurrent OHE tip from "optional" to "strongly recommended" at the time of mass removal?
