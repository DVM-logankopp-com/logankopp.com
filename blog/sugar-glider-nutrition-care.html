Sugar Glider Nutrition and Care: Keeping Your Glider Healthy

October 2026

Sugar gliders are small, social marsupials with big personalities. Diet is one of the most important parts of their care. It is also where many health problems start. Nutrition problems and injuries are among the most common reasons sugar gliders need a vet. Here's what owners should know.

A little about sugar gliders

  • They're marsupials, more closely related to kangaroos than to squirrels. Females carry their young in a pouch.
  • They're nocturnal, most active in the evening and at night.
  • They're very social. Gliders do best in pairs or small groups. A lonely glider can become stressed, which can lead to over-grooming or even self-injury.
  • They need room to climb and glide, in a tall, secure cage with branches, a sleeping pouch and a solid exercise wheel.

What do sugar gliders eat?

In the wild, sugar gliders eat a mix of tree sap and gum, nectar, pollen, insects and other small prey. That's hard to copy at home, so pet gliders need a carefully planned diet. A balanced diet generally includes:

  • Protein sources, such as insects (crickets or mealworms) and cooked egg
  • A pelleted or prepared diet formulated for sugar gliders, often nectar-based
  • Vegetables and leafy greens
  • Fruit in moderation
  • Vitamin and mineral supplements, especially calcium, as directed by your veterinarian

Because many "glider diets" online vary widely in quality, it's a good idea to review your glider's diet with your veterinarian rather than relying on pet store or internet recipes alone.

The most common nutrition problems

Calcium deficiency and weak bones

Diets heavy in fruit and insects but low in calcium can lead to metabolic bone disease. Bones become weak and fragile, and the body struggles to keep calcium levels stable. Signs include:

  • Weakness or paralysis of the back legs
  • Trembling or seizures
  • Trouble climbing or gliding
  • Fractures from minor falls

This is one of the most important reasons to get the diet right, and to use supplements properly.

Obesity

Too much fruit, nectar and sugary treats, combined with too little exercise, can lead to obesity. Overweight gliders are at risk of liver problems and reduced activity. A larger cage, a safe wheel and supervised play time all help.

Too little protein

A diet with too little protein can lead to weight loss, a poor coat and a weaker immune system.

Dental disease

Sugary, soft foods can contribute to tartar build-up and dental infections.

Other common health concerns

  • Injuries, from falls, ceiling fans, other pets, or getting caught in cage accessories
  • Self-injury linked to stress or loneliness
  • Parasites and infections, which can cause diarrhea or weight loss
  • Lumps, including growths around the pouch in older females

Simple tips for a healthy glider

  1. Keep gliders in pairs or groups.
  2. Feed a balanced diet and keep fruit and treats limited.
  3. Use calcium and vitamin supplements as your vet recommends.
  4. Offer fresh water daily, and check bottles for clogs.
  5. Weigh your glider regularly on a gram scale.
  6. Glider-proof play areas: close toilets, turn off fans and watch for gaps.
  7. Schedule a wellness exam every year.

When to call your vet

Contact your veterinarian promptly if your sugar glider:

  • Is weak, dragging the back legs or can't climb
  • Isn't eating, or is losing weight
  • Has diarrhea or is straining to urinate or defecate
  • Has eye or nose discharge, or is sneezing a lot
  • Is over-grooming, has hair loss or wounds
  • Has a lump, including on or near the pouch
  • Is circling, trembling or having seizures

A glider that is suddenly weak should be seen right away.
