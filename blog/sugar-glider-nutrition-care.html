Sugar Glider Nutrition and Care: Keeping Your Glider Healthy

October 2026

Sugar gliders are tiny, social marsupials with big personalities, and anyone who has watched one sail across a room knows how special they are. Diet is one of the most important parts of their care, and it's also where many of their health problems begin. Nutrition problems and injuries are among the most common reasons sugar gliders need a vet. The encouraging part is that both are often preventable.

Getting to know your glider

Sugar gliders are marsupials, and females carry their young in a pouch. They're nocturnal, most active in the evening and at night. They're also very social and do best in pairs or small groups. A lonely glider can become stressed, which can lead to over-grooming or even self-injury.

Housing and enrichment are health care

Many common problems in exotic pets aren't random. They're the predictable result of an environment that doesn't match the animal's biology. For gliders, that means height, climbing, a companion and things to do. A tall, secure cage with branches at different levels, a sleeping pouch, a solid exercise wheel, foraging toys and hidden food all encourage natural behavior and keep a glider active. A bored, under-exercised glider is more likely to become overweight or stressed.

What sugar gliders eat

In the wild, sugar gliders eat a mix of tree sap and gum, nectar, pollen, insects and other small prey. That's hard to copy at home, so pet gliders need a carefully planned diet. A balanced plan generally combines protein sources such as insects and cooked egg, a prepared diet formulated for sugar gliders, vegetables and leafy greens, fruit in moderation, and vitamin and mineral supplements, especially calcium, as your veterinarian directs.

Glider diets found online vary widely in quality, so review your glider's diet with your veterinarian rather than relying on pet store or internet recipes alone. When a diet doesn't fit the species, disease isn't bad luck. It's expected.

The nutrition problems I see most

The most important one is calcium deficiency. Diets heavy in fruit and insects but low in calcium can lead to metabolic bone disease, in which bones become weak and fragile and the body struggles to keep calcium levels stable. The classic sign is weakness or paralysis of the back legs. Gliders may also tremble, have seizures, struggle to climb or glide, or break bones in minor falls. This is the biggest reason to get the diet right and use supplements properly.

Obesity is the other common problem. Too much fruit, nectar and sugary treats, combined with too little exercise, can lead to weight gain and liver problems. A larger cage, a safe wheel and supervised play time help. Too little protein causes its own trouble, including weight loss, a poor coat and weaker immunity, and sugary, soft foods can contribute to tartar and dental infections.

Other health concerns

Injuries are common, from falls, ceiling fans, other pets or getting caught in cage accessories. Stress and loneliness can lead to self-injury. Parasites and infections can cause diarrhea or weight loss, and older females can develop lumps around the pouch.

Simple habits for a healthy glider

Keep gliders in pairs or groups, feed a balanced diet with limited fruit and treats, and use calcium and vitamin supplements as your vet recommends. Offer fresh water daily and check bottles for clogs. Weigh your glider regularly on a gram scale. Glider-proof play areas by closing toilets, turning off ceiling fans and blocking gaps. And schedule a wellness exam every year.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your glider is suddenly weak, dragging the back legs or unable to climb, is trembling or having seizures, or isn't eating.

Book a prompt visit for weight loss, diarrhea or straining, eye or nose discharge, sneezing, over-grooming, hair loss, wounds, or a lump, including on or near the pouch.
