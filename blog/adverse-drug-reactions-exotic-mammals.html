Adverse Drug Reactions in Rabbits, Ferrets, and Rodents: Notes From the New VCNA Toxicology Issue

November 23, 2026

The January 2027 toxicology issue of Veterinary Clinics of North America: Exotic Animal Practice has a review of adverse drug reactions across exotic species by Gardiner and Stern. A lot of it covers birds, reptiles, and amphibians. The mammal sections are worth a careful read, because the drugs involved are the ones most of us reach for every week.

A companion article in the same issue, Willens and Wismer's summary of ASPCA Poison Control calls from 2021 to 2025, gives some context. Of 6206 calls about pet mammals other than dogs and cats, antibiotics accounted for 201, NSAIDs for 199, and CNS medications for 169. Rodents and lagomorphs made up most of the antibiotic calls, and lagomorphs made up most of the NSAID and CNS calls.

The authors draw a distinction I find useful. An adverse drug reaction is an unintended effect at a therapeutic dose. Toxicosis usually follows a supratherapeutic dose, although it can occur at therapeutic doses too. In exotic practice, both happen with drugs that are perfectly reasonable choices in a dog.

Antibiotics

Oral beta-lactams, macrolides, and lincosamides. None of this is new, but it bears repeating. Oral penicillins and cephalosporins disrupt the cecal flora of hindgut fermenters and can cause severe dysbiosis. Clostridium difficile toxin has been detected in guinea pigs with penicillin-associated colitis, and oral macrolides and lincosamides have been associated with clostridial enterotoxemia and enterocolitis in rabbits, guinea pigs, and hamsters. Parenteral beta-lactams don't carry the same GI risk, which is why injectable penicillin G procaine remains common for rabbit dental infections. The authors' care point is to avoid oral penicillins, lincosamides, and macrolides in rabbits and most hystricomorphs, with azithromycin as the exception. The azithromycin data cited come from an experimental otitis model in chinchillas, where oral azithromycin up to 120 mg/kg/day for up to 5 days had 12% mortality, attributed mainly to anesthetic or gavage complications rather than GI disease.

Fluoroquinolones. Injectable enrofloxacin is highly alkaline (pH up to 11.5) and can cause muscle necrosis, sterile abscesses, and skin lesions at the injection site. Given orally, its bitter taste may reduce food and water intake, and fecal microbiota changes have been documented in chinchillas and mice. In the poison control data, fluoroquinolones were the most frequently reported antibiotic class (52 of 201 calls), which Willens and Wismer attribute to how often we use them.

Aminoglycosides. Ferrets are described as very sensitive to gentamicin-induced renal tubular necrosis, even at a standard 4 to 8 mg/kg/day. Amikacin is generally less nephrotoxic, but ototoxicity is a concern. In 14 rabbits given amikacin IM for 2 weeks, cochlear activity fell in both a 15 mg/kg once-daily group and a 7.5 mg/kg twice-daily group, and the reduction was broader and greater with twice-daily dosing despite the same cumulative dose. The care point is to monitor renal values and clinical signs closely, especially in ferrets.

Metronidazole. Rabbits given 40 mg/kg IP twice daily for 20 days developed neuronal degeneration centrally and in the sciatic nerve. The route and dose don't match clinical use, but it's worth remembering when a rabbit on a long course develops neurologic signs.

Sedatives and injectable anesthetics

  • Benzodiazepines. In 10 ferrets sedated with butorphanol combined with xylazine, diazepam, or acepromazine, the butorphanol-diazepam combination produced the greatest drop in respiratory rate, and one ferret had paradoxical excitement. Flumazenil can reverse benzodiazepine effects.
  • Ketamine. The authors make the point that IM ketamine is not as benign as many of us assume. It has been associated with myonecrosis, sciatic nerve axonal degeneration, and self-mutilation in rabbits, rats, and guinea pigs. Older reports also describe renal tubular necrosis in New Zealand White rabbits given tiletamine or tiletamine-zolazepam at 32 mg/kg, while zolazepam alone caused no lesions.
  • Alfaxalone. In rabbits, the most frequently reported problems are respiratory depression and hypoxemia, particularly with IV use or IM doses of 6 to 8 mg/kg or higher.
  • Propofol. In an older study of New Zealand White rabbits maintained on propofol alone at a mean 0.876 mg/kg/min for 8 hours, hypotension and hypoxemia were significant and 4 of 10 died. Propofol infusion syndrome has been modeled in rabbits at high infusion rates.
  • Alpha-2 agonists. Ketamine 35 mg/kg with xylazine 5 mg/kg in healthy rabbits reduced heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, fractional shortening, and cardiac output.

Local anesthetic systemic toxicity

The rabbit data here come from a clinical case series. Six rabbits were accidentally given lidocaine CRIs above 100 μg/kg/min or IV boluses above 2 mg/kg and developed tremors, seizures, arrhythmias, and collapse. Five received IV lipid emulsion at 12 to 15 mL/kg, and clinical signs resolved in 4 of the 6 rabbits. Lidocaine CRIs are widely used for analgesia and to help prevent ileus, so checking the concentration and pump rate deserves its own step. I've written separately about local and regional anesthesia in companion mammals.

Analgesics

Acetaminophen. Ferrets have a functional UGT1A6 enzyme, but its activity is deficient. The authors recommend treating any acetaminophen exposure in a ferret as potentially serious.

NSAIDs. Neurologic signs were reported in 93.1% of ferret ibuprofen toxicosis cases, and a single 200 mg tablet can be lethal. Naloxone and lipid emulsion have been suggested for the neurologic signs. On the dispensing side, the poison control data are humbling. Meloxicam was the most frequently reported NSAID (117 of 199 calls), lagomorphs were involved in most NSAID calls, and many of the intoxications involved decimal errors such as 0.2 mL versus 0.02 mL. That's an argument for dispensing concentrations and syringe sizes that make a tenfold error hard to draw up.

Parasiticides and THC

Fipronil. Despite documented therapeutic use, the manufacturer discourages fipronil in rabbits because of severe adverse events. Reported signs after dermal application include anorexia, adipsia, lethargy, hypothermia, hypersalivation, tremors, seizures, ileus, and death, with a reported mortality of 27%. Treatment is bathing, fluid and nutritional support, and antiepileptics. The care point is simply to avoid it.

Ivermectin gets most of its attention in chelonians in this review, but the decontamination article in the same issue lists published lipid emulsion protocols for ivermectin toxicosis in rabbits and guinea pigs, which I cover in my post on triage and decontamination.

THC. Signs in exotic animals include depression, hypothermia, bradycardia, respiratory depression, seizure-like activity, coma, and death. Treatment is mainly supportive nursing care, and lipid emulsion has been used successfully for severe toxicosis in ferrets.

What I take from it

Most of this review describes drugs that are appropriate in the right species, route, and dose. The problems come from extrapolating a dog regimen, choosing the oral route in a hindgut fermenter, or drawing up the wrong volume. The authors' closing advice is to search the literature before using an unfamiliar drug in a given species, and to treat toxicosis symptomatically and supportively. For lipophilic drugs, they suggest considering lipid emulsion early in severe cases.

The limits of the evidence are worth keeping in mind. Much of the mammal data come from laboratory models, small groups of healthy animals, or single case reports, often using routes such as IP dosing that we don't use clinically.
