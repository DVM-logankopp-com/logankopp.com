Rabbit Spay and Neuter: Protecting Your Bunny's Health

October 2026

Spaying or neutering your rabbit is one of the most important health decisions you'll make for them. It isn't only about preventing litters. For female rabbits in particular, spaying is one of the best ways to protect against serious reproductive diseases, including cancer, later in life.

Why spaying matters for female rabbits

Unspayed female rabbits (does) are at high risk of developing disease of the uterus as they get older. The most serious is uterine adenocarcinoma, a cancer of the uterus that is well recognized as common in older, intact does. Other problems include uterine infections, abnormal thickening of the uterine lining, and blood in the urine that actually comes from the uterus.

These conditions often develop quietly. By the time a rabbit shows signs, the disease may already be advanced, and uterine cancer can spread to other organs such as the lungs.

Spaying removes the uterus and ovaries, which removes the risk of uterine cancer and many other reproductive problems.

Mammary (breast) tumors in rabbits

Female rabbits can also develop mammary gland problems, including cysts and tumors, and these are often linked to hormonal changes in the uterus. Some mammary tumors in rabbits are malignant.

Spaying helps by removing the hormonal influences behind many of these changes. If your doe already has a mammary lump, your veterinarian may recommend removing the lump, spaying her at the same time, and checking her chest with imaging to look for spread.

Signs to watch for include:

  • Lumps or swelling along the belly near the nipples
  • Enlarged, firm or discolored nipples
  • Clear or bloody discharge from a nipple

Benefits of neutering male rabbits

Neutering males (bucks) helps with:

  • Behavior. Less spraying, mounting, territorial aggression and chewing driven by hormones
  • Litter habits. Neutered rabbits are usually easier to litter train
  • Bonding. Neutered rabbits generally bond more easily with a companion rabbit
  • Testicular disease. Neutering removes the risk of testicular tumors and infection

Rabbits are social animals and usually happiest with a bonded partner. Spaying and neutering makes that companionship possible without surprise litters.

When should my rabbit be spayed or neutered?

The best timing depends on your rabbit's sex, breed, size and health. Your veterinarian will recommend an age for your individual rabbit. Older rabbits can still benefit, but they should have a thorough exam and pre-surgical testing first.

What to expect with surgery

A rabbit-experienced team will:

  • Examine your rabbit and discuss pre-surgical bloodwork
  • Keep your rabbit's gut moving by not fasting them the way dogs and cats are fasted
  • Use careful anesthetic monitoring and keep your rabbit warm
  • Provide pain relief before, during and after surgery
  • Encourage eating soon after waking up

Most rabbits go home the same day. At home, offer hay and greens right away, keep them in a clean, quiet space, and watch their appetite and droppings closely.

When to call your vet

Call your veterinarian if your rabbit:

  • Has blood in the urine or bloody vaginal discharge (in an unspayed doe)
  • Has a lump on the belly or near a nipple
  • Stops eating or produces fewer droppings, especially after surgery
  • Has redness, swelling or discharge at an incision
  • Seems painful, hunched or is grinding their teeth
