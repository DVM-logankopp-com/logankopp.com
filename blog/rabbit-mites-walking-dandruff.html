Rabbit Mites: Walking Dandruff, Ear Crusts and What to Do

October 20, 2026

If your rabbit's coat looks dusty even after grooming, or one ear fills with thick crusts that smell and bother them, mites are high on the list. These parasites are common in pet rabbits, including here in Middle Tennessee, and they are very treatable once we identify which mite is involved and treat both the rabbit and the living space.

Walking dandruff (Cheyletiella)

Cheyletiella parasitovorax is often called walking dandruff because the scale on the fur can look like it is moving when you watch closely. Families usually notice broken hairs, patchy thin spots, and white flakes along the back or neck. Some rabbits itch a lot. Others barely scratch.

This mite lives on the surface of the skin and feeds on keratin. Adult females and eggs can survive in the environment for up to about ten days, which is why a single treatment that leaves a gap often fails. People in the household can occasionally get itchy spots too, so mention that to your veterinarian if anyone at home has unexplained itching.

Ear mites (Psoroptes)

Psoroptes cuniculi produces the classic thick, layered crusts inside the ear. The ear may look packed with material, and your rabbit may shake their head, hold one ear differently, or seem painful when the ear is touched. Less often, similar crusting shows up on the feet or around the bottom.

An important safety note: do not pull the crusts out yourself. That hurts and can cause bleeding. With a veterinarian-prescribed parasite treatment, the crusts loosen and fall away as the mites die.

Fur mites, fleas and look-alikes

Leporacarus gibbus, the rabbit fur mite, is sometimes found on rabbits that seem fine, and sometimes on rabbits that itch. Because it can affect people as well, identification and treatment are still worthwhile.

Fleas can jump onto rabbits when the home or yard is heavily infested, especially if dogs or cats share the space. Treating every pet in the household with a product safe for that species is part of getting ahead of the problem.

Not every flaky or itchy rabbit has mites. Ringworm, allergies, barbering from a bonded partner, and sore or dirty skin from other illnesses can look similar. That is why a proper exam matters before you reach for an over-the-counter product.

How we figure it out

At the visit I start with a full physical exam, then use clear tape on the coat, a careful skin scrape with a dull blade or spatula (rabbit skin is thin), or a look at ear debris under the microscope. The hooked mouthparts of Cheyletiella are distinctive. Ear mites are often obvious on exam, and the microscope confirms them.

Why the whole environment matters

Killing mites on the rabbit is only half the job. Because some mites and fleas survive off the host, bedding, fleece covers, toys, and shared living areas need cleaning on a schedule your veterinarian recommends. If another rabbit, guinea pig, or household pet shares space or grooming tools, ask whether they need treatment too.

Skipping environmental cleanup is one of the most common reasons mites seem to "come back" a week or two later. This is biology, not a failing on your part.

Soft flooring and clean, dry living space

Soft, grippy mats covered with fleece protect the heels, encourage movement, and wash easily when you are dealing with scale or flea dirt. Keep litter areas clean and dry. Do not bathe a rabbit to treat mites unless your veterinarian walks you through it. Rabbits chill easily and are hard to dry thoroughly.

When to call your vet

Call promptly if your rabbit stops eating, produces fewer or no droppings, seems very painful around the ears or skin, develops swelling or discharge, or if ear crusts are severe.

Book a visit soon if you notice new flaking, broken hair, patchy thin fur, mild itching, or early ear debris. Catching mites early is kinder and usually simpler.

Avoid guessing with products meant for dogs or cats. Some are unsafe in rabbits. A veterinarian-prescribed parasite treatment matched to the mite you actually have is the reliable path.
