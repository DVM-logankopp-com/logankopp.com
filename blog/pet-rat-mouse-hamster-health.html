Keeping Pet Rats, Mice, Hamsters and Gerbils Healthy

October 2026

Small rodents are wonderful first pets and loyal companions. Rats in particular are clever and affectionate. Because these pets are small and short-lived, health problems can move quickly. A little knowledge goes a long way toward catching problems early.

Know your species

  • Rats are social and do best with at least one same-sex companion. They're intelligent and enjoy daily interaction and enrichment.
  • Mice are also social (females especially) and need secure, escape-proof housing.
  • Hamsters, including the common Syrian hamster, are generally solitary and should live alone. They're mostly active at night and may nip if woken suddenly.
  • Gerbils are social, active burrowers that enjoy deep bedding for digging.

All of these rodents have front teeth that grow continuously, so they need safe things to chew.

Housing basics

  • A roomy, well-ventilated enclosure that is easy to clean
  • Solid flooring, or at least solid areas to rest on, to protect feet
  • Dust-free paper bedding. Avoid cedar and pine shavings, which can irritate the airways.
  • Hiding spots, nesting material and chew toys
  • A solid exercise wheel of the right size for the species
  • A cool, quiet spot away from direct sunlight and drafts. Most small rodents don't handle heat well.

Clean the enclosure regularly. Ammonia from urine builds up quickly and is hard on the lungs.

Common health problems

Respiratory disease (especially in rats)

Respiratory infections are among the most common problems in pet rats. They're often linked to bacteria that many rats carry, and made worse by ammonia, dusty bedding and stress. Watch for:

  • Sneezing, sniffling or noisy breathing
  • Red-brown discharge around the eyes or nose (this is a pigment rats produce, not blood)
  • Breathing with effort, or a hunched posture
  • Weight loss and reduced activity

Respiratory disease is easier to manage when treated early.

Lumps and tumors

Rats, especially females, are prone to mammary tumors, which are usually benign but can grow large. Mice and hamsters can also develop lumps. Any new lump deserves a check, because early removal is usually simpler. Spaying young female rats greatly reduces the risk of mammary tumors.

Dental problems

Overgrown or misaligned front teeth can make eating difficult. Signs include drooling, dropping food, weight loss and teeth that look too long or crooked.

Skin problems

Mites and lice can cause itching, scabs and hair loss. Over-grooming from stress or cage mates can look similar.

Digestive problems

Hamsters in particular can develop stress-related digestive upsets. Diarrhea or a wet, dirty tail area in a hamster needs prompt attention.

Simple ways to keep them healthy

  • Feed a quality lab block or pellet made for the species, plus small amounts of fresh vegetables
  • Limit sugary treats and seed mixes
  • Keep housing clean, dry and well ventilated
  • Handle your pets gently every day and feel for lumps
  • Weigh them regularly on a gram scale
  • Schedule a yearly wellness exam

A note on hygiene

Rodents can carry germs that affect people, including the bacteria that cause rat-bite fever. Wash your hands after handling pets or cleaning cages, clean any bite or scratch well, and see your doctor if you feel unwell after a bite.

When to call your vet

Call your veterinarian if your rodent:

  • Is breathing hard, wheezing or making clicking sounds
  • Has stopped eating or is losing weight
  • Has a new or growing lump
  • Has diarrhea or a wet tail area
  • Has a head tilt, is circling or seems weak
  • Is itching, losing fur or has scabs

Small animals can go downhill quickly. If yours hasn't eaten since yesterday, call rather than waiting for a routine appointment.
