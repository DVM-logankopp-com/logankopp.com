Gut Motility Drugs for Rabbits: Why Metoclopramide and Cisapride Aren't the Answer to GI Stasis

November 23, 2026

When a rabbit stops eating and stops making droppings, families are often told the gut has "slowed down," and the rabbit may go home with a medicine meant to speed it up. The two most common are metoclopramide and cisapride. Veterinarians call these prokinetics.

I don't consider these drugs standard care for rabbits, and in my experience most veterinarians who specialize in exotic pets don't use them routinely. This article explains why, what the research shows, and what actually helps a rabbit whose gut has slowed. If you are reading this because your rabbit has stopped eating right now, please skip to When to call your vet and call.

How a rabbit's gut works

Rabbits are built to eat grass and hay all day. Their stomach and small intestine work much like ours, but most of the work happens further down, in a large fermentation chamber called the cecum and in the colon.

The gut sorts what the rabbit eats by size. Long, coarse fiber moves through and comes out as the round, dry droppings you see in the litter box. Smaller particles go to the cecum, where helpful bacteria break them down. The cecum makes soft, nutrient-rich droppings called cecotropes, which rabbits eat straight from their bottom.

A part of the gut called the fusus coli works like a pacemaker for the whole gut, and what keeps it working is long fiber. That's why hay matters so much. Long fiber does not provide much nutrition, but it is what keeps the gut moving.

Rabbits also can't vomit, and they make a lot of saliva and gut fluid even when they're not eating. When the gut slows, a rabbit can become dehydrated quickly, and the gut contents can dry out and get harder to move.

GI stasis is a sign, not a diagnosis

"GI stasis" describes what is happening: the gut has slowed or stopped. It doesn't explain why. Pain, dental disease, dehydration, stress, a poor diet, or a blockage in the intestines can all cause it. I use the term rabbit gastrointestinal syndrome because it reminds us to look for the cause. My post on GI stasis warning signs goes through those causes.

What the research shows about these drugs

These drugs were first developed for people and work mostly on the upper gut, in species that can vomit. Rabbits are very different. For years, the rabbit doses in reference books were based on other reference books or personal experience, not on studies in rabbits. Researchers have now tested them directly.

  • Single doses in healthy rabbits. A 2025 study gave 10 healthy rabbits a single dose of metoclopramide, cisapride, two other gut drugs, or a placebo. None of the drugs changed how much the rabbits ate or how many droppings they made over the next three days (Di Girolamo and colleagues).
  • High doses in rabbits that had stopped eating. A 2026 study made healthy young rabbits eat less using a pain medication known to slow the gut, then gave metoclopramide at ten times the usual dose, twice a day for two days. It didn't help them eat more or produce more droppings, and they didn't recover any faster than rabbits given a placebo (Stratton and colleagues).
  • Cisapride with a pain medication. A 2021 study found that cisapride did not change how fast food moved through the gut, how much rabbits ate, or how many droppings they made, whether or not they were also on a pain medication (Feldman and colleagues).

These studies were done in laboratory rabbits, not pet rabbits that got sick at home, so they don't answer every question. But none of them found a benefit.

Why these drugs can cause problems

Even if a medicine doesn't help, you might think it can't hurt. I'm not sure that's true here, for two reasons.

Blockages. Sometimes a rabbit that has stopped eating has something stuck in the intestines, such as a wad of hair. Early on, a blockage can look a lot like ordinary stasis. Pushing the gut to move harder against a blockage is not what that rabbit needs. Sometimes doing something is worse than doing nothing.

A false sense of security. When a rabbit goes home with a "gut medicine," it's natural to feel the problem is being treated. That can make it easier to wait when the rabbit really needs the cause found, pain treated, fluids, and feeding support.

Cisapride was also taken off the human market in the United States in 2000 because of serious heart rhythm problems in people. We don't have good safety data for these drugs in sick rabbits treated for days at a time.

What actually helps

Treatment for a rabbit whose gut has slowed focuses on the cause and on support:

  • Finding the cause, with an exam and often x-rays. A painful, bloated, or very quiet rabbit needs urgent care.
  • Pain relief. Most rabbits won't eat until their pain is controlled.
  • Fluids, because rabbits with a slow gut become dehydrated.
  • Assisted feeding with a recovery food that has fiber, given by syringe, when your veterinarian recommends it. Getting fiber moving is likely to do far more than a prokinetic.
  • Hay, and plenty of it, once your rabbit is eating again, along with fresh water.

Please don't give your rabbit an enema at home. Rabbits with stasis usually aren't passing droppings because they aren't eating, and an enema can injure the colon.

Is there ever a place for these drugs?

Possibly, in a small number of cases. One idea that hasn't been tested is giving metoclopramide as a continuous drip through an IV in the hospital; no study has yet shown whether that works in rabbits. In a hospitalized rabbit where a blockage has been ruled out and everything else is already being done, I can see a prokinetic as a last-ditch addition to a full treatment plan. That is very different from sending a rabbit home on it as the main treatment.

If your rabbit is already on one of these medicines, don't stop or change it without talking to your veterinarian. Ask what it's for and what else is part of the plan.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your rabbit:

  • Has not eaten or has not passed droppings for several hours
  • Has a swollen, tight, or bloated belly
  • Is hunched, pressing into a corner, or grinding their teeth
  • Is very quiet, weak, or not responding normally
  • Is already on medication for stasis and is not improving or is getting worse

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Smaller, fewer, or misshapen droppings for more than a day while your rabbit is otherwise eating
  • Eating less hay than usual, or picking only at treats
  • Repeated bouts of stasis, which often point to an underlying cause
