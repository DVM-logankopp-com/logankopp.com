CT Scans for Rabbits and Other Exotic Pets: What Owners Should Know

October 2026

Sometimes a physical exam and X-rays don't tell the whole story. Rabbits, guinea pigs, chinchillas and ferrets are small, and important structures like tooth roots, inner ears and chest masses can be hard to see clearly on standard X-rays. That's where computed tomography, or CT, can help.

What is a CT scan?

A CT scan uses X-rays and a computer to build detailed, cross-sectional images of the body. Instead of one flat picture, your veterinarian can look at bones, teeth, soft tissue and organs slice by slice, and even in 3D. For tiny patients with complicated anatomy, that extra detail can change the treatment plan.

When might my pet need a CT?

Dr. Kopp may recommend a CT scan for problems such as:

  • Head tilt in rabbits. CT helps look for middle or inner ear infection, tumors or other structural causes.
  • Dental disease. Rabbits, guinea pigs and chinchillas can develop tooth root problems and abscesses that are hard to judge on standard X-rays.
  • Tumors and masses. CT can show the size and location of a mass and whether it has spread, which helps with surgical planning.
  • Breathing problems. CT can evaluate the nose, airways and lungs.
  • Abdominal problems. It can help look for obstructions, organ changes or growths.
  • Injuries and skeletal problems. CT shows fractures, joint and spinal problems in detail. Jaw injuries in rabbits, for example, are often more extensive than they first appear.

How CT works for exotic patients in Nashville

CT scans for Dr. Kopp's exotic patients are performed at Nashville Veterinary Specialists (NVS) through a working relationship with Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital. Dr. Kopp oversees the process for exotic companion mammals, including sedation and monitoring.

The basic steps are:

  1. Preparation. Most pets are sedated so they stay comfortable and still, which gives clear images. Your vet will explain what's best for your pet.
  2. Imaging. The scan itself usually takes just a few minutes, depending on the area being imaged.
  3. Interpretation. The images are reviewed by a board-certified radiologist, and Dr. Kopp uses the results to build a treatment plan with you.

Is sedation safe?

Any sedation carries some risk, but careful planning makes it much safer. That planning includes a pre-procedure exam, keeping your pet warm, monitoring vital signs and getting them home and eating as quickly as possible. Ask your vet about your pet's specific risks, especially for older pets or those with other health problems.

How to prepare

  • Ask whether your pet should eat before the appointment. Rabbits and guinea pigs usually should not be fasted.
  • Bring your pet's usual hay and food for afterward.
  • Bring a list of current medications.
  • Expect your pet to be a little sleepy for the rest of the day. Offer food and water, and keep them warm and quiet.

When to call your vet

Contact your veterinarian promptly if your exotic pet has:

  • A head tilt, circling, rolling or loss of balance
  • Swelling on the face or jaw, or a discharging lump
  • Drooling, dropping food or a change in appetite
  • Noisy breathing, discharge from the nose or breathing with effort
  • Weight loss without a clear reason

These signs don't always mean your pet needs a CT, but they do mean an exam is needed soon.
