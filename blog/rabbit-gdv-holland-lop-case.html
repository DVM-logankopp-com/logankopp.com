Gastric Dilatation and Volvulus in a Holland Lop Rabbit

November 2, 2026

GDV is a dog disease, mostly in large and giant breeds. It is not the first thing most of us think of when a 1.8 kg rabbit comes in painful and off her food.

This rabbit made me think of it, eventually. When I published her case in 2025, it was the third case of GDV in a rabbit in the peer-reviewed literature and the first in a Holland Lop. Liver lobe torsion and GDV both sit on the acute abdomen list in rabbits, and this case shows how easily one can pass for the other.

The paper

Kopp L. Gastric dilatation and volvulus in a 5-year-old intact female Holland lop rabbit. Can Vet J. 2025 Nov;66(11):1180-1183. PMID: 41142972. PMCID: PMC12548677

Case presentation

A 5-year-old intact female Holland Lop, 1.8 kg, was suddenly hyporexic and lethargic. She had no prior medical or surgical history and was current on her RHDV2 vaccine.

She scored 15 of 24 on the CANCRS composite pain scale, which indicates severe pain. Temperature was 37.8°C, heart rate 288 beats/min, and respiratory rate 78 breaths/min. The cranial abdomen was painful on palpation. The rest of the exam was within normal limits.

Stabilization and bloodwork

She was sedated with IM buprenorphine and midazolam, and a lidocaine and prilocaine topical cream went on over the lateral saphenous vein before the blood draw. A 24-gauge saphenous catheter followed, with IV lactated Ringer's solution, a lidocaine bolus, and a lidocaine CRI.

The biochemistry panel and CBC showed:

  • ALT 792 U/L (RI 20 to 104)
  • ALP 266 U/L (RI 24 to 128)
  • Amylase 771 U/L (RI 113 to 334)
  • Total bilirubin 0.5 mg/dL (RI 0.1 to 0.4)
  • Absolute heterophilia and absolute lymphopenia, with no left shift or toxic change on the smear
  • Hematocrit 31.68% (RI 33 to 45%)

A painful cranial abdomen and a big ALT made the liver the obvious suspect.

Imaging, and the decision that changed the plan

Three-view whole-body radiographs were concerning for right-sided liver lobe torsion. There was a soft-tissue opacity caudal to the last ribs and medial displacement of the stomach. Taken together with the bloodwork, that was a reasonable read.

Because liver lobe torsion was on the table, I did a point-of-care ultrasound. Blood flow was normal to every liver lobe. The gallbladder was markedly enlarged. And the spleen was in the left cranial abdomen.

That splenic position is the finding I'd want every rabbit vet to remember. It moved GDV to the top of the list and made surgery the next step. CT remains the gold standard for a definitive diagnosis when it's available. Without it, consistent bloodwork plus splenic displacement on ultrasound is enough to support a presumptive diagnosis and an exploratory laparotomy.

After an hour of stabilization her vital signs had normalized, and she went to surgery.

Surgery

She was preoxygenated, induced with IV ketamine and midazolam, intubated with a 2.5 mm uncuffed tube, and maintained on isoflurane with the lidocaine CRI continued throughout. She received crystalline free ceftiofur SC and was positioned in dorsal recumbency on a heating pad.

Through a ventral midline incision from xiphoid to pubis, with a Lone Star retractor for exposure, the findings were:

  • The spleen in the left cranial abdomen
  • The stomach rotated 180° clockwise
  • A markedly distended gallbladder and common bile duct

I derotated the stomach carefully, then performed an incisional gastropexy to prevent recurrence:

  1. A 2 cm incision in the avascular portion of the pyloric muscularis
  2. A matching 2 cm incision in the peritoneum and transversus abdominis of the right lateral body wall
  3. 2-0 polydioxanone to suture the dorsal edge of the muscularis to the transversus abdominis, then the same on the ventral edge

Within 10 minutes of derotation, the gallbladder and common bile duct had largely returned to normal size. I didn't do a full abdominal exploration, because adhesions after abdominal surgery are so common in rabbits. Closure was routine, with a ropivacaine splash block before the subcutaneous layer.

Recovery and outcome

She ate 15 mL/kg of a commercial herbivore syringe-feeding formula willingly on recovery. Fluids and the lidocaine CRI ran for about 12 hours, until the catheter failed. Meloxicam continued for 5 days.

Repeat bloodwork on day 5 showed every abnormality had resolved. She was doing well 92 days after surgery.

Why the liver values went up

All the reported rabbit cases had elevated ALT. ALP was elevated in all except the case described by Barak and colleagues. Total bilirubin was elevated in this rabbit and in the case reported by Vasyliev and colleagues.

My working explanation is mechanical. The rotated stomach compresses the common bile duct, causing cholestasis and secondary rises in ALT, ALP, and bilirubin. That fits the distended bile duct at surgery and how quickly it shrank after derotation. It is still speculation from a handful of cases.

A normal bilirubin doesn't rule GDV out. Bilirubin may be only intermittently elevated in rabbits, partly because analyzers have limited sensitivity and partly because rabbits convert only about 30% of biliverdin to bilirubin.

What we don't know yet

In dogs, GDV carries a mortality rate of about 15%, the incisional technique is the most commonly used gastropexy, and recurrence after gastropexy is under 5%. Large and giant breeds are overrepresented. None of that has been established in rabbits. The recurrence rate after gastropexy is unknown, and so are any breed or sex predispositions. The previous peer-reviewed cases were in New Zealand Whites, and two cases in lop-eared rabbits have been reported in conference proceedings. That is not enough rabbits to call a pattern.

Take-homes

Put GDV on the list for a rabbit with acute hyporexia, abdominal pain, elevated ALT, and variably elevated ALP and bilirubin.

Ultrasound before you commit to a diagnosis. Radiographs here suggested liver lobe torsion. A quick POCUS showed normal hepatic blood flow and a displaced spleen.

Treat splenic displacement into the left cranial abdomen as an urgent finding. It strongly supports GDV and calls for immediate exploration, or CT if you have it.

Stabilize, then go. One hour of analgesia, fluids, and a lidocaine CRI was enough to normalize her vitals before anesthesia.

Gastropexy after derotation. We have no rabbit recurrence data. The incisional technique is the one most used in dogs, and this rabbit was doing well three months later.

For discussion

  1. How often do you add a POCUS when rabbit radiographs suggest liver lobe torsion?
  2. Would you do a full abdominal exploration in a rabbit GDV, given the adhesion risk?
  3. How would your approach change if CT were available the same hour?
