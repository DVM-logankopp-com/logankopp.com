Spaying Your Rabbit: Uterine Cancer, Surgery Choices, and What to Watch For

November 24, 2026

If you share your home with a female rabbit who has not been spayed, the most important health reason to consider spaying her is uterine cancer. The medical name is uterine adenocarcinoma. It is the most common cancer in older female rabbits, it often grows without obvious signs, and it can spread to the lungs. Spaying removes the organs where it starts.

I've also written a broader post on spaying and neutering rabbits that covers behavior, litter training, and bonding.

How common is uterine cancer in rabbits?

Researchers have looked at this in several ways, and the numbers depend on which rabbits they studied.

  • In rabbits examined after death. A German pathology study found uterine tumors in about 13% of unspayed female rabbits that had a full necropsy, and the risk climbed steadily with age. Uterine cancer was very rare in rabbits younger than about two and a half years (Bertram and colleagues, 2021).
  • In rabbits spayed for any reason. A Finnish study examined the uterus under the microscope after spay surgery. In rabbits that were not thought to have any uterine problem, 44% had changes under the microscope, and 13% had tumors. Among all rabbits older than 3 years, 40% had a cancerous uterine tumor, compared with about 3% of rabbits younger than 3 (Mäkitaipale and colleagues, 2022).
  • In rabbits spayed in Japan. A large survey of 1,928 rabbits spayed at 43 hospitals found uterine cancer in about half. That group was not typical, because many of those rabbits were spayed after a problem was already suspected. Even so, almost half of the rabbits that had no signs at all already had cancer when the uterus was examined (Settai and colleagues, 2020).

None of these studies tells us the exact chance that your rabbit will get uterine cancer. They do agree that the risk is low in young rabbits and rises a lot after 3 years of age.

Why many rabbits show no signs

Uterine disease often grows quietly. In a study of 59 rabbits with uterine disease in Munich, only 10 had bleeding from the vulva or blood in the urine (Walter and colleagues, 2010). The others came in for lumps in the mammary glands, skin problems, poor appetite, or general poor health.

When signs do appear, they can include:

  • Blood in the urine, or what looks like blood in the urine. In rabbits, this blood often comes from the uterus rather than the bladder, and it may show up only when she urinates.
  • Discharge from the vulva.
  • Firm lumps, cysts, or swelling near the nipples.
  • A larger belly.
  • Weight loss, lower energy, or a smaller appetite.
  • Faster or harder breathing, which can mean the cancer has spread to the lungs.

If you are not sure whether what you are seeing is blood, your veterinarian can help sort it out.

Hormones and uterine cancer

Uterine cancer develops in rabbits whose ovaries are still working, and it becomes more common the longer those hormones act on the uterus. That is why removing the ovaries is the core of prevention.

The surgery options

"Spay" can mean different operations in rabbits, and the differences matter more than they do in cats and dogs.

  • Ovariectomy (OVE) removes only the ovaries. It is a shorter, less invasive surgery with a smaller incision, and in my observation, veterinary practice in Europe is moving toward it.
  • Ovariohysterectomy (OVH) removes the ovaries and the uterus.
  • OVH with partial vaginectomy removes the ovaries, the uterus, and the upper part of the vagina, along with the cervix.

Rabbits are built differently from cats and dogs. A rabbit has two separate uterine horns, each with its own cervix, and both open into a long, soft, floppy vagina. A standard OVH can leave both cervices behind, which is why I usually remove a portion of the vagina too. That way, no uterine tissue is left.

Why I usually recommend the more complete surgery

With an OVE, the uterus stays in place. In a young rabbit with a healthy uterus, that may be reasonable, and some veterinarians prefer it. My concern is twofold.

First, an older rabbit's uterus may already have changes that cannot be seen during surgery. In the Finnish study, about 1 in 7 rabbits had a uterus that looked normal but was abnormal under the microscope.

Second, rabbits can develop adrenal gland tumors, usually later in life. The adrenal glands sit near the kidneys and can make hormones similar to the ones the ovaries make. My worry is that if a rabbit who had an OVE later develops an adrenal tumor that makes these hormones, her remaining uterus could be affected. I want to be honest that this has not been proven, and there are no studies on it. Adrenal tumors are uncommon in rabbits and are mostly seen in older rabbits; the youngest case I have consulted on was about 4 years old. Because this is a possibility I can prevent, I generally recommend a full OVH with partial vaginectomy.

If your veterinarian recommends OVE for a young rabbit, that is a reasonable choice. It's a good topic to talk through together.

What surgery involves

Your rabbit will have a full exam first. For older rabbits, imaging is often recommended, such as ultrasound of the belly and x-rays of the chest, because finding a problem before surgery changes the plan. Research has shown that ultrasound finds uterine disease much more often than feeling the belly by hand.

During surgery, the blood vessels to the ovaries and uterus are tied off. I typically use sutures; some surgeons use small metal clips. Rabbits have a large blood vessel in the tissue that holds the uterus, so it is tied off carefully rather than torn, and the end of the vagina is closed securely.

After surgery, the removed uterus can be sent to a laboratory. This tells us whether any cancer was present, even if the uterus looked normal.

When is the best time to spay?

Recommendations range from about 3 months to 2 years of age, and most experts recommend spaying in the first year. Uterine disease is rare in young rabbits and becomes common after age 3, so spaying earlier avoids the years when risk rises. The best age for your rabbit depends on her breed, size, and health, so ask your veterinarian.

Older rabbits can still benefit from being spayed. They need a careful exam and testing first, because an older rabbit may already have uterine disease, and surgery then becomes treatment as well as prevention.

If your rabbit already has uterine cancer

Spaying is also the main treatment for uterine cancer. Before surgery, chest x-rays help check whether the cancer has spread to the lungs, because that changes what surgery can offer. In the Munich study, some rabbits with uterine cancer lived for nearly two years or more after surgery, while others developed lung spread in the months afterward (Walter and colleagues, 2010). Rechecks after surgery help catch spread early.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your rabbit:

  • Has blood in her urine or bleeding from the vulva, especially with weakness or pale gums
  • Is breathing fast or with effort
  • Stops eating or stops passing droppings
  • Seems painful, is hunched, or grinds her teeth

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Your female rabbit is over a year old and not spayed
  • Lumps, cysts, or swelling near the nipples
  • Discharge from the vulva
  • Urine that might have blood in it, even if she seems fine
  • Slow weight loss or a bigger belly
