Recognizing Pain in Exotic Companion Mammals

October 2026

Rabbits, guinea pigs, ferrets, and other exotic companion mammals often hide pain. That is a survival behavior, and it means a quiet animal can be uncomfortable long before anyone notices. Catching it early changes the outcome, especially with dental disease, urinary disease, and a gut that has slowed down.

Subtle signs to watch for

The changes are often small. Common subtle signs include:

  • Reduced activity
  • Decreased appetite or hay consumption
  • Changes in posture, such as hunching or a reluctance to move
  • Teeth grinding
  • Reduced grooming

In some species, pain can also appear as aggression, hiding, or changes in litter-box habits. These animals are small, and they can decline quickly, so a mild change is still a reason to call.

How Dr. Kopp approaches pain

Dr. Kopp is a Fellow of the American Board of Veterinary Practitioners in Companion Animal Pain Management. In practice, that means looking hard for pain in exotic mammals and treating it from several angles: pain medication, changes at home, and treating the underlying problem.

When to call

If you notice a change in appetite, activity, or behavior, call us. Catching pain early usually makes it easier to treat. Call now if your pet has stopped eating, is hunched and unwilling to move, or seems suddenly weak. Book soon for a slower change, such as less grooming, a pickier appetite, or a pet who is quieter than they used to be.
