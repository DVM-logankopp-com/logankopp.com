Recognizing Pain in Exotic Companion Mammals

October 2026

Rabbits, guinea pigs, ferrets, and other exotic companion mammals often hide signs of pain. This is a natural survival behavior, but it can make it harder for owners and veterinarians to recognize when an animal is uncomfortable. Early recognition improves outcomes, especially for conditions such as dental disease, urinary issues, or gastrointestinal slowdown.

Common subtle signs include reduced activity, decreased appetite or hay consumption, changes in posture (hunching or reluctance to move), teeth grinding, or reduced grooming. In some species, pain can also appear as aggression, hiding, or changes in litter-box habits. Because these animals are small and can decline quickly, even mild changes warrant attention.

Dr. Kopp is a Fellow of the American Board of Veterinary Practitioners in Companion Animal Pain Management. In practice this means a deliberate focus on recognizing pain in exotic mammals and using multimodal approaches—including appropriate analgesics, environmental modifications, and treatment of the underlying problem—to keep patients more comfortable.

Owners who notice any change in appetite, activity, or behavior are encouraged to call the clinic. Prompt evaluation often allows earlier intervention and better recovery.
