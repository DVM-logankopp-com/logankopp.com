Metoclopramide and Cisapride in Rabbits: What the Pharmacokinetic and Efficacy Studies Show

November 23, 2026

Metoclopramide and cisapride have been on rabbit GI stasis protocols for as long as most of us have been in practice. In my view, they are not standard of care for rabbits with reduced food intake or fecal output, and in my experience most exotic animal specialists do not use them routinely. The evidence has been moving in one direction: each controlled study published so far has found no measurable effect on food intake or fecal output at the doses tested.

The physiology first

Rabbits are obligate herbivores with a large GI tract: a simple, thin-walled stomach, a small intestine that makes up about 12% of the tract, and a large hindgut. The cecum accounts for about 40%. A well-developed cardiac sphincter means rabbits cannot vomit. Digestion in the stomach and small intestine is similar to that of other monogastric species.

The hindgut is where rabbits differ most from dogs and cats. At the ileocecocolic junction, the gut separates ingesta by particle size. Large particles, roughly over 0.5 mm, move on and leave as hard feces after water is absorbed. Smaller particles go to the cecum for fermentation and come back as cecotropes, which the rabbit eats directly from the anus, usually once or twice a day. Feldman and colleagues describe the same design: indigestible fiber is moved out as quickly as possible, while fine particles and solutes are held in the cecum for microbial fermentation. Large particle fiber has little nutritional value, but it matters a great deal for motility.

The way I think about motility in the rabbit hindgut starts with the fusus coli. The fusus coli is the pacemaker of the rabbit GI tract, and what drives it is fiber length. If you understand that, the treatment of hypomotility follows: the most direct way to get the hindgut moving is to get long fiber moving through it.

A few other details explain why rabbits with GI disease get sick quickly and why their radiographs can mislead:

  • Rabbits produce large amounts of saliva continuously, even with an intestinal obstruction, and water is secreted into the stomach and small intestine and reabsorbed in the cecum and colon. As hypomotility worsens, less water is secreted, ingesta thickens, and impaction becomes more likely. Dehydration can become profound.
  • Di Girolamo and colleagues note that the rabbit stomach is relatively large and stays partly full even after a long fast, with about half of its contents still present after 24 hours. A rabbit's stomach on radiographs can look replete during anorexia, and they suggest that this normal finding may be overread as delayed gastric emptying.
  • Reported transit times are about 14 hours on a high-fiber diet and 20 hours on a low-fiber diet (as cited by Di Girolamo and colleagues).

I use the term rabbit gastrointestinal syndrome (RGIS) rather than "stasis" because it keeps the differential list open. RGIS is caused by anything that disrupts normal GI function. It is not a primary diagnosis. Pain, dental disease, dehydration, diet, stress, liver lobe torsion, and obstruction can all present as a rabbit that has stopped eating with small or few fecal pellets. I've written more about that differential in referring a rabbit with suspected GI stasis.

How the drugs are supposed to work

Metoclopramide is a dopamine (D2) antagonist with serotonergic effects. Stratton and colleagues describe its prokinetic action as mainly 5-HT4 agonism in GI smooth muscle, which increases acetylcholine release from enteric cholinergic neurons. De Vito and colleagues describe D2 and 5-HT3 antagonism and sensitization of gastric and colonic longitudinal muscle to acetylcholine. In people, it increases duodenal peristalsis and gastric emptying. It crosses the blood-brain barrier, which is the source of its central side effects in people.

Cisapride is a 5-HT4 agonist with some 5-HT3 antagonist activity. In people, dogs, cats, and mice, it increases motility of the esophagus, stomach, small intestine, and colon (Feldman and colleagues).

Both drugs were developed around upper GI motility in species that vomit and have very different hindguts. Their effects in dogs, cats, and people cannot be assumed to carry over to rabbits.

Pharmacokinetics: what reaches the plasma

Pharmacokinetic (PK) studies tell us whether a drug is absorbed and how long it stays in plasma. They do not tell us whether it changes food intake or fecal output.

  • De Vito and colleagues (2015) gave metoclopramide to six healthy intact male New Zealand White rabbits at 2 mg/kg intra-arterially, intramuscularly, and subcutaneously, and 4 mg/kg per rectum, in a crossover design. Bioavailability was 96% IM and 112% SC, with peak plasma concentrations reached in less than 10 minutes. Per rectal bioavailability was low and erratic, averaging 12%. Elimination was rapid, with mean terminal half-lives of roughly 1 hour after parenteral dosing. No adverse effects were observed. The authors concluded that IM and SC routes may be useful when vascular access is not available, that the rectal route is likely unreliable, and that clinical efficacy studies were needed. They also note that the dose range recommended for rabbits at the time was wide (0.1 to 2 mg/kg).
  • Oral metoclopramide. Stratton and colleagues cite a single-dose oral PK study at 0.5 mg/kg that found a half-life of 4.5 hours. Di Girolamo and colleagues, writing earlier, note that the PK study they found did not investigate the oral route.
  • Cisapride. Di Girolamo and colleagues found that the two articles most often cited for cisapride in rabbits were a 1987 PK study at 5 mg/kg, ten times the commonly suggested dose, and an ex vivo study they considered of doubtful translational value. They found no PK or pharmacodynamic studies with more direct clinical relevance.

So for metoclopramide, we know that parenteral doses are absorbed well and cleared quickly. For cisapride at the doses in common formularies, the PK basis is thin.

Efficacy: does anything change?

Single doses in healthy rabbits (Di Girolamo and colleagues, 2025)

In a randomized, blinded, complete crossover trial, 10 healthy intact male New Zealand White rabbits each received a single oral dose of metoclopramide (0.5 mg/kg), cisapride (0.5 mg/kg), pyridostigmine (1 mg/kg), capromorelin (3 mg/kg), or saline placebo. Fecal weight and pellet number, food intake, urine production, and water intake were measured before and for 72 hours after each dose.

None of the four drugs changed any outcome compared with placebo at 24, 48, or 72 hours. The authors point out that metoclopramide and cisapride have been listed in the Exotic Animal Formulary at these doses for more than 20 years, and that the formulary's references for rabbit prokinetics cite other formularies or personal communications rather than original research.

Limitations: healthy rabbits, a single dose, compounded formulations, and a small sample. The authors argue that effective prokinetics usually increase defecation in healthy subjects as well, so a clinically relevant effect in sick rabbits at these doses seems unlikely, but they did not test sick rabbits.

Repeated high doses in rabbits eating less (Stratton and colleagues, 2026)

Stratton and colleagues addressed the two biggest criticisms of the single-dose study: healthy rabbits and a single dose. They first cut the rabbits' food intake with high-concentration buprenorphine (0.24 mg/kg SC every 24 hours for 2 days), which worked in 11 of 12 rabbits. Pilot work found no effect at 0.5 or 2 mg/kg of oral metoclopramide, so the main study used 5 mg/kg orally every 12 hours for 2 days, ten times the most commonly recommended dose. The design was a randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled complete crossover in 3-month-old New Zealand White rabbits (6 males and 6 females).

Metoclopramide had no clinically relevant or statistically significant effect on food intake or fecal output over time compared with placebo, and rabbits on metoclopramide did not return to baseline faster. On two individual days the metoclopramide group did worse (lower food intake on day 5, lower fecal output on day 11). No overt adverse effects were seen. The authors concluded that current evidence does not support routine use of metoclopramide in hyporexic rabbits.

Limitations, as the authors describe them: a small, homogeneous group of juvenile laboratory rabbits of one breed, a pellet-only diet during the study, opioid-induced hyporexia that may not match RGIS in a painful pet rabbit, no ultrasound to confirm ileus or measure motility directly, and only four doses. They note that metoclopramide acts within 30 to 60 minutes in people, so a longer course at the same dose and interval seems unlikely to change the result.

Cisapride with and without buprenorphine (Feldman and colleagues, 2021)

Feldman and colleagues studied 10 healthy female New Zealand White rabbits in a randomized crossover design. Each received oral vehicle plus SC saline, oral vehicle plus buprenorphine (0.03 mg/kg SC), oral cisapride (0.5 mg/kg) plus SC saline, or cisapride plus buprenorphine, every 8 hours for 2 days. GI transit was measured with barium-filled spheres given by orogastric tube.

Buprenorphine prolonged GI transit and reduced fecal output and food and water intake. Cisapride did not change transit time, fecal output, food and water intake, or body weight, alone or with buprenorphine. The authors suggest that higher doses, or rabbits with ileus from other causes, could be studied.

Other data

Di Girolamo and colleagues cite an earlier rabbit study in which oral cisapride at 0.5 mg/kg three times a day had no effect on GI transit time, fecal output, or food and water intake. Stratton and colleagues cite a chinchilla study in which oral cisapride given with syringe feeding produced a slight increase in fecal output that was not clinically relevant, and cisapride without syringe feeding had no effect.

The three studies side by side

Three controlled rabbit studies, using metoclopramide at up to ten times the usual dose and cisapride at the usual dose, in healthy rabbits, rabbits on opioids, and rabbits eating less, found no effect on the outcomes that matter to us clinically. These are laboratory studies, and none enrolled pet rabbits with naturally occurring RGIS. That is a real gap. But the burden of proof sits with the drug, and at this point the drugs have not met it.

Safety: what we do and do not know

The short studies above reported no overt adverse effects in rabbits. De Vito and colleagues did not see the lip licking, head shaking, or head pressing reported in goats. Absence of adverse effects in a few healthy laboratory rabbits over 2 to 4 days is not evidence of safety in a sick pet rabbit treated for a week.

What is documented outside rabbits:

  • Metoclopramide crosses the blood-brain barrier, and central effects such as shaking and muscle stiffness have been reported in people and goats (De Vito and colleagues).
  • Cisapride was withdrawn from the US human market in 2000 because of serious cardiac arrhythmias and deaths (Di Girolamo and colleagues). Di Girolamo's group calls for larger safety and effectiveness studies before prokinetics are used more widely in rabbits.

The safety concern I think about most is not in these papers. It is obstruction. A rabbit with a partial or complete intestinal obstruction can look like a rabbit with uncomplicated RGIS in the first hours. Driving motility against a blockage is not something I want to do, and a prokinetic given early, before obstruction has been ruled out, adds that risk for no demonstrated benefit. Sometimes doing something is worse than doing nothing. I have written about how obstruction, liver lobe torsion, and gastric dilation present in rabbits.

The false standard of care

When a rabbit goes home with metoclopramide or cisapride, the family reasonably assumes it is getting the treatment that is supposed to fix the problem. That gives a false sense of what standard care is. It can shift attention away from what actually helps: finding the cause, treating pain, correcting dehydration, and getting fiber moving through the gut.

Di Girolamo and colleagues offer one explanation for why prokinetics have seemed to work for so long. Rabbits often do not resume eating until 12 to 24 hours after surgery, and with a transit time of about 14 hours, normal fecal output may not be expected until about 36 hours after surgery. A prokinetic given at 24 hours is likely to get credit for output that was coming anyway. A replete stomach on radiographs during anorexia, which is normal in rabbits, can also prompt a prokinetic that was not needed.

What I do instead

My approach to a rabbit with RGIS centers on the cause and on supportive care:

  • Look for the reason. Pain, dental disease, dehydration, diet, stress, and obstruction all need to be considered. A rabbit that is painful, bloated, or shocky needs imaging and possibly surgery, not a prokinetic.
  • Fluids, because these rabbits lose a lot of water into the proximal GI tract.
  • Assisted feeding with a mix of particle sizes, both above and below 0.5 mm. Feeding long fiber is, in my view, far more likely to help motility than any prokinetic, because it acts on the same mechanism the fusus coli depends on.
  • Analgesia. Stratton and colleagues note that most rabbits will not start eating until pain is controlled, and that opioids should be considered for moderate to severe pain. The opioid studies above (Feldman, Gronsky, Stratton) were done in rabbits that were not in pain. Gronsky and colleagues found that a single SC injection of high-concentration buprenorphine (0.24 mg/kg) reduced median food intake in the next 24 hours to 17 g, compared with 122 g in saline controls, in healthy rabbits. That is a reason to monitor intake and output closely after opioids and to use them deliberately in painful rabbits. I've written about analgesia in rabbits separately.
  • No enemas. These rabbits are usually not defecating because they are not eating, and colonic rupture can occur.

Is there any place for a prokinetic?

Two possibilities remain open.

The first is a constant rate infusion. Metoclopramide has a short half-life, and in dogs and cats it is given up to four times daily or as an IV CRI. Stratton and colleagues raise the possibility that more frequent dosing or an IV CRI might produce an effect that every-12-hour oral dosing did not. A CRI is the only way I think metoclopramide might work in rabbits. I did not find any study of metoclopramide CRI efficacy in rabbits in the literature I reviewed, so this remains a hypothesis.

The second is the Hail Mary. In a hospitalized rabbit with obstruction ruled out, a cause identified and treated, pain controlled, fluids and assisted feeding underway, and still no progress, I can see adding a prokinetic as one part of a full plan, with the family told plainly that the evidence for it is weak. That is very different from sending a rabbit home on metoclopramide as the treatment.

What we still need

  • An efficacy study of metoclopramide CRI in rabbits.
  • Studies in pet rabbits with naturally occurring RGIS, ideally with motility measured directly, not only food intake and fecal output.
  • Multiple-dose and higher-dose studies of cisapride, with PK data at the doses actually used.
  • Safety data for longer treatment courses in sick rabbits.
