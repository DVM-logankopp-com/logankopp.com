Meloxicam in Rabbits and Guinea Pigs: Dosing, COX Data, and Safety

November 12, 2026

Meloxicam is the most commonly used NSAID in rabbits in some countries, and the most prescribed analgesic in guinea pigs.1,2 It's easy for families to give at home, and it fits into almost every multimodal plan. The doses many of us first learned, though, were borrowed. Rabbit and guinea pig doses were originally set at the dog and cat range of 0.1 to 0.3 mg/kg.3

Over the last twenty years the species data have pushed that number up. Here are the rabbit and guinea pig papers in one place, sorted into what has been measured, what has been inferred, and what is simply habit.

Rabbit pharmacokinetics: why the dose went up

The early oral studies used low doses. Carpenter and colleagues gave 8 New Zealand White rabbits 0.2 mg/kg PO daily for 10 days. Peak plasma concentrations were 0.17 µg/mL on day 1 and 0.24 µg/mL on day 10, and the half-life was about 8 hours. It was well tolerated. The authors suggested 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg every 24 hours, while noting that a higher dose might be needed and would require efficacy and safety studies.4

Turner and colleagues gave female rabbits (5 per group) 0.3 or 1.5 mg/kg PO as single doses and once daily for 5 days. Peak concentrations of 0.14 and 0.3 µg/mL were reached at 6 to 8 hours, and plasma levels fell to near-undetectable by 24 hours. Increases at the higher dose were less than proportional, the half-life was about 8 hours, and there was no accumulation over 5 days. They concluded that rabbits may need more than 0.3 mg/kg once daily.5

Fredholm and colleagues then tested 1 mg/kg PO in 6 healthy New Zealand White rabbits, first as a single dose and then every 24 hours for 5 days. Mean peak concentration was 0.83 µg/mL after one dose and 1.33 µg/mL after five. Time to peak was 6.5 and 5.8 hours, and terminal half-life was 6.1 and 6.7 hours. Meloxicam accumulated, with a mean accumulation ratio of 1.55 that ranged from 1.27 to 2.08 between rabbits.6 That single-dose peak is close to the 0.82 µg/mL reported in dogs given a clinically effective 0.2 mg/kg. So rabbits needed about five times the canine dose to reach a similar plasma concentration. Efficacy was not tested.

Delk and colleagues extended 1 mg/kg PO every 24 hours to 29 days in 6 healthy rabbits. Mean peak concentration rose from 0.67 µg/mL after the first dose to 1.07 µg/mL after dose 29, with no significant difference among doses 8 through 29. Weekly biochemistry stayed within reference ranges, and gross necropsy and histology found nothing clinically relevant.7 This is the study behind "safe for up to 29 days," and it is worth remembering what it was: six healthy young rabbits.

What the COX data add

Plasma concentration is a borrowed target. Sarvi and colleagues asked a more direct question: does 1 mg/kg actually suppress cyclooxygenase products in rabbits, and for how long?1

They gave 6 New Zealand White rabbits a single oral dose of 1 mg/kg and measured thromboxane B2 (a marker of COX-1 activity) and prostaglandin E2 (a marker of COX-2 activity) in stimulated whole blood over 48 hours. Both fell significantly. The lowest values came at 8 hours, about 80% below baseline for thromboxane B2 and about 60% below baseline for prostaglandin E2. Prostaglandin E2 then climbed back toward baseline between 8 and 24 hours. It was back to about 50% of baseline by 12 hours and only about 10% below baseline at 24 hours.

Two points matter clinically. First, COX-1 was suppressed as much as or more than COX-2, so the authors suggest meloxicam may behave as a nonselective NSAID in rabbits. The COX-2 preference seen in dogs, cats, horses, and people may not carry over. Second, the timing of COX-2 suppression lines up with the half-lives from Fredholm and Delk, and the authors suggest a dosing interval shorter than 24 hours may be needed. No rabbit showed lethargy, inappetence, or fecal changes. Still, this was one dose in six rabbits, and the authors point out that COX suppression doesn't prove analgesia.

Does it control surgical pain?

Leach and colleagues gave 28 laboratory rabbits oral placebo or one of three meloxicam regimens (7 per group) before ovariohysterectomy and for 2 days after. The high-dose group received 1 mg/kg, then 0.5 mg/kg daily. By their behavioral criteria, none of the doses gave adequate analgesia. The medium and high doses did appear to give some degree of analgesia, and the authors concluded that higher doses, or meloxicam combined with an opioid, may be needed for consistent analgesia after soft-tissue surgery.8

Two laboratory studies looked at meloxicam alongside buprenorphine. After ovariohysterectomy in 30 female Dutch Belted rabbits, feed intake and fecal output did not differ between meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg SC every 24 hours and buprenorphine 0.03 mg/kg IM every 12 hours.9 After a femoral vascular cut-down in 39 male New Zealand White rabbits, meloxicam alone at 0.2 mg/kg SC every 24 hours did not prevent a rise in fecal corticosterone metabolites. Low-dose buprenorphine combined with meloxicam (0.01 and 0.1 mg/kg SC every 24 hours) kept them roughly flat until treatment stopped on day 3, and those rabbits gained more weight over 28 days.10 Fecal corticosterone is a stress marker, not a pain score, but the multimodal signal fits the Leach data.

In pet rabbits, Serpieri and colleagues gave 50 client-owned rabbits a single postoperative SC dose of meloxicam (1 mg/kg) or carprofen (2 mg/kg) after neutering. Pain scores and return of eating and fecal output did not differ, and no clinically detectable adverse effects were seen. There was no placebo group and only one pain scale, so this shows the two drugs performed similarly in that protocol, not that either is sufficient alone.11

Timing has been tested once. Liles and colleagues randomized 42 rabbits undergoing castration to meloxicam 1 mg/kg SC either 1 hour before surgery or on recovery. Eating, defecation, and pain scores did not differ between groups. Age did matter: the odds of not eating rose 1.16 times per month of age.12

Miller and colleagues compared behavior and rabbit grimace scale scores after castration in Dutch Belted rabbits (8 per group).13 Rabbits given meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg SC showed more pain behaviors at 1 and 5 hours than rabbits given meloxicam 0.6 mg/kg SC plus local lidocaine (0.4 mg/kg) and bupivacaine (0.2 mg/kg).

Safety and toxicity

The study most often quoted on rabbit meloxicam toxicity comes from the International Journal of Biosciences.14 Ahmad and colleagues gave 18 domestic-breed rabbits, about 3 months old and bought at a local market, no drug, 1.5 mg/kg, or 3.0 mg/kg of meloxicam for 7 days, then examined liver and kidney from 3 rabbits per group at days 5 and 10 after treatment. At 1.5 mg/kg there was mild sinusoidal and central vein dilation, mild Kupffer cell proliferation, and mild tubular changes at day 5, all reversed by day 10. At 3.0 mg/kg there was severe hepatocellular necrosis and vacuolation, glomerular shrinkage, and tubular damage that persisted to day 10. A companion paper from the same group, with the same dose groups given intramuscularly, reported rises in ALT, ALP, and urea on day 1 at 1.5 mg/kg and more sustained elevations, including creatinine, at 3.0 mg/kg.15

These findings are dose dependent, but they come from 3 young market rabbits per group at each time point, and the companion paper describes intramuscular rather than oral dosing. I don't treat them as universal toxicity thresholds for individual patients. They are still a fair reminder that higher doses carry more risk.

The clinical case is Scarabelli and Nardini's report in the Journal of Exotic Pet Medicine.16 A 7-year-8-month-old spayed female rabbit had received meloxicam at 0.2 mg/kg twice daily for 5 months for a renal pelvic urolith. She had a nephrotomy, seemed to be improving, then stopped eating on day 5 and arrested. Necropsy found multiple gastric ulcers, a 4 mm perforated duodenal ulcer with vasculitis, thrombosis, necrosis and fibrosis, and septic peritonitis. The authors raise both an occult GI disease and the prolonged meloxicam course as possible causes and state that a single case can't establish causation. The dose was low, and the rabbit had also had major surgery, so a direct causal link is unclear. Gastric ulceration is a common postmortem finding in rabbits in general.1 The authors' practical conclusion is that periodic monitoring is indicated when meloxicam is prescribed long term.

On gut flora, Eshar and Weese sequenced hard feces from six young female New Zealand White rabbits given 1 mg/kg PO every 24 hours for 21 days. There were few differences between time points at the phylum level, though Proteobacteria abundance did change significantly over time. The authors concluded that significant alteration of the hard fecal microbiota is not an expected adverse effect at that dose.17

Hamouzova and colleagues gave 8 rabbits meloxicam 1 mg/kg PO every 24 hours for 14 days and compared them with 8 controls.18 The proportion of CD5+CD8+ lymphocytes rose, T and B cell activation fell, and the T:B cell ratio decreased slightly, while food intake, feces, and clinical exams did not change.

Guinea pigs

The guinea pig evidence is thinner. Moeremans and colleagues gave 6 healthy male Dunkin Hartley guinea pigs a single 1.5 mg/kg dose PO and IV in a crossover design.3 After oral dosing, peak concentration was 0.92 µg/mL at 3.7 hours, similar to rabbits given 1 mg/kg. Terminal half-life was much shorter, about 3.5 to 3.7 hours, and absolute oral bioavailability in unfasted animals was 54%, ranging from 25% to 77%. From simulations, the authors proposed 1.5 mg/kg PO every 12 hours. Even that regimen fell short of the mean effective concentrations calculated for dogs and horses, though it would be sufficient compared with cats. Giving 3 mg/kg once daily would produce higher peaks, which they flagged for caution. No efficacy or repeated-dose safety data exist yet in this species.

Evans and Benato's 2025 review sets that against the UK licence, which is 0.2 mg/kg on day 1, then 0.1 mg/kg daily, with up to 0.5 mg/kg on an individual basis. Based on the pharmacokinetic data, they conclude the licensed dose is probably subtherapeutic, and they advise caution with NSAIDs in guinea pigs with existing renal disorders.19 Sadar and Mans reach a similar conclusion: the short half-life may mean dosing more often than every 24 hours, but multiple-dose safety still needs study.2

Ellen and colleagues studied 16 male guinea pigs after orchiectomy. Animals that received meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg SC before surgery with local bupivacaine and lidocaine had lower composite pain behavior scores than untreated controls. With local anesthesia in the mix, the effect can't be credited to meloxicam alone. The useful lesson is about recognition: pain behaviors were subtle and infrequent. The most common was a shift from standing to recumbency, sometimes with one hind leg extended to the side or behind the body.20 I covered pain recognition across species in my post on recognizing pain in exotic companion mammals.

How I use it

In rabbits, I use 1 mg/kg every 24 hours for routine perioperative and postoperative analgesia, and the 29-day Delk data support that course length. Based on Fredholm's pharmacokinetics and Sarvi's COX data, I occasionally use 1 mg/kg every 12 hours. I start NSAIDs after surgery, once temperature and blood pressure are stable, because of the renal risk during anesthetic hypotension. I'm cautious in rabbits with advanced renal disease. Similar to how we approach cats with chronic kidney disease, meloxicam can still be used carefully in these patients.

In guinea pigs, the 1.5 mg/kg PO every 12 hours regimen comes from pharmacokinetic modeling and has not been clinically validated. I use 1.5 mg/kg PO every 12 hours in guinea pigs routinely. I watch closely for hyporexia (reduced appetite) and stop meloxicam if it occurs. If you use meloxicam in guinea pigs, monitor lab work. The low licensed dose may well undertreat pain, but moving to higher or repeated doses means watching hydration, renal function, appetite, and body weight.

Takeaways

  • Rabbits need roughly 1 mg/kg PO to reach plasma concentrations that are effective in dogs and cats. Doses of 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg fall well short.
  • In rabbits, meloxicam appears to inhibit COX-1 about as much as COX-2, and COX-2 suppression after a single dose fades well before 24 hours.
  • Meloxicam alone at the doses tested did not consistently control pain after soft-tissue surgery. Plan multimodal analgesia.
  • The 29-day safety data come from six healthy rabbits. Monitor long-term patients, especially older rabbits or those with GI or renal disease.
  • Guinea pigs clear meloxicam faster than rabbits and absorb it less completely. Dosing every 12 hours is a pharmacokinetic proposal, not a validated regimen.
