Meloxicam in Rabbits and Guinea Pigs: Dosing, Pharmacokinetics, and Safety

November 12, 2026

Meloxicam is one of the NSAIDs I use most frequently in rabbits and guinea pigs. It is relatively easy for families to administer, generally well tolerated, and an important component of multimodal analgesia. However, there continues to be considerable variation in the doses recommended for these species, particularly when compared with dogs and cats.

Historically, meloxicam was administered to rabbits and guinea pigs at doses of approximately 0.1 to 0.3 mg/kg, largely extrapolated from recommendations for dogs and cats.3 Over the past two decades, several pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and clinical studies have demonstrated that these lower doses may not provide adequate drug exposure or analgesia in rabbits. The evidence in guinea pigs remains considerably more limited.

I wanted to review the available literature, particularly the studies evaluating dosing, duration of action, analgesic efficacy, and safety, and discuss how these findings influence my clinical use of meloxicam.

Meloxicam pharmacokinetics in rabbits

Carpenter et al. evaluated oral meloxicam administered at 0.2 mg/kg every 24 hours for 10 days in eight New Zealand White rabbits.4 Peak plasma concentrations were 0.17 µg/mL on the first day and 0.24 µg/mL on day 10, with an elimination half-life of approximately 8 hours. Although the medication was well tolerated, the authors suggested that higher doses might be necessary to achieve therapeutic concentrations. They initially recommended 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg every 24 hours, pending additional efficacy and safety studies.

Turner et al. subsequently evaluated oral doses of 0.3 and 1.5 mg/kg in rabbits following single and repeated administration.5 Peak plasma concentrations were approximately 0.14 and 0.3 µg/mL, respectively, occurring 6 to 8 hours after administration. Plasma concentrations declined to nearly undetectable levels within 24 hours. Interestingly, increasing the dose fivefold did not produce a proportional increase in plasma concentrations. The elimination half-life was approximately 8 hours, and no accumulation was identified following five days of administration. The authors concluded that doses greater than 0.3 mg/kg every 24 hours might be necessary in rabbits.

Fredholm et al. evaluated meloxicam administered orally at 1 mg/kg in six healthy New Zealand White rabbits, initially as a single dose and subsequently every 24 hours for five days.6 The mean peak plasma concentration was 0.83 µg/mL after the initial dose and increased to 1.33 µg/mL following repeated administration. The elimination half-life ranged from approximately 6.1 to 6.7 hours. Unlike the Turner study, some drug accumulation was observed, with a mean accumulation ratio of 1.55.

One important finding from this study was that a dose of 1 mg/kg in rabbits produced peak plasma concentrations comparable to those reported in dogs receiving 0.2 mg/kg, a dose known to provide analgesia in that species. This suggests that rabbits require substantially higher doses to achieve comparable plasma concentrations. However, achieving similar plasma concentrations does not necessarily establish equivalent analgesic efficacy between species.

Delk et al. extended the evaluation of 1 mg/kg orally every 24 hours to 29 days in six healthy rabbits.7 Mean peak plasma concentrations increased from 0.67 µg/mL following the first dose to 1.07 µg/mL after the 29th dose, with no significant differences identified among doses 8 through 29. Serial serum biochemical testing remained within reference intervals, and gross and histopathological examinations did not identify clinically relevant abnormalities.

This study provides some reassurance regarding the administration of meloxicam at 1 mg/kg every 24 hours for approximately one month. However, only six healthy rabbits were evaluated. These findings cannot necessarily be extrapolated to older rabbits or patients with preexisting renal disease, dehydration, or other systemic illnesses.

Cyclooxygenase inhibition and duration of action

While pharmacokinetic studies are useful for determining drug absorption and elimination, they do not establish how effectively meloxicam inhibits cyclooxygenase activity in rabbits.

Sarvi et al. evaluated the effects of a single oral dose of meloxicam at 1 mg/kg on cyclooxygenase activity in six New Zealand White rabbits.1 Thromboxane B2 was measured as an indicator of COX-1 activity, while prostaglandin E2 was used to evaluate COX-2 activity.

Both markers decreased significantly following administration. Maximum inhibition occurred approximately 8 hours after dosing, with thromboxane B2 concentrations decreasing approximately 80% and prostaglandin E2 approximately 60% from baseline.

However, prostaglandin E2 concentrations began returning toward baseline between 8 and 24 hours. By 12 hours, concentrations had returned to approximately 50% of baseline, and by 24 hours, COX-2 inhibition was minimal.

There are two clinically relevant findings from this study.

First, meloxicam appeared to inhibit COX-1 to a similar or greater extent than COX-2 in rabbits. Although meloxicam is generally considered a preferential COX-2 inhibitor in several other species, this selectivity may not apply to rabbits.

Second, the relatively short duration of COX-2 inhibition suggests that administration every 24 hours may not provide sustained pharmacodynamic effects. This is consistent with the relatively short elimination half-life reported in the pharmacokinetic studies.

The authors suggested that more frequent administration might be necessary. However, this study evaluated only a single dose in six healthy rabbits. Additional research is needed to determine whether more frequent administration improves analgesia and whether repeated dosing increases the risk of adverse effects.

Clinical analgesic efficacy in rabbits

Several studies have evaluated meloxicam following surgical procedures in rabbits, although differences in study design, doses, and methods of pain assessment make direct comparisons difficult.

Leach et al. evaluated several oral meloxicam dosing regimens following ovariohysterectomy in 28 laboratory rabbits.8 The highest-dose group received 1 mg/kg initially, followed by 0.5 mg/kg every 24 hours. Although the medium- and high-dose groups demonstrated some evidence of analgesia, none of the evaluated regimens consistently provided adequate postoperative pain control based on the behavioral assessments used. The authors suggested that higher doses or the addition of an opioid might be necessary.

Cooper et al. compared meloxicam at 0.2 mg/kg subcutaneously every 24 hours with buprenorphine at 0.03 mg/kg intramuscularly every 12 hours following ovariohysterectomy.9 No significant differences were identified in food consumption or fecal production between the groups.

Goldschlager et al. evaluated meloxicam alone and in combination with low-dose buprenorphine following a femoral vascular cut-down procedure.10 Rabbits receiving meloxicam alone demonstrated increased fecal corticosterone metabolites, while rabbits receiving multimodal analgesia maintained more consistent concentrations during treatment and demonstrated improved postoperative weight gain. Although fecal corticosterone metabolites are indicators of physiological stress rather than direct measurements of pain, these results provide additional support for a multimodal approach.

More recently, Serpieri et al. compared meloxicam at 1 mg/kg with carprofen at 2 mg/kg, both administered subcutaneously, in 50 client-owned rabbits following neutering.11 No significant differences were identified in postoperative pain scores, food consumption, or fecal production. No clinically apparent adverse effects were reported. However, because there was no placebo group, the study demonstrates comparable outcomes between these two treatment protocols rather than establishing that either medication provides sufficient analgesia when used alone.

Liles et al. evaluated whether administering meloxicam at 1 mg/kg subcutaneously before castration provided an advantage over administration during recovery.12 No significant differences were identified in postoperative food consumption, fecal production, or pain indicators. Interestingly, increasing age was associated with a greater likelihood of postoperative inappetence.

Miller et al. compared postoperative behavior and rabbit grimace scale scores following castration in rabbits receiving different analgesic protocols.13 Rabbits receiving meloxicam at 0.2 mg/kg subcutaneously demonstrated more pain-associated behaviors than rabbits receiving 0.6 mg/kg combined with local anesthetics. Although the use of local anesthesia prevents attributing the improvement entirely to the higher meloxicam dose, the findings further support using multimodal analgesia for surgical procedures.

These studies suggest that the lower doses historically recommended for rabbits may be inadequate, particularly following painful surgical procedures. Meloxicam should not necessarily be relied upon as the sole analgesic for procedures associated with moderate to severe pain.

Safety and potential toxicity in rabbits

Safety remains an important consideration, especially when using higher doses or administering meloxicam for extended periods.

One of the relatively few studies specifically evaluating hepatic and renal histopathological changes associated with higher meloxicam doses in rabbits was published by Ahmad et al. in the International Journal of Biosciences.14

The investigators evaluated 18 young domestic rabbits divided among a control group and groups receiving meloxicam at 1.5 or 3 mg/kg for seven days. Liver and kidney tissues were subsequently examined at two time points.

At 1.5 mg/kg, mild hepatic changes, including sinusoidal and central vein dilation and Kupffer cell proliferation, were reported. Mild renal tubular changes were also identified. These abnormalities were no longer evident at the later evaluation.

In contrast, rabbits receiving 3 mg/kg demonstrated more substantial histopathological abnormalities, including hepatocellular necrosis, vacuolation, glomerular changes, and renal tubular damage. Some abnormalities persisted at the later evaluation.

A separate study from the same investigators evaluated changes in serum biochemical parameters following intramuscular administration of the same meloxicam doses.15 Increases in ALT, ALP, and urea were reported at 1.5 mg/kg, while more persistent biochemical abnormalities, including increased creatinine concentrations, occurred in the higher-dose group.

These studies are important because relatively few investigations have specifically evaluated potential organ toxicity associated with higher meloxicam doses in rabbits. However, there are significant limitations. The sample sizes were small, the rabbits were young, and the evaluation periods were relatively short. Additionally, the intramuscular administration described in the biochemical study may not be directly comparable to the oral dosing protocols commonly used in clinical practice.

I think these findings warrant consideration, particularly when using higher doses, but they should not be interpreted as establishing specific toxicity thresholds for individual rabbits. They also need to be considered alongside studies such as Delk et al., in which no clinically relevant biochemical or histopathological abnormalities were identified following 29 days of oral administration at 1 mg/kg.7

Gastrointestinal adverse effects

Scarabelli and Nardini described a rabbit that developed severe gastrointestinal lesions following prolonged meloxicam administration.16 The rabbit, a 7-year-8-month-old spayed female, had received meloxicam at 0.2 mg/kg every 12 hours for approximately five months for a renal pelvic urolith.

Following a nephrotomy, the rabbit initially appeared to recover but subsequently developed anorexia and experienced cardiopulmonary arrest five days after surgery. Necropsy identified multiple gastric ulcers, a perforated duodenal ulcer, gastrointestinal vasculitis, thrombosis, necrosis, and septic peritonitis.

The authors considered prolonged NSAID administration a possible contributing factor, although an underlying gastrointestinal disorder could not be excluded.

It is difficult to establish a direct association between meloxicam and these lesions based on a single case, particularly given the concurrent renal disease and recent surgical intervention. Nevertheless, rabbits receiving prolonged NSAID therapy should be monitored, even when relatively low doses are being administered.

Effects on the gastrointestinal microbiota and immune system

Eshar and Weese evaluated changes in the fecal microbiota of six healthy rabbits receiving meloxicam at 1 mg/kg orally every 24 hours for 21 days.17 Although some changes in bacterial populations were identified, including variation in Proteobacteria abundance, the investigators concluded that significant disruption of the hard fecal microbiota was not an expected consequence of this dosing regimen.

Hamouzová et al. evaluated immune system changes in rabbits receiving meloxicam at 1 mg/kg orally every 24 hours for 14 days.18 Changes in lymphocyte subpopulations and activation were identified, including alterations in T- and B-cell activity. However, no significant clinical abnormalities involving food consumption, fecal production, or physical examinations were observed.

The clinical significance of these immunological findings remains uncertain, but they demonstrate that meloxicam may have effects beyond those associated with analgesia and inflammation.

Meloxicam in guinea pigs

Compared with rabbits, considerably less information is available regarding appropriate meloxicam dosing in guinea pigs.

Moeremans et al. evaluated the pharmacokinetics and oral bioavailability of meloxicam in six healthy male Dunkin Hartley guinea pigs.3 A single dose of 1.5 mg/kg was administered orally and intravenously in a crossover design.

Following oral administration, the mean peak plasma concentration was 0.92 µg/mL, occurring approximately 3.7 hours after dosing. The elimination half-life was approximately 3.5 to 3.7 hours, considerably shorter than reported in rabbits.

Oral bioavailability was approximately 54%, although substantial individual variation was identified, ranging from 25% to 77%.

Based on pharmacokinetic modeling, the authors proposed a dose of 1.5 mg/kg orally every 12 hours. However, this recommendation was based on predicted plasma concentrations rather than demonstrated analgesic efficacy. No repeated-dose safety evaluation was performed.

A 2025 review by Evans and Benato discussed these findings in relation to currently licensed meloxicam doses in the United Kingdom, which begin at 0.2 mg/kg and decrease to 0.1 mg/kg every 24 hours, with increases up to 0.5 mg/kg on an individual basis.19 The authors suggested that these lower doses may be insufficient based on the available pharmacokinetic data. They also advised caution in guinea pigs with renal disease.

Sadar and Mans similarly noted that the relatively short elimination half-life in guinea pigs may support administration more frequently than every 24 hours, although additional safety and efficacy studies are necessary.2

Ellen et al. evaluated postoperative pain behaviors in 16 male guinea pigs following orchiectomy.20 Animals receiving meloxicam at 0.2 mg/kg subcutaneously in combination with local anesthetics demonstrated lower composite pain behavior scores than untreated controls.

Because local anesthesia was included in the treatment protocol, the degree of analgesia attributable specifically to meloxicam cannot be determined. However, the study also demonstrated how subtle pain-associated behaviors can be in guinea pigs.

How I use meloxicam in clinical practice

Rabbits

For routine perioperative and postoperative analgesia in rabbits, I generally administer meloxicam at 1 mg/kg every 24 hours as part of a multimodal analgesic protocol.

The pharmacokinetic studies by Fredholm and Delk, along with the clinical studies evaluating higher doses, provide support for this approach. However, I recognize that much of the available information comes from relatively small populations of healthy laboratory rabbits.

In selected patients, I will administer meloxicam at 1 mg/kg every 12 hours, particularly when I am concerned that once-daily dosing may not provide adequate duration of effect. This decision is based in part on the elimination half-life and the COX inhibition data reported by Sarvi et al. This more frequent dosing interval has not been established as safe or effective through controlled repeated-dose clinical studies.

I generally prefer to begin NSAID administration following surgery, once the patient is normothermic and cardiovascular parameters, particularly blood pressure, are stable. My primary concern is the potential for renal injury when NSAIDs are administered to patients experiencing anesthetic hypotension or inadequate renal perfusion.

I am also more cautious in rabbits with preexisting renal disease, dehydration, or other conditions that may compromise renal perfusion. However, renal disease is not necessarily an absolute contraindication to NSAID administration. Similar to our approach in cats with chronic kidney disease, there are situations in which the benefits of analgesia may justify careful administration with appropriate monitoring.

For rabbits receiving prolonged treatment, I recommend periodic evaluation of hydration, appetite, body weight, and renal parameters, with monitoring tailored to the patient's underlying disease and overall clinical condition.

Guinea pigs

In guinea pigs, I routinely use meloxicam at 1.5 mg/kg orally every 12 hours.

This protocol is based primarily on the pharmacokinetic study by Moeremans et al. and the relatively short elimination half-life identified in guinea pigs. However, this dosing regimen has not been validated in controlled clinical analgesic or repeated-dose safety studies.

I closely monitor appetite, fecal production, body weight, and hydration, particularly when treatment is continued beyond the immediate postoperative period. If a guinea pig develops hyporexia, I will generally discontinue meloxicam while evaluating for other potential causes.

For patients requiring prolonged NSAID treatment, I also recommend periodic serum biochemical testing, particularly to assess renal function.

Although the lower doses historically used in guinea pigs may not provide adequate analgesia, increasing the dose should not be interpreted as eliminating the potential for adverse effects. Until additional studies are available, clinical judgment and appropriate patient monitoring remain essential.

Conclusions

The available literature supports the use of higher meloxicam doses in rabbits than were historically recommended based on canine and feline dosing.

Pharmacokinetic studies demonstrate that approximately 1 mg/kg orally is necessary to achieve plasma concentrations comparable to those associated with analgesia in dogs. However, comparable plasma concentrations do not necessarily establish equivalent analgesic efficacy.

The COX inhibition data also suggest that meloxicam may not demonstrate the same preferential COX-2 activity in rabbits as in other species, and that its pharmacodynamic effects may decline substantially before the next dose when administered every 24 hours.

Although several studies have evaluated meloxicam safety in healthy rabbits, the available evidence remains limited, particularly regarding prolonged administration, more frequent dosing, and use in older or systemically ill patients.

In guinea pigs, the available pharmacokinetic data support consideration of higher and more frequent doses than traditionally recommended. Nevertheless, controlled studies evaluating clinical efficacy and safety with repeated administration are still needed.

In both species, I consider meloxicam an important component of multimodal analgesia rather than a replacement for appropriate opioid administration, local and regional anesthesia, or other analgesic techniques when indicated.
