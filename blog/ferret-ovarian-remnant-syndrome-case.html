Ovarian Remnant Syndrome in a Young Desexed Ferret

November 9, 2026

A spayed female ferret with a swollen vulva usually sends my thoughts straight to the adrenal glands. That reflex is reasonable. This case is a reminder that it isn't the only answer, and that the patient's age shouldn't take ovarian remnant syndrome (ORS) off the list.

My co-authors and I published this one in 2025. Here it is the way I'd present it at rounds.

The paper

Kopp L, Baravik-Munsell ED, Clary E. Hormonal, ultrasonographic and histopathological evidence of ovarian remnant syndrome in a young desexed ferret (Mustela putorius furo) in the United States. Vet Rec Case Rep. 2025;13(3):e70103. doi:10.1002/vrc2.70103

Case presentation

The patient was a 5.5-month-old, 0.92 kg spayed female ferret, bought from a pet store that sourced her from a large commercial farm in the United States. She had the farm's two blue dots tattooed on the right pinna, which signals desexing and descenting. Her only prior history was a bout of sneezing at 4 months that resolved.

She presented for sudden, marked vulvar swelling. Heart and respiratory rates were within reference intervals. Everything else on the exam was unremarkable.

Diagnostics

Minimum database and adrenal panel

The CBC, biochemistry panel, and urinalysis were all unremarkable. The adrenal panel (University of Tennessee Endocrinology Lab) was not:

  • Oestradiol 265.6 pmol/L (RI 30 to 180)
  • Androstenedione 6.2 nmol/L (RI <0.1 to 1.5)
  • 17-hydroxyprogesterone 0.33 nmol/L (RI <0.1 to 0.8)

At that point the list was ORS, ovarian neoplasia, or adrenal disease. An adrenal panel can't separate those three, because each disease can shift each hormone in variable ways. It confirms that something is producing hormones. It doesn't tell you what.

hCG response test

We gave human chorionic gonadotropin, 100 IU IM. At the 7-day recheck the vulva looked the same, so we gave a second dose. Within a few days, the family noticed a marked reduction in swelling.

That response is the useful part. Ferrets are induced ovulators, and functional ovarian tissue should respond to hCG. A response points toward an ovarian remnant. No response points toward adrenal disease. A second dose is sometimes needed, given 7 days after the first, and this ferret needed it. If we had stopped after one injection, we would have read this case wrong.

Ultrasound

A board-certified radiologist performed abdominal ultrasound about a month after the hCG response. The scan showed a small, hypoechoic, rounded to amorphous structure immediately caudal to the left kidney, measuring 0.79 × 0.45 × 0.25 cm. It looked like a complete ovary with a partial fallopian tube. Nothing similar was seen on the right. Both adrenal glands were normal in size and echogenicity, and the uterine stump, cervix, and vagina were normal. A few small incidental renal cysts were present.

With a normal-appearing remnant and normal adrenals, ORS moved to the top of the list. Ultrasound has two limits worth keeping in mind. It can't type an ovarian tumor, and a suture granuloma can look like a remnant.

Surgery and outcome

Exploratory laparotomy took place three weeks after the ultrasound. Before isoflurane, PCV and total solids were normal (39%, 6.2 g/dL). Through a ventral midline incision from umbilicus to pubis, the surgeon found a red, multinodular mass of about 5 × 3 × 3 mm in the usual location of the left ovary, attached to a gonadal vascular pedicle with the suspensory ligament at its cranial pole. The right ovary and both uterine horns were absent. The pedicle was ligated and the tissue went to histopathology.

She recovered uneventfully and went home six hours after surgery on buprenorphine and meloxicam.

Histopathology showed complete removal of a normal ovary with active folliculogenesis. No neoplasia. She was cleared for normal activity 14 days after surgery. Six weeks after surgery, the repeat adrenal panel was entirely within reference intervals (oestradiol 132.3 pmol/L, androstenedione 0.5 nmol/L, 17-hydroxyprogesterone <0.1 nmol/L). At a phone follow-up seven months out, she was normal.

Differentials, and why adrenal disease stays on the list

For vulvar swelling in a desexed female ferret, the primary differentials are:

  • Adrenal disease. The signs of ORS can be indistinguishable from adrenal disease, and in ferrets, surgical neutering itself has been associated with adrenal disease. The chronic rise in luteinising hormone after desexing is considered a prerequisite for neoplastic transformation.
  • Ovarian remnant syndrome. In ferrets, surgical error is the most common cause, and residual tissue is often bilateral. That differs from dogs and cats, where the right ovary is the more common culprit.
  • Ovarian neoplasia. Neoplasia is commonly associated with ORS, and hormone values don't reliably separate neoplastic from non-neoplastic remnants. Histopathology is required.

Age deserves a note. In the four previous reports we found, affected ferrets were 1 to 5 years old. This ferret was 5.5 months. Most ferrets in the United States come from a single large commercial farm and are desexed very young, so a remnant can already be there on the day a young ferret goes home.

Why the family chose surgery

In dogs and cats, ovariectomy is the treatment of choice for ORS. Ferrets are less simple, because surgical desexing has been linked to adrenal disease, and many clinicians now advocate chemical castration with deslorelin instead. In the United States that isn't an option. Deslorelin is approved here only for managing adrenal disease, and its extra-label use is prohibited because the drug is indexed rather than fully approved by the FDA. Other ways to suppress ovarian activity, such as repeated hCG, medroxyprogesterone acetate, and proligestone, work for a while and then wear off.

Prolonged oestrus from a remnant is not benign. Hyperoestrogenemia can cause bone marrow suppression and a potentially fatal anaemia. With that risk and no legal long-term chemical option, the family elected ovariectomy.

How I'd use this in practice

Keep ORS on the list regardless of age. A ferret under six months old with a swollen vulva can have a remnant.

Check the blood before you cut. Ferrets with suspected ORS should be screened for anaemia (haematocrit below 25%) and thrombocytopenia (platelets below 20,000). If both are present, give hCG several weeks ahead of surgery so the patient can stabilise. An emergency ovariectomy may need a transfusion.

Give the hCG test a fair trial. One dose of 100 IU IM, then a second at 7 days if nothing changes.

Ultrasound every ORS case. It screens the remnant for overt neoplastic change and checks the adrenals. It doesn't replace histopathology.

Bracket the surgery with hormone panels. A baseline panel alone won't sort out adrenal disease, ORS, and neoplasia. A baseline panel plus a normal repeat after ovariectomy is strong confirmation that you removed the source.

This is one ferret from one clinic. It shows what the combined workup can look like in a young patient, not how often young ferrets carry remnants.
