Can an Opened RHDV2 Vaccine Vial Stay Sterile? Our 16-Day Multidose Study

November 1, 2026

When the Medgene RHDV2 vaccine first reached practices, it came in one presentation: a 10-dose vial labeled for immediate use [1]. If you saw rabbits then, you remember what that meant. We built vaccine clinics around the vial, filled appointments in groups of ten, and hoped nobody canceled.

I wanted to know whether an opened vial, handled carefully and kept refrigerated, would stay free of bacterial and fungal growth long enough to make individual appointments realistic. With my coauthors, and with cultures run by the Bacteriology/Mycology laboratory at the University of Tennessee College of Veterinary Medicine, that question became a short paper in the Journal of Exotic Pet Medicine [1]. Single-dose vials have since changed the picture, and I'll get to that. The data and their limits are still worth walking through.

Why we asked the question

The vaccine is a killed recombinant subunit product targeting the VP60 protein of RHDV2 [1]. It contains two preservatives, gentamicin and thimerosal, which is why we considered it an excellent candidate for extended use [1]. Both preservatives appear, alone or together, in other multidose products, including the European RHDV2 vaccine Eravac and the IMRAB 3 rabies vaccine [1].

The label pushed many practices toward scheduled vaccine clinics, with all the logistics that come with them [1]. We modeled the design on a study of liposomal bupivacaine, a preservative-free single-use vial that stayed sterile for several days when used in a multiple-dose manner [1, 11].

How the study worked

Three vials of the Medgene RHDV2 vaccine were stored at 5°C on a refrigerator shelf, and the metal caps were removed from all vials at the start [1]. One person collected every sample at the same time each day. Before each draw, the vial was gently inverted 10 times, wiped with an alcohol pad and allowed to air dry. Each 0.5 mL sample was drawn with a sterile 25 gauge needle on a 1 mL syringe [1].

The first two vials were punctured daily, 0.5 mL at a time, until 10 samples had been collected from each. The third vial served as the control and was punctured on the last day of the study [1]. Collections ran Monday through Thursday so samples reached the lab promptly, and the study spanned 16 days [1].

After each draw, the needle was changed and the sample was injected into a sterile no-additive tube through an alcohol-swabbed stopper. Samples were shipped overnight on an ice pack to the bacteriology diagnostic laboratory [1]. Aerobic bacterial culture was run on samples from collection days 1 through 10, for 21 cultures, and fungal culture on samples from days 5 and 10, for 5 cultures [1]:

  • Columbia blood agar, about 50 µL of vaccine, 35°C in 6% CO2 for 5 days
  • Thioglycolate broth, about 200 µL, 35°C for 5 days
  • Inhibitory mold agar, about 50 µL, 27°C for 14 days

We calculated 95% confidence intervals using the Newcombe method with a continuity correction [1].

What we found

Nothing grew. None of the samples showed aerobic bacterial growth (0/21, 95% CI 0 to 0.19) or fungal growth (0/5, 95% CI 0 to 0.54) [1].

Those upper bounds deserve as much attention as the zeros. Twenty-one negative cultures still leave room for a true contamination rate as high as 19 percent, and five fungal cultures leave room for 54 percent. Zero growth in a small sample is encouraging. It is not proof.

The preservatives, together with aseptic technique, likely account for what we saw [1]. Our conclusion was that, with appropriate aseptic precautions, the vaccine "could potentially be used extra-label, once fully approved by the USDA, as a multidose vial for at least 16 calendar days without compromising sterility, pending pyrogen and endotoxin testing as well as anaerobic cultures and potentially next generation DNA sequencing" [1].

What the study does not show

  • Small numbers. Two test vials and one control, with a single person collecting every sample under careful aseptic technique [1]. A busy treatment room may not match that.
  • No anaerobic or special media cultures. We left these out because of cost [1].
  • No pyrogen or endotoxin testing [1].
  • Sixteen days. Some clinicians were using the same bottle for 30 days, which is well past what we tested [1].
  • Extra-label use. Using one vial across multiple days is off label, and the veterinarian, not the manufacturer, is responsible for any adverse effects [1].

In the paper we said the vaccine remained "at least partially sterile" for 16 days [1]. I still think that is the right level of confidence.

Where the vaccine stands in 2026

The USDA Center for Veterinary Biologics granted Medgene's vaccine emergency use authorization in 2021 and a conditional license in November 2023 [8]. APHIS lists it as product 474-1R00R0, Rabbit Hemorrhagic Disease Vaccine, Serotype 2, Killed Baculovirus Vector [7]. Medgene's March 2025 announcement still described the rabbit license as conditional [9].

The same announcement introduced single-dose vials, sold in 20-count clamshells [9]. At Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital we now use single-dose vials. For most practices, that solves the scheduling problem that led to this study. The data still apply to anyone working from a multidose vial, and they are a reminder of how much handling matters.

The efficacy and safety evidence in brief

  • First challenge study. None of the vaccinated rabbits (0/9) developed clinical disease or died after oral RHDV2 challenge, while 9/13 (69%) of controls died [3]. The same research group later noted that vaccination does not necessarily prevent infection and shedding [4].
  • Duration of immunity. Rabbits received 0.5 mL subcutaneously on study days 0 and 21 and were challenged about six months later. All vaccinates survived, while 18 of 19 placebo controls died or were euthanized [4]. In four vaccinates followed longer, geometric mean titers fell from 1902 at 7 months to 951 at 12 months, and a single booster at 12 months raised them to 18,101 and 15,221 at 14 and 28 days [4]. Medgene funded this study, and five of the eleven authors list a Medgene affiliation [4].
  • Pet rabbits in practice. In 29 pet rabbits, neither families nor clinicians noted physical abnormalities after vaccination, and median hematology and chemistry values stayed within reference intervals, with a mild BUN increase [5]. Antibody levels rose significantly at day 21 and day 49 (p < 0.0001), but older rabbits responded less (r = -0.56, p = 0.006). A separate cohort tested 11 to 12 months after the first vaccine had antibody levels no different from baseline (p = 0.21) [5].
  • Vaccine failure happens. Four vaccinated pet rabbits in Europe died of RHDV2 [12].

My reading: protection against death is strong, individual antibody responses vary and fade over a year, and that supports keeping every rabbit on an annual booster.

Practical takeaways for practices

  • Use single-dose vials when you can. If you are working from a multidose vial, our data cover careful aseptic draws from refrigerated vials over 16 days and nothing beyond that [1].
  • Schedule. Two doses 21 days apart, then annual boosters. Medgene states rabbits should be fully protected 14 days after the second dose [6].
  • Keep RHDV2 on the list for vaccinated rabbits. Vaccinated rabbits can still become infected, and some have died [4, 12].
  • Biosecurity still counts. Tennessee recommends quarantining newly acquired rabbits for at least 30 days and cleaning surfaces, equipment, shoes, clothes and hands [10].
  • Report suspected cases. All RHD cases must be reported to federal and state animal health officials [2]. In Tennessee, call the State Veterinarian's office at (615) 837-5120 [10].

What comes next

The paper names what a definitive answer would take: repeated studies with more vials and more controls, pyrogen and endotoxin testing, anaerobic and special media cultures, possibly next generation DNA sequencing, and testing beyond 16 days to match how long some clinics keep a bottle in use [1]. Until that work exists, I treat 16 days of careful aseptic use as the outer edge of what our data support.

Discussion questions

  1. If your practice still has a multidose vial, how do you track when it was first punctured and who drew from it?
  2. Which of the gaps in our data (anaerobic cultures, endotoxin testing, longer use periods) matters most to you before using a vial across several days?
  3. Given the waning antibody levels by 12 months, how do you handle a rabbit that is overdue for its booster?
