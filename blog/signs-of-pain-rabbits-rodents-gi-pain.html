Is Your Rabbit, Guinea Pig, or Rat in Pain? How to Tell

November 10, 2026

Rabbits, guinea pigs, rats, and other small pets are prey animals. In the wild, an animal that looks hurt attracts predators, so they've evolved to hide pain very well. By the time a rabbit or guinea pig looks obviously unwell, they've often been uncomfortable for a while.

That's why I want families to know the quieter signs. You see your pet every day, and you're often the first to notice when something is off.

Why it matters

Pain isn't just unpleasant. In rabbits and guinea pigs, pain can slow the gut, and a slow gut causes more pain and less eating. That cycle can become dangerous quickly. Treating pain early helps pets eat, move, and heal.

Watch the face

Researchers have developed "grimace scales" that describe how a rat's or mouse's face changes with pain. Similar changes have been described in rabbits and other species. You don't need a scale at home, but it helps to know what to look for:

  • Eyes: squinting or partly closed, when your pet is awake
  • Ears: folded, pulled back, or angled outward instead of their usual position
  • Whiskers: pulled back against the cheeks or bunched together, instead of fanned out
  • Nose and cheeks: in rats, the nose and cheeks look flatter. In mice, the cheeks may bulge.

Some of these changes can also happen when a pet is sleepy, grooming, or scared, so look for them when your pet is awake and calm.

Watch posture and movement

Common pain signs across rabbits and rodents include:

  • Sitting hunched, with the back rounded, and not wanting to move
  • Grinding teeth (a louder, harsher sound than the soft tooth purr of a content rabbit)
  • Pressing the belly against the floor, stretching out, or arching the back
  • Shifting weight from foot to foot, or not wanting to put weight on a leg
  • Fluffed-up fur
  • Moving slowly, stiffly, or in short, incomplete movements
  • Licking, chewing, or scratching one spot, including an incision

Guinea pigs in pain often sit still with their eyes squinting and their fur fluffed, and they may stop doing normal active things like moving around the cage, rearing up, or eating their droppings.

Chinchillas don't yet have a published pain checklist. Fur chewing is more often linked to stress, but pain is something we check for.

Watch daily habits

Changes in routine are often the first clue:

  • Eating less, eating more slowly, or dropping food
  • Fewer, smaller, or no droppings
  • Hiding more, or less interest in you or cage mates
  • Not grooming, or a messy coat
  • Not building a nest or burrowing like usual, in rats, mice, hamsters, and gerbils
  • Weight loss

Weight is one of the most useful things you can track at home. Researchers studying mice have suggested watching for losses as small as about 5%, well before an animal looks thin. A kitchen scale that weighs in grams makes this easy. Here's how to weigh your small pet at home.

Rats, mice, and hamsters are most active at night, so a pet that seems quiet at noon may be perfectly normal. Pay attention to the times your pet is usually busy.

Belly pain: when it's urgent

Belly pain in rabbits and rodents can be serious. Gut problems such as slowed movement (often called GI stasis), blockages, and bloat are common, and in rabbits and guinea pigs they can quickly lead to more problems, including harmful changes in gut bacteria.

Signs of belly pain include:

  • Rabbits: sitting hunched in a corner, not wanting to move, no interest in food or treats, and few or no droppings. A rabbit with a blockage may be very quiet and barely respond to you.
  • Guinea pigs and chinchillas: a swollen or tight belly, not eating, and few droppings. A guinea pig with a twisted stomach may stand stiffly with the belly lifted off the floor.
  • Rats and mice: an arched back, stretching, or pressing the belly down.

A rabbit or guinea pig that stops eating needs to be seen the same day.

Pain after surgery

After surgery, it can be hard to tell whether your pet is sleepy from anesthesia or uncomfortable. If you aren't sure, call us. I'd much rather check and adjust pain medicine than have your pet go without it. Our article on what to expect when your small pet has anesthesia covers recovery at home.

Please don't give human pain medicine

Pain relievers from your own medicine cabinet can be dangerous for small pets. Acetaminophen, for example, can be toxic to ferrets at any dose. Doses that are safe for people or dogs aren't safe for rabbits and rodents, and some drugs can cause stomach ulcers or kidney damage. Pain medicine for your pet should always come from a veterinarian who knows the species.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Has stopped eating, or has produced no droppings for several hours
  • Sits hunched and won't move, or is barely responsive
  • Has a swollen, tight, or bloated belly
  • Is grinding teeth loudly, or squinting and holding very still
  • Is breathing fast or with an open mouth
  • Won't put weight on a leg, or has had a fall or injury
  • Is chewing at an incision, or the incision is open or bleeding

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Eating a little less or more slowly than usual
  • Gradual weight loss on your home scale
  • Less activity, grooming, nesting, or interest in play
  • Drooling or dropping food, which can point to dental pain
  • A lump, a limp, or sensitivity when touched in one area
