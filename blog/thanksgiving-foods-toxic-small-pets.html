Thanksgiving Foods That Are Dangerous for Rabbits and Small Pets

November 23, 2026

When animal poison control lines get calls about pets other than dogs and cats, rabbits come up again and again. A big part of that is simply how many rabbits live loose in the house. A rabbit with run of the kitchen on Thanksgiving Day has access to a lot of food that was never meant for them.

I've written before about general holiday safety for small pets, including cords, tinsel, and houseguests. This post is narrower. It's about the foods on a typical Thanksgiving table that can actually poison a rabbit, guinea pig, ferret, or rodent, as opposed to just upsetting their stomach.

Avocado and guacamole

This is the one I'd put at the top of the list for rabbit families. Avocado contains a toxin called persin, and every part of the plant carries it: the fruit, skin, pit, and leaves. In rabbits it damages the heart. In one report, laboratory rabbits that got into avocado leaves overnight were found dead the next morning. Others in the same group became weak, stopped eating, and struggled to breathe, and several of those died too.

There's no antidote. Treatment is supportive care for the heart and lungs, and the outlook for a rabbit that's already sick is guarded to poor. So the practical advice is simple. Keep the guacamole bowl, the avocado toast, and any avocado houseplant well out of reach.

Interestingly, rats and mice seem to tolerate avocado far better than rabbits do. I still wouldn't offer it to them.

Onions, garlic, leeks, and chives

Stuffing, green bean casserole, gravy, and most savory sides start with something from the onion family. These plants contain sulfur compounds that damage red blood cells, which can lead to a dangerous anemia (a low red blood cell count). Raw, cooked, dried, and powdered forms all count.

Signs can take a few days to show up, which is why this one is easy to miss. Watch for weakness, fast breathing, pale or yellow gums, reluctance to move, or reddish urine. Severely affected animals may need a blood transfusion.

Chocolate and coffee

Chocolate and caffeine contain stimulants that speed up the heart and nervous system. Most of what we know about chocolate poisoning comes from dogs, and published cases in small mammals are few. That doesn't mean they're safe. Chocolate is one of the most common reasons poison control gets calls about rabbits. Darker chocolate, baking chocolate, and cocoa powder are the most concentrated.

If your pet eats chocolate, call. Don't wait to see if they seem hyper or restless first.

Sugar-free desserts and gum

Xylitol is a sugar substitute in some sugar-free pies, candies, gum, and baked goods. It shows up regularly in poison control calls about small mammals. Keep purses, coats, and bags off the floor when guests arrive, since rabbits and ferrets investigate anything at their level.

Salt and salty snacks

Salt poisoning happens when an animal takes in more sodium than its body can balance, especially if water is limited. It can cause serious neurologic signs such as stumbling, rolling, seizure-like activity, and unresponsiveness. One pet rabbit developed exactly these signs after eating a salt supplement sold as "rabbit-friendly," and that rabbit had water available the whole time.

Chips, pretzels, salted nuts, and ham are not good snacks for any small pet. Rabbits and guinea pigs eating a proper hay-based diet don't need added salt products, and I'd skip them.

The rest of the table

Rolls, pie crust, sweet potatoes, and desserts aren't poisons in the same sense, but they don't belong in a small pet's diet. I covered why in my holiday safety post. Ferrets deserve one extra note. High-carbohydrate diets are thought to raise the risk of insulinoma, a common pancreatic tumor in ferrets, so sweets have no place in their bowl at any time of year.

If your pet gets into something

  • Move your pet away from the food and pick up what's left.
  • Figure out what was eaten and roughly how much. Save the wrapper, the recipe, or a photo of the dish.
  • Note the time.
  • Call us, or an animal poison control center, before you try anything at home.

Please don't try to make your pet vomit. Rabbits, guinea pigs, chinchillas, and other rodents can't vomit, and trying to force it can hurt them. Ferrets can vomit, but even for ferrets it's only safe for certain exposures and should only be done when a veterinarian directs it.

When to call your vet

Call right away if your pet:

  • Ate any avocado, chocolate, coffee grounds, or anything containing xylitol
  • Ate food made with onions, garlic, leeks, or chives
  • Got into salty snacks and seems wobbly, confused, or is twitching
  • Is weak, breathing hard, has pale gums, or has collapsed
  • Stops eating or stops passing droppings

Book a visit soon if you notice:

  • Soft droppings or a smaller appetite for more than a day after a few bites of people food
  • Lower energy in the days after a holiday meal, even if you aren't sure what they ate
