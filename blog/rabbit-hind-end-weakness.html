Why Is My Rabbit Dragging or Weak in the Back Legs?

October 2026

You notice your rabbit scooting instead of hopping, leaving one back leg out to the side, or struggling to get into the litter box. Hind-end weakness is something I see often in rabbits, and it usually has a reason we can work on. The goal of this guide is to help you spot changes early and know when to call, not to alarm you.

Rabbits hide discomfort well. A small change in how they move is worth taking seriously.

What families often notice

Common clues include a bunny that hops less, slides the back feet, sits with the hind end sprawled, has more litter-box accidents, or leaves a messier bottom because grooming the rear has become hard. Some rabbits seem fine in the front and weak only behind. Others slow down overall, eat less hay, or stop using ramps they used every day.

Short phone videos at home help a lot. Many rabbits freeze or hop more normally once they are on the exam table.

Common reasons for hind-end weakness

Several different problems can look similar from across the room.

Pain and arthritis. Sore hips, knees or spine change how a rabbit sits and pushes off. Older rabbits are especially prone to this. Pain also reduces movement, which leads to weight gain and weaker muscles, so the problem can build on itself.

Sore hocks. Pressure sores on the heels hurt. A rabbit may shift weight, hop less, or sit oddly to protect the feet. Soft, grippy flooring often helps these rabbits as much as medicine does.

Extra weight. Extra pounds load the joints and make every hop harder. Obesity is common in pet rabbits and is one of the most useful things we can change together.

Spinal injury. A bad fall, a twist while being handled, or trauma can injure the back. Sudden inability to use the back legs, loss of bladder or bowel control, or crying out needs same-day care.

Encephalitozoon cuniculi (E. cuniculi). This protozoan can affect the nervous system, kidneys or eyes. Some rabbits develop a head tilt, balance trouble or weakness. Others drink and urinate more, or eat less. Not every weak rabbit has E. cuniculi, and a positive blood test does not always mean it is the active cause. Diagnosis needs a full exam and often bloodwork, imaging and a careful history.

Other neurologic disease. Disc disease, toxin exposure and other nerve problems are less common but still part of a thorough workup when the exam points that way.

Often more than one factor is present. An older rabbit may have arthritis and sore hocks and a diet that left them heavier than ideal. Sorting that out is part of the visit.

What a veterinary visit usually includes

I start with how your rabbit is living: flooring, litter-box height, space to move, diet and any recent falls or handling mishaps. Husbandry and nutrition are not separate from medicine. They are medicine.

Then comes a hands-on exam of the spine, hips, knees, feet and abdomen, plus a neurologic check. Depending on the findings, we may recommend bloodwork, urinalysis, radiographs or more advanced imaging. If E. cuniculi or kidney disease is a concern, we talk through what testing can and cannot tell us.

Treatment depends on the cause. Plans often include pain relief, home changes (non-slip floors, lower litter-box entry, food and water close by), weight support, foot care for sore hocks, and medication when an infection or other disease is confirmed. Success means the return of normal behavior: hopping, grooming, using the litter box and eating hay again.

When to call your vet

Call now (same day) if your rabbit suddenly cannot use the back legs, seems unable to feel the feet, is dragging with a floppy hind end, cannot urinate, has a sudden head tilt or rolling, stops eating, or stops producing droppings.

Book a visit soon if hopping is gradually getting weaker, the bottom is messier, litter-box habits are slipping, or your rabbit avoids jumps and ramps they used to manage easily.

Do not wait for a rabbit to become completely unable to move. Early care is kinder and usually gives us more options.
