Waste Gas and the Small Mammal Induction Chamber

November 18, 2026

You lift the lid on an induction box, or a rabbit wriggles half out of a mask, and for a second the room smells like the vaporizer. That moment is easy to treat as part of exotics practice.

A new occupational exposure study in JAVMA measured sevoflurane in the room during those moments.

The paper

Tomasi SE, Lee EG, Kobos L, Harvey RR. Sevoflurane exposure during anesthesia procedures in a veterinary clinic. J Am Vet Med Assoc. 2026 (published online November 11):1-10. doi:10.2460/javma.26.07.0588

What they did

The investigators assessed waste anesthetic gas exposure at a single veterinary hospital that used sevoflurane. They interviewed 13 of the 15 employees (87%) about work practices, personal protective equipment, and health symptoms. Over 2 workdays they collected personal air samples across full shifts and during specific tasks, plus full-shift area samples. They also ran real-time monitoring to catch short peaks that time-weighted samples would average out. Results were compared with established occupational exposure limits.

What they found

The time-weighted averages were low:

  • Full-shift sevoflurane concentrations (19 personal and 13 area samples) ranged from 0.03 to 0.25 ppm.
  • Task-based personal samples (n = 82) ranged from less than 0.03 to 1.10 ppm, with the highest values among veterinarians and assistants during surgical procedures.
  • Every full-shift and task-based personal exposure was below established occupational exposure limits.

The real-time monitoring is where small mammals came in. During small mammal induction by mask or chamber box, concentrations briefly exceeded the instrument's upper limit of 100 ppm. Patient recovery produced readings of 5.6 to 27.9 ppm.

The interviews found variable use of the scavenging system, inconsistent PPE practices, and uneven adherence to protocols. Staff reported headaches, drowsiness, and eye irritation.

The authors concluded that, although averaged exposures were below limits, small mammal induction and recovery are the points where targeted controls would make the most difference. They recommended consistent scavenger use, standardized training, and engineering and administrative controls.

Strengths and limitations

The real-time monitoring is what showed the peaks. A full-shift average of 0.25 ppm would have suggested there was nothing to fix. The peaks only showed up because someone was watching in real time.

The limits are mostly about scope. This was one hospital over 2 workdays, using one inhalant. Hospitals differ in caseload, room size, ventilation, and equipment, so the absolute numbers may not transfer to your building. The real-time instrument topped out at 100 ppm, so we know the induction peaks went above that but not how far. The study measured exposure and asked about symptoms. It wasn't designed to show that a given exposure causes a given symptom, so I would not draw that link from these data.

What it means in practice

Small mammals are where many of us still reach for a mask or a box, because a fractious rabbit or a hamster with no visible vein doesn't leave many easy options. This paper doesn't say those techniques are forbidden. It says they are the moments in the day when the people holding the patient breathe the most anesthetic gas.

There is also a patient side to this, separate from staff exposure. A struggling rabbit in a mask is stressed, and rabbits are well known to breath-hold during inhalant induction. Injectable premedication or induction followed by a secured airway is easier on the rabbit and keeps the gas inside the circuit.

How I'd use this

If your small mammal inductions still run through a mask or chamber, this is what I would look at first:

  1. Premedicate. A sedated patient fights the mask less, needs less inhalant to reach a plane, and leaks less gas into the handler's face.
  2. Consider injectable induction plus an airway (endotracheal tube or a supraglottic device such as the rabbit v-gel) when the patient and procedure allow it.
  3. If you use a mask, fit it well and scavenge it actively. A loose mask on a rodent is a gas leak with a patient attached.
  4. If you use a chamber, scavenge it before you open it. Flush with oxygen into the scavenger rather than lifting the lid on a box full of sevoflurane.
  5. Pay attention to recovery. The 5.6 to 27.9 ppm readings came from patients exhaling inhalant. Keep the patient on the circuit with oxygen as long as is practical after the vaporizer is turned off, and make sure recovery spaces are ventilated.
  6. Write it down. The interviews found the equipment was there but not always used. A short written protocol and training for new staff address that directly.

Questions for rounds

  1. Which small mammal patients in your practice still get chamber inductions, and what would it take to switch them to injectable induction?
  2. Does your scavenging system connect to your induction chamber and masks, or only to the breathing circuit?
  3. How would you monitor your own hospital's exposure if you wanted to know where your peaks are?
