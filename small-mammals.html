Degu diabetes and dental disease

Degus are seen for diabetes, dental disease, and diet-related problems. Cataracts, weight change, more water drinking, or a degu that stops eating hay needs a call. Tail injuries, including degloving, need a call while the hospital is open. Always call first at (615) 361-4646. Diet counseling is part of almost every degu visit. Skull and dental radiographs are taken in the hospital for dental evaluation. Dr. Kopp is experienced in treating these problems.

  • Diabetes mellitus and hyperglycemia evaluation
  • Cataracts
  • Dental disease, malocclusion, and tooth root disease
  • Hypomotility, gastrointestinal stasis, and nutritional disease
  • Obesity and diet counseling
  • Pododermatitis and skin disease
  • Tail degloving and other trauma
  • Respiratory infections
  • Urinary disease and reproductive disease
  • Soft-tissue masses and mass removal
  • Dental procedures
  • Chronic pain, geriatric care, and emergency stabilization

Hamster cheek pouch and dental disease

Call if a cheek pouch looks stuck out, if the hamster is straining at the pouch, or if a lump is growing in the pouch. Diarrhea, especially in a young hamster, can be proliferative ileitis and can dehydrate a hamster quickly. Diabetes is a concern in predisposed dwarf hamsters and is evaluated when the signs fit. Selected surgery is considered when the hamster is a reasonable anesthetic candidate. There is a family overview in health problems in pet rats, mice, hamsters, and gerbils.

  • Cheek pouch impaction, prolapse, and pouch tumors
  • Dental malocclusion
  • Skin disease, mites, and dermatophytosis
  • Diarrhea, proliferative ileitis, and other gastrointestinal disease
  • Dehydration
  • Respiratory disease
  • Diabetes in predisposed dwarf hamsters
  • Other tumors and reproductive disorders
  • Trauma, fractures, and abscesses
  • Geriatric disease, anesthesia, selected surgery, and palliative care

Gerbil scent gland tumors and tail injuries

A lump on the belly midline of a gerbil is often a scent gland tumor and should be seen while it is still small. Tail injuries and tail degloving need prompt care. Seizures, overgrown teeth, and respiratory infections are other common reasons to call. Mass removal is considered for selected gerbils.

  • Scent gland tumors and mass removal
  • Tail injuries and degloving
  • Seizures
  • Dental malocclusion
  • Respiratory infections
  • Gastrointestinal disease, skin disease, and parasitic disease
  • Otitis and reproductive disease
  • Geriatric medicine, anesthesia, pain management, and emergency stabilization

Small mammal anesthesia and emergency care

The hospital provides high-quality anesthesia, with dedicated anesthesia technicians and full monitoring (ECG, SpO2, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature), including for hamsters, gerbils, and degus. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. Some fractures can be repaired in partnership with board-certified veterinary surgeons (DACVS). Others are referred. Call before you drive if the animal is collapsed, bleeding, or has stopped eating. The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM.

  • High-quality anesthesia with dedicated anesthesia technicians and full monitoring
  • Pain management and palliative care
  • Selected soft-tissue surgery and mass removal
  • Some fractures repaired in partnership with board-certified veterinary surgeons (DACVS), and others referred

Dr. Kopp's background

Dr. Logan Kopp grew up in Nashville and earned his veterinary degree at the University of Tennessee. He is board-certified by the American Board of Veterinary Practitioners in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. Those credentials are DABVP (ECM), and he is the only veterinarian currently practicing in Tennessee with that certification. He is also a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). When to call

Call while the hospital is open if your small mammal:

  • Has not eaten since yesterday, or is weak or cold
  • Has diarrhea, a stuck cheek pouch, or a tail injury
  • Has a new lump, a scent gland mass, or a swelling
  • Is drinking and urinating much more, or has cataracts
  • Is having seizures, trouble breathing, or trouble walking

The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Saturday and Sunday are closed. During those hours, Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital can usually accept urgent transfers from primary care veterinarians and may be able to see emergency cases. Always call first at (615) 361-4646. After hours, exotic companion mammal consults go through Veterinary Emergency Group in Franklin, Murfreesboro, and West Nashville. Do not give human pain medicine.
