Small Mammal Veterinarian in Nashville

Dr. Logan Kopp is Nashville's exotic companion mammal specialist. He sees degus, hamsters, gerbils, and other companion mammals at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch, for families across Middle Tennessee.

Which mammals are seen

  • Rabbits
  • Guinea pigs
  • Ferrets
  • Chinchillas
  • Rats and mice

This page covers degus, hamsters, gerbils, and other companion mammals, including foxes and African pygmy squirrels. He is an exotic companion mammal specialist. He is trained in the medicine and surgery of these species. Birds, reptiles, zoo species, livestock, primates, and wildlife are outside the practice.

These animals are small, and they decline quickly. If one has not eaten since yesterday, call while the hospital is open. Bloodwork and radiographs are used when they answer the question. Ultrasound is used when it is the right next test. Dental radiographs are obtained when they are available for the case. CT and MRI are arranged through referral facilities. Emergency care depends on hours and staffing.

Degus

Degus are seen for diabetes, dental disease, and diet-related problems. Cataracts, weight change, more water drinking, or a degu that stops eating hay needs a call. Tail injuries, including degloving, need same-day care while the hospital is open. Diet counseling is part of almost every degu visit. Dr. Kopp is experienced in treating these problems.

  • Diabetes mellitus and hyperglycemia evaluation
  • Cataracts
  • Dental disease, malocclusion, and tooth root disease
  • Hypomotility, gastrointestinal stasis, and nutritional disease
  • Obesity and diet counseling
  • Pododermatitis and skin disease
  • Tail degloving and other trauma
  • Respiratory infections
  • Urinary disease and reproductive disease
  • Soft-tissue masses and mass removal
  • Dental procedures
  • Chronic pain, geriatric care, and emergency stabilization

Hamsters

Call if a cheek pouch looks stuck out, if the hamster is straining at the pouch, or if a lump is growing in the pouch. Diarrhea, especially in a young hamster, can be proliferative ileitis and can dehydrate a hamster quickly. Diabetes is a concern in predisposed dwarf hamsters and is evaluated when the signs fit. Selected surgery is considered when the hamster is a reasonable anesthetic candidate. There is a family overview in health problems in pet rats, mice, hamsters, and gerbils.

  • Cheek pouch impaction, prolapse, and pouch tumors
  • Dental malocclusion
  • Skin disease, mites, and dermatophytosis
  • Diarrhea, proliferative ileitis, and other gastrointestinal disease
  • Dehydration
  • Respiratory disease
  • Diabetes in predisposed dwarf hamsters
  • Other tumors and reproductive disorders
  • Trauma, fractures, and abscesses
  • Geriatric disease, anesthesia, selected surgery, and palliative care

Gerbils

A lump on the belly midline of a gerbil is often a scent gland tumor and should be seen while it is still small. Tail injuries and tail degloving need prompt care. Seizures, overgrown teeth, and respiratory infections are other common reasons to call. Mass removal is considered for selected gerbils.

  • Scent gland tumors and mass removal
  • Tail injuries and degloving
  • Seizures
  • Dental malocclusion
  • Respiratory infections
  • Gastrointestinal disease, skin disease, and parasitic disease
  • Otitis and reproductive disease
  • Geriatric medicine, anesthesia, pain management, and emergency stabilization

Foxes, African pygmy squirrels, and other companion mammals

Anesthesia, pain, and emergencies

Anesthesia for hamsters, gerbils, and degus is planned for a small patient, with warmth, monitoring, and pain control. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. Selected fracture care is considered when it is indicated. Complex bone surgery is arranged with a referral facility when the case needs it. Call before you drive if the animal is collapsed, bleeding, or has stopped eating. The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM.

  • Anesthesia for small patients
  • Pain management and palliative care
  • Selected soft-tissue surgery and mass removal
  • Emergency stabilization when hours and staffing allow it

When to call

Call while the hospital is open if your small mammal:

  • Has not eaten since yesterday, or is weak or cold
  • Has diarrhea, a stuck cheek pouch, or a tail injury
  • Has a new lump, a scent gland mass, or a swelling
  • Is drinking and urinating much more, or has cataracts
  • Is having seizures, trouble breathing, or trouble walking

Saturday and Sunday are closed. Whether an urgent patient can be seen the same day depends on hours and staffing. After hours, exotic companion mammal consults go through Veterinary Emergency Group in Franklin, Murfreesboro, and West Nashville. Do not give human pain medicine.
