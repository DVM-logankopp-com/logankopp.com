Referring an exotic companion mammal

For primary care veterinarians in Nashville and the Middle Tennessee area.

Dr. Logan Kopp, DVM, DABVP (Exotic Companion Mammal Practice), FABVP-CAPM, is Nashville's exotic companion mammal specialist. He is the only veterinarian currently practicing in Tennessee as a Diplomate in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice, and he sees referral cases at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital. That includes rabbits, ferrets, guinea pigs, chinchillas, hedgehogs, sugar gliders, rats, mice, degus, servals, and other companion mammals. Privately owned, legally kept foxes, squirrels, raccoons, skunks, and some other non-domestic mammals can be seen by appointment after the hospital confirms the animal can be booked. Wild animals are not accepted. Those families are directed to Walden's Puddle. Primates and non-mammals, including birds, reptiles, and amphibians, are outside this practice.

After the visit or procedure, your patient comes back to you for ongoing care, along with a full written summary.

How to refer

Send the hospital referral form, call (615) 361-4646, or email info@priestlakevet.com. The hospital is at 2445 Morris Gentry Blvd, Antioch, TN 37013. If the animal is painful, bloated, or shocky, call before transfer instead of finishing the workup.

During hospital hours, Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital can usually accept urgent transfers from primary care veterinarians and may be able to see emergency cases. Always call first at (615) 361-4646. After hours, exotic companion mammal consults go through Veterinary Emergency Group in Franklin, Murfreesboro, and West Nashville. Dr. Kopp consults with those teams.

What to send

Please send:

  • History: timeline, diet, last meal, fecal output, and current weight
  • Medications already given, with doses and times
  • Prior diagnostics and imaging, including radiographs or a CT if you already have them
  • Laboratory results

Common reasons to refer

  • Dental disease (see referring dental cases)
  • Gastrointestinal stasis, especially a rabbit that has stopped eating (see referring GI stasis cases)
  • Surgery, including spay and neuter and mass removal
  • Ferret adrenal disease and insulinoma
  • Urinary disease
  • Imaging, including CT
  • Anesthesia for a high-risk patient
  • Pain that is hard to recognize or hard to control
