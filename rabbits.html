Care for pet rabbits

Rabbits are quiet about pain. A smaller pile of hay, fewer droppings, or a rabbit pressed into a corner can be the first sign of illness. Their teeth grow throughout life, and the gut is built to keep moving on hay. When eating slows, the gut slows with it.

Dr. Kopp is board-certified in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. He sees primary care and referral cases at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch. That includes dwarf, lop, Rex, and giant rabbits, and rabbits that are young, old, or already medically complicated.

He is the only Gold-level rabbit-friendly veterinarian certified by the Rabbit Welfare Association and Fund in North America. At this hospital that means a separate waiting area, housing away from dogs and cats, and staff trained to handle rabbits. Stress slows the gut and makes pain harder to sort out.

The plan is chosen for the individual rabbit. Evidence for many treatments in exotic companion mammals is limited, so the options are discussed with you before treatment starts.

Preventive care

A wellness visit is a weight check, a dental look, and a review of hay, flooring, and the small changes you see at home. Older rabbits that have gotten picky or stiff belong on the schedule too. RHDV2 vaccination is available when a rabbit should have it. There is a longer note on annual exams for exotic pets.

  • Physical and species-specific wellness exams
  • New-rabbit, juvenile, and geriatric exams
  • Preventive bloodwork, weight, and body condition
  • Nutrition evaluation and dental exams
  • Skin, muscle and joint, and heart and lung assessment
  • Reproductive screening
  • RHDV2 vaccine when appropriate
  • Parasite screening, treatment, and prevention
  • Counseling about infections that can pass between rabbits and people
  • Preanesthetic assessment
  • Behavior, environment, enclosure, enrichment, and social housing
  • Senior quality of life assessment, and teaching early signs of illness

How problems are investigated

Testing follows the question. Bloodwork, urine tests, cytology, cultures, and fecal tests are used often. Radiographs are part of many workups. Dental radiographs are obtained when they are available for that case. Ultrasound is used when it answers a question the radiographs do not, including a focused look at the belly or chest in an emergency. Heart ultrasound is arranged when a heart problem needs it. CT and MRI are arranged through referral facilities. When the roots or the skull need more detail, CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists. What a CT visit involves is written for families.

  • Complete blood count, chemistry, electrolytes, and glucose
  • Urinalysis, sediment, and culture
  • Bacterial and fungal cultures, cytology, fine-needle aspirates, and biopsy
  • Skin scrapings and dermatophyte testing
  • Fecal and parasite testing, and species-specific infectious disease tests
  • Radiographs, and dental radiographs when available for the case
  • Ultrasound, including emergency abdominal or thoracic ultrasound when indicated
  • Echocardiography by appropriate arrangement
  • CT and MRI through referral facilities
  • Workups for weight loss, chronic anorexia, recurrent respiratory disease, chronic gastrointestinal disease, anemia, abnormal liver or kidney values, neurologic signs, and suspected cancer

Emergency and critical care

A rabbit that is cold, weak, breathing hard, or in severe pain needs a call before you drive, while the hospital is open. Emergency care depends on the hours, the staff on duty, and what the case needs. The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Care can include warming or cooling, oxygen, fluids, pain control, and surgery when an operation is required and the team can do it safely that day. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating shock and sepsis in rabbits, including fluid support and medicine to support blood pressure when it is indicated.

  • Hypovolemic and septic shock, fluid resuscitation, and blood pressure support when indicated
  • Intravenous and intraosseous access, and central access when appropriate
  • Constant-rate infusions when indicated
  • Dehydration, electrolyte problems, and low or high blood sugar
  • Low or high body temperature, oxygen, respiratory distress, and airway support
  • Severe pain, acute abdomen, gastrointestinal obstruction, and gastric dilation-volvulus
  • Urinary obstruction, seizures, trauma, hemorrhage, and anemia

  • Emergency surgery when hours and staffing allow it
  • Postoperative monitoring, assisted feeding, feeding tubes when indicated, and serial rechecks

Gastrointestinal disease

Call the same day if your rabbit stops eating, makes smaller or fewer droppings, or sits hunched. Many of these rabbits have gastrointestinal stasis and can be treated and go home. Some have a blockage, a twisted liver lobe, gastric dilation-volvulus, or another abdominal emergency, and those rabbits need imaging and sometimes surgery. Do not give human pain medicine. The family guide to GI stasis warning signs is a useful start if the hay has not been touched.

  • Gastrointestinal stasis and hypomotility
  • Acute anorexia
  • Cecal dysbiosis and cecal impaction
  • Gastric dilation and gastric outflow obstruction
  • Small intestinal obstruction and foreign bodies
  • Gastric dilation-volvulus (GDV)
  • Liver lobe torsion and appendectomy when those are the diagnosis
  • Ulceration, diarrhea, and abnormal cecotroph production or consumption
  • Hepatic lipidosis and gastrointestinal pain
  • Chronic gastrointestinal dysfunction and nutrition plans
  • Emergency decompression and surgery for gastrointestinal emergencies

Dental disease and oral surgery

Incisors are the part you can see. Cheek teeth and roots hide, and an awake look often misses the reason for drooling, a wet chin, watery eyes, or a rabbit that picks at pellets and leaves the hay. A dental visit often includes an anesthetized exam. Treatment can include crown reduction and extraction of diseased teeth. When the roots or the skull need a closer look, CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists. Read about dental disease in pet rabbits.

  • Incisor and cheek tooth malocclusion
  • Acquired dental disease, spurs, and oral ulcers
  • Root elongation and tooth root disease
  • Odontogenic and facial abscesses
  • Mandibular and maxillary osteomyelitis
  • Dental epiphora and nasolacrimal disease
  • Crown reduction, incisor reduction, and incisor extraction
  • Cheek tooth extraction and abscess surgery
  • Oral trauma
  • Dental imaging, long-term monitoring, nutrition, and dental pain control

Urinary disease

Thick, chalky urine, straining, blood, or urine stuck to the fur is a reason to call. Sludge and stones hurt, and a rabbit that cannot urinate needs to be seen while the hospital is open. There is a practical note on sludgy urine.

  • Urine sludge and uroliths
  • Cystitis and urinary tract infection
  • Pyelonephritis and other kidney disease
  • Ureteral obstruction
  • Incontinence and urine scald
  • Bladder surgery and urinary imaging

Infectious disease and prevention

Respiratory noise, a head tilt, abscesses, or a rabbit that becomes suddenly weak can be infectious disease, and some of those rabbits are septic. Call the same day if your rabbit is collapsed, breathing hard, or not eating. RHDV2 vaccination is part of prevention for rabbits that should have it. Some antibiotics that are used in dogs and cats are unsafe in rabbits. Do not give a leftover antibiotic. Call first.

  • RHDV2 vaccination and prevention
  • Encephalitozoon cuniculi (E. cuniculi)
  • Pasteurellosis and other respiratory infections
  • Otitis media and otitis interna
  • Abscesses, sepsis, and septic shock
  • Bacterial enteritis and antibiotic-associated enterotoxemia
  • Dermatophytosis (ringworm)
  • Parasites, including mites, and myiasis
  • Osteomyelitis

Reproductive disease and spay and neuter

Rabbit spays and neuters are planned as rabbit procedures, with warming, monitoring, and a plan for pain and eating afterward. Unspayed females have a real risk of uterine disease as they age, including uterine adenocarcinoma. A doe that is bleeding, straining, or suddenly weak needs a call the same day. The spay and neuter guide walks through why families book this in Nashville.

  • Spay (ovariectomy or ovariohysterectomy) and neuter
  • Cryptorchid surgery
  • Uterine adenocarcinoma, hyperplasia, and uterine infection
  • Ovarian disease and testicular disease
  • Mammary disease
  • Reproductive emergencies

Cancer care

A lump, a mammary change, or weight loss with an unclear cause needs a workup before anyone can say what it is. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating rabbit cancer, including thymoma, lymphoma, uterine adenocarcinoma, osteosarcoma, and soft-tissue tumors. Surgery is often the treatment. For some rabbits, selected chemotherapy is planned when it is appropriate, including a tyrosine kinase inhibitor when that drug fits the case. Not every chemotherapy drug is used at the hospital. Radiation therapy is arranged through a referral facility when it is indicated. Comfort and quality of life stay part of the plan.

  • Mass workup, cytology, fine-needle aspirate, biopsy, and histopathology
  • Staging, thoracic and abdominal imaging, and evaluation for spread
  • Thymoma, lymphoma, osteosarcoma, uterine adenocarcinoma, and soft-tissue tumors
  • Surgical removal, including amputation for selected tumors
  • Chemotherapy planning and selected systemic chemotherapy when appropriate
  • Tyrosine kinase inhibitors when appropriate
  • Adverse effect monitoring, serial bloodwork, and follow-up imaging
  • Palliative care, cancer pain, quality of life, and end of life planning
  • Radiation therapy through a referral facility when indicated

Anesthesia and pain management

Rabbits grind their teeth, stop grooming, or sit still. They rarely cry out. Pain control is part of dental disease, urinary disease, surgery, cancer, and arthritis in older rabbits. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. Anesthesia is planned for the individual rabbit, with monitoring, warmth, and food again as soon as it is safe. Recognizing pain and chronic pain in older rabbits cover what to watch for at home.

  • Anesthetic planning and risk evaluation, including geriatric, critical, and heart or lung patients
  • Sedation for diagnostics and imaging
  • Injectable and inhalant anesthesia
  • Intubation when feasible, and alternative airways
  • Monitoring of ECG, oxygen saturation, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature
  • Intraoperative fluids and multimodal pain control
  • Local and regional blocks, opioids, anti-inflammatory medicine, and other adjuncts
  • Analgesic constant-rate infusions when indicated
  • Acute and chronic pain assessment, including osteoarthritis, cancer pain, neuropathic pain, and postoperative pain
  • Pain recognition in a prey species, palliative pain care, and long-term monitoring

Other conditions

Sore heels, a head tilt, a cloudy eye, or an older rabbit that is slowing down still needs a species-specific workup. Bald, red, or sore hocks usually have a story behind them: flooring, weight, or sore joints. Treating the feet and changing the setup go together. See sore hocks, flooring, and weight. Eye surgery that is beyond a medical corneal ulcer is referred when the rabbit needs it. Heart ultrasound is arranged when heart disease needs that test.

  • Pododermatitis (sore hocks)
  • Osteoarthritis, chronic musculoskeletal pain, spinal injury, and fractures
  • Head tilt, vestibular disease, otitis, and facial nerve dysfunction
  • Corneal ulcers and nasolacrimal disease
  • Respiratory disease and heart disease
  • Chronic anemia, geriatric multisystem disease, and palliative care
  • Ringworm and mites, covered in the ringworm and mite guides

What a visit is like

Bring the rabbit in a secure carrier. A solid side, or a towel over a wire door, keeps the trip quieter. A little of the hay they already eat is useful, both for comfort and so the hospital can see what dinner looks like at home.

At the hospital, rabbits wait away from dogs and cats. Handling stays low-stress. Hides, familiar food, and a quiet recovery spot matter as much as the medicine. A rabbit that has stopped eating is triaged as soon as the team can see them.

If your veterinarian is sending the rabbit, ask them to send the history, medications, and any lab work or images ahead. Veterinarians can see the referral information for veterinarians, including referring GI stasis cases and referring dental cases.

When to call

Call the clinic while it is open if your rabbit:

  • Has eaten little or nothing for several hours, or the droppings have shrunk or stopped
  • Is hunched, grinding teeth, or hiding when they usually greet you
  • Is drooling, has a wet chin, or is dropping food
  • Is straining to urinate, or has thick urine caked on the fur
  • Is cold, weak, breathing hard, or lying on their side

The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Saturday and Sunday are closed. Whether an urgent rabbit can be seen the same day depends on hours and staffing. Call Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital at (615) 361-4646 and ask for Dr. Logan Kopp. The hospital is at 2445 Morris Gentry Blvd, Nashville, TN 37013, in Antioch. He is the rabbit vet there.

My rabbit stopped eating. Should I wait?

Call the same day. A rabbit that stops eating, makes fewer droppings, or sits hunched may have GI stasis, and sometimes a blockage or another emergency that looks similar at first. Skip human pain medicine.

Do you spay and neuter rabbits?

Yes. Rabbit spays and neuters are planned around rabbit anesthesia and pain control. Many families book this to lower the chance of uterine disease and to prevent litters.

Can you see rabbit dental disease?

Yes. Cheek teeth are difficult to judge in an awake rabbit, so a dental visit often includes an anesthetized exam and extractions when a tooth is diseased. When the roots or the skull need a closer look, CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists.
