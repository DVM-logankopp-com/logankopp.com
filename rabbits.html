Why rabbits need a rabbit-savvy vet

Rabbits are quiet about pain. A smaller pile of hay, fewer droppings, or a bunny pressed into a corner can be the first sign that something hurts. Their teeth never stop growing, and the gut is built to keep moving on hay. When eating slows, the gut slows with it.

A veterinarian who sees rabbits every week knows what a comfortable rabbit looks like at rest, and which small change should move a rabbit to the front of the day. Dr. Kopp is that veterinarian for Nashville and Antioch. Rabbit visits with him are a regular part of the week at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital, in a hospital set up for them.

Gold-level rabbit-friendly care, which this hospital meets, means a separate waiting area, housing away from dogs and cats, and staff trained to handle rabbits. Stress is a medical problem in this species. It slows the gut and makes pain harder to sort out.

Care rabbits come in for

Wellness exams

A yearly visit is a weight check, a look at the teeth and the bottom, and a conversation about hay, flooring, and the small habits you notice at home. RHDV2 vaccination is available for rabbits that should have it. An older rabbit who has gotten picky or stiff is worth the same kind of visit. There is a longer note on annual exams for exotic pets.

Spay and neuter

Rabbit spays and neuters are planned as rabbit procedures, with warming, monitoring, and a plan for pain and eating afterward. Unspayed females carry a real risk of uterine disease as they age. The spay and neuter guide walks through why families book this in Nashville.

Dental care and skull CT

Incisors are the easy part. Cheek teeth and roots hide, and an awake look often misses the problem behind drooling, a wet chin, or a rabbit who picks at pellets and leaves the hay. Dental treatment can include shaping and extractions. When the skull needs more detail, CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists. Read about dental disease in pet rabbits and what a CT visit involves.

GI stasis and emergencies

A rabbit who stops eating is seen the same day while the clinic is open. Many of these cases are gastrointestinal stasis and can be treated and sent home. A few are blockages, a twisted liver lobe, or an inflamed appendix, and those rabbits need surgery. The family guide to GI stasis warning signs is the place to start if the hay pile is untouched tonight.

Urinary sludge and stones

Thick, chalky urine, straining, or urine stuck to the fur is a reason to call. Sludge and stones are common, and they hurt. There is a practical note on sludgy urine.

Sore hocks

Bald, red, or sore heels usually have a story behind them: flooring, weight, or sore joints. Treating the feet and changing the setup go together. See sore hocks, flooring, and weight.

Pain

Rabbits grind their teeth, stop grooming, or sit still. They rarely cry. Pain is part of the plan on dental disease, urinary disease, surgery, and older rabbits with arthritis. Recognizing pain and chronic pain in older rabbits cover what to watch for at home.

Anesthesia and surgery

Surgery includes spay and neuter, dental extractions, abscesses, and abdominal procedures such as appendectomy, obstruction, and liver lobe torsion, along with some nasal and ear surgeries. Anesthesia is built for a rabbit: monitoring, warmth, pain control, and food again as soon as it is safe. If a procedure is coming up, preparing for surgery and anesthesia is written for families.

What a visit is like

Bring the rabbit in a secure carrier. A solid side, or a towel over a wire door, keeps the trip quieter. A little of the hay they already eat is useful, both for comfort and so we can see what dinner looks like at home.

At the hospital, rabbits wait away from dogs and cats. Handling stays low-stress: gentle, with a firm hold only when a procedure needs it. Hides, familiar food, and a quiet recovery spot matter as much as the medicine. A rabbit who has stopped eating is not left in the lobby until a later slot.

If your veterinarian is sending the rabbit, ask them to send the history, medications, and any lab work or images ahead. See the referral page, including referring GI stasis cases and referring dental cases.

When to call

Call the clinic now if your rabbit:

  • Has eaten little or nothing for several hours, or the droppings have shrunk or stopped
  • Is hunched, grinding teeth, or hiding when they usually greet you
  • Is drooling, has a wet chin, or is dropping food
  • Is straining to urinate, or has thick urine caked on the fur
  • Is cold, weak, or lying on their side

Questions people ask

Where do I take a rabbit in Nashville?

My rabbit stopped eating. Should I wait?

Call the same day. A rabbit that stops eating, makes fewer droppings, or sits hunched may have GI stasis, and sometimes a blockage or another emergency that looks similar at first. Skip human pain medicine.

Do you spay and neuter rabbits?

Yes. Rabbit spays and neuters are planned around rabbit anesthesia and pain control. Many families book this to lower the chance of uterine disease and to prevent litters.

Can you see rabbit dental disease?

Yes. Cheek teeth are difficult to judge in an awake rabbit, so a dental visit often includes an anesthetized exam and extractions when a tooth is diseased. When the roots or the skull need a closer look, CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists.

Do you see rabbits from outside Nashville?

Yes. Most rabbits come from Nashville and Antioch. Families also come from Franklin, Brentwood, Murfreesboro, Mount Juliet, and Smyrna, and from across the Middle Tennessee area.
