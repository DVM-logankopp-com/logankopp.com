Rabbit veterinary care in Nashville

Rabbits are quiet about pain. A smaller pile of hay, fewer droppings, or a rabbit pressed into a corner can be the first sign of illness. Their teeth grow throughout life, and the gut is built to keep moving on hay. When eating slows, the gut slows with it.

Dr. Kopp is board-certified in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. He sees primary care and referral cases at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch. That includes dwarf, lop, Rex, and giant rabbits, and rabbits that are young, old, or already medically complicated.

He is the only Gold-level rabbit-friendly veterinarian certified by the Rabbit Welfare Association and Fund in North America. At this hospital that means a separate waiting area, housing away from dogs and cats, and staff trained to handle rabbits. Stress slows the gut and makes pain harder to sort out.

The plan is chosen for the individual rabbit. Evidence for many treatments in exotic companion mammals is limited, so the options are discussed with you before treatment starts.

Rabbit GI stasis and intestinal blockage treatment

Call while the hospital is open if your rabbit stops eating, makes smaller or fewer droppings, or sits hunched. Many of these rabbits have gastrointestinal stasis and can be treated and go home. Some have a blockage, a twisted liver lobe, gastric dilation-volvulus, or another abdominal emergency, and those rabbits need imaging and sometimes surgery. Do not give human pain medicine. The family guide to GI stasis warning signs is a useful start if the hay has not been touched.

  • Gastrointestinal stasis and hypomotility
  • Acute anorexia
  • Cecal dysbiosis and cecal impaction
  • Gastric dilation and gastric outflow obstruction
  • Small intestinal obstruction and foreign bodies
  • Gastric dilation-volvulus (GDV)
  • Liver lobe torsion and appendectomy when those are the diagnosis
  • Ulceration, diarrhea, and abnormal cecotroph production or consumption
  • Hepatic lipidosis and gastrointestinal pain
  • Chronic gastrointestinal dysfunction and nutrition plans
  • Emergency decompression and surgery for gastrointestinal emergencies

Rabbit dental disease and oral surgery

Incisors are the part you can see. Cheek teeth and roots hide, and an awake look often misses the reason for drooling, a wet chin, watery eyes, or a rabbit that picks at pellets and leaves the hay. Treatment can include crown reduction and extraction of diseased teeth. When the roots or the skull need a closer look, CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists. Rabbit infectious disease and RHDV2 prevention

Respiratory noise, a head tilt, abscesses, or a rabbit that becomes suddenly weak can be infectious disease, and some of those rabbits are septic. Call while the hospital is open if your rabbit is collapsed, breathing hard, or not eating. RHDV2 vaccination is part of prevention for rabbits that should have it. Some antibiotics that are used in dogs and cats are unsafe in rabbits. Do not give a leftover antibiotic. Call first.

  • RHDV2 vaccination and prevention
  • Encephalitozoon cuniculi (E. cuniculi)
  • Pasteurellosis and other respiratory infections
  • Otitis media and otitis interna
  • Abscesses, sepsis, and septic shock
  • Bacterial enteritis and antibiotic-associated enterotoxemia
  • Dermatophytosis (ringworm)
  • Parasites, including mites, and myiasis
  • Osteomyelitis

Rabbit anesthesia and pain management

Rabbits grind their teeth, stop grooming, or sit still. They rarely cry out. Pain control is part of dental disease, urinary disease, surgery, cancer, and arthritis in older rabbits. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. The hospital provides high-quality anesthesia, with dedicated anesthesia technicians and full monitoring (ECG, SpO2, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature), plus warmth and food again as soon as it is safe. Recognizing pain and chronic pain in older rabbits cover what to watch for at home.

  • Full monitoring: ECG, SpO2, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature, with dedicated anesthesia technicians

  • Local and regional blocks, opioids, anti-inflammatory medicine, and other adjuncts

What a visit is like

Bring the rabbit in a secure carrier. A solid side, or a towel over a wire door, keeps the trip quieter. A little of the hay they already eat is useful, both for comfort and so the hospital can see what dinner looks like at home.

At the hospital, rabbits wait away from dogs and cats. Handling stays low-stress. Hides, familiar food, and a quiet recovery spot matter as much as the medicine. A rabbit that has stopped eating is triaged as soon as the team can see them.

If your veterinarian is sending the rabbit, ask them to send the history, medications, and any lab work or images ahead. Veterinarians can see the referral information for veterinarians, including referring a rabbit GI stasis case and referring rabbit dental disease.

When to call

Call the clinic while it is open if your rabbit:

  • Has eaten little or nothing for several hours, or the droppings have shrunk or stopped
  • Is hunched, grinding teeth, or hiding when they usually greet you
  • Is drooling, has a wet chin, or is dropping food
  • Is straining to urinate, or has thick urine caked on the fur
