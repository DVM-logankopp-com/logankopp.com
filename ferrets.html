Why ferrets need a ferret vet

Ferrets get a short list of diseases that do not look like dog or cat problems. A thinning tail, an itchy back, a swollen vulva, or a few seconds of staring into space can be the start of adrenal disease or insulinoma. A young ferret who vomits, or who suddenly sleeps through playtime, may have swallowed a piece of rubber or foam.

Not many clinics in the Middle Tennessee area see ferrets often enough to know those patterns cold. Dr. Kopp does. He sees ferrets at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch. Ferret medicine and surgery with him are part of ordinary clinic days, and referring veterinarians are welcome to send cases.

Problems ferrets are seen for

Wellness exams

Middle age is when adrenal disease and insulinoma become common, and some ferrets develop both. A yearly exam is the easiest time to catch hair loss, a change in weight, or a spell the family almost dismissed. The family guide to adrenal disease and insulinoma is worth reading before that visit.

Adrenal disease

Hair loss often starts at the tail and moves over the hips and back. Itch is common. Females may show a swollen vulva, and some ferrets get a stronger odor or a return of mating behavior. Males need a closer eye: an enlarged prostate can make it hard to urinate, and a ferret who strains and produces little or no urine should be seen the same day.

Treatment depends on the ferret. A hormone implant can quiet the signs and has to be repeated over time. For some ferrets, surgery to remove an affected adrenal gland is the better path. Dr. Kopp performs that surgery here.

Insulinoma

Insulinoma is a pancreatic tumor that releases too much insulin and drops the blood sugar. At home it looks like staring, a spaced-out moment, wobbly hind legs, drooling, or pawing at the mouth. Severe episodes become collapse or a seizure. The spells often follow exercise or a long gap without food.

A portable glucometer is tempting because it is fast. Our study, published in the American Journal of Veterinary Research, found that those meters do not reliably place a ferret on the correct side of the low-blood-sugar cutoff. A low or borderline reading is confirmed on a laboratory analyzer before a diagnosis or a change in plan. The write-up of our study is written for veterinarians. If you want the day-to-day picture, you can stay with the adrenal and insulinoma guide.

Swallowed objects

Rubber toys, foam, earplugs, and anything soft and chewy are the usual finds, especially in ferrets under a couple of years old. Eating less, grinding teeth, vomiting, or a black, tarry stool can mean a blockage, and rubber often hides on an X-ray. Surgery is the usual answer when something is stuck. Ferret-proofing and swallowed objects covers what to look for at home.

Anesthesia and surgery

Adrenal surgery and foreign-body surgery are the operations families ask about most. Anesthesia is planned for a ferret, with monitoring, warmth, and pain control, and with food again as soon as it is safe. If a procedure is already on the calendar, the note on preparing an exotic pet for surgery is a useful read. Older ferrets who have slowed down may also be painful. Chronic pain in older small pets includes ferrets.

What a visit is like

A secure carrier and a familiar blanket make the drive easier. Ferrets are escape artists, so a latch that actually latches matters more than a pretty carrier. At the hospital the exam is unhurried: weight, skin, and, when the history fits, blood sugar and a look at whether a male can urinate comfortably.

Handling stays calm. A ferret who is already weak does not need a wrestling match in the exam room. If your ferret is collapsed, call before you put them in the car so the team knows you are coming.

Referring veterinarians can send the history, current medications, and any bloodwork or imaging ahead. Details are on the referral page. The clinical note on the anorectic ferret is written for veterinarians sorting out a ferret who has stopped eating. After the visit, ongoing care can stay with the clinic that already knows the ferret.

When to call

Call now if your ferret:

  • Collapses, has a seizure, or cannot be woken
  • Strains to urinate and passes little or no urine
  • Has stopped eating, is vomiting, or has black, tarry stool
  • Has repeated staring spells, hind-end weakness, or drooling
  • Is losing hair, itching, or has a swollen vulva

For a weak or collapsed ferret, call before you drive. The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. After hours, exotic companion mammal consults go through Veterinary Emergency Group in Franklin, Murfreesboro, and West Nashville.

Questions people ask

Is there a ferret vet in Nashville, TN?

Yes. Dr. Logan Kopp sees ferrets at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital, 2445 Morris Gentry Blvd, Nashville, TN 37013. Call (615) 361-4646. He is Nashville's ferret specialist.

What does ferret adrenal disease look like?

Hair loss often starts on the tail and spreads over the back. Many ferrets itch. A spayed female may develop a swollen vulva. A male who strains and passes little or no urine needs to be seen right away.

What if my ferret stares into space or seems weak?

Those spells can be insulinoma, a pancreatic tumor that drops blood sugar. Signs often come and go, especially after play or a long stretch without food. Call, and if your ferret is collapsed or having a seizure, call before you drive.

What if my ferret swallows something?

Rubber, foam, and soft toys are common. A ferret who stops eating, vomits, grinds their teeth, or seems suddenly quiet may have a blockage. Call the same day. Young ferrets do this most often.

Can my veterinarian refer a ferret?
