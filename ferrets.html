Ferret veterinary care in Nashville

Ferrets get a short list of diseases that show up in middle age and later. A thinning tail, an itchy back, a swollen vulva, or a few seconds of staring into space can be the start of adrenal disease or insulinoma. A young ferret that vomits, or that suddenly sleeps through playtime, may have swallowed a piece of rubber or foam.

Dr. Kopp is an exotic companion mammal specialist with experience treating ferret endocrine disease, and he sees young, adult, and older ferrets at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch. Other veterinarians send ferrets to him too. Not every ferret treatment is equally well studied, so we'll go over the choices with you before we start.

Ferret adrenal disease and insulinoma treatment

These two diseases are why many ferrets in Nashville and Middle Tennessee are referred, and some ferrets develop both. A yearly exam is a good time to catch hair loss, a change in weight, or a spell the family almost dismissed. Our family guide to ferret adrenal disease and insulinoma is worth reading before that visit.

Insulinoma

Insulinoma is a pancreatic tumor that releases too much insulin and drops the blood sugar. At home it looks like staring, a spaced-out moment, wobbly hind legs, drooling, or pawing at the mouth. Severe episodes become collapse or a seizure. The spells often follow exercise or a long gap without food. Call, and if your ferret is collapsed or having a seizure, call before you drive.

For many ferrets, the plan is medical management and frequent meals. Surgery is considered for selected ferrets once the low blood sugar is confirmed and the rest of the health picture is clear. A portable glucometer is tempting because it's fast, but a study Dr. Kopp published in the American Journal of Veterinary Research found those meters don't reliably place a ferret on the correct side of the low blood sugar cutoff. So before we make a diagnosis or change the plan, we confirm a low or borderline reading on a laboratory analyzer. Veterinarians can read the write-up of that study.

Adrenal disease

Hair loss often starts at the tail and moves over the hips and back, and itching is common. Females may have a swollen vulva, and some ferrets get a stronger odor or start mating behavior again. Males need a closer look, because an enlarged prostate can block the urethra. A ferret that strains and passes little or no urine is an emergency, so call (615) 361-4646 before you drive.

Adrenal disease can be treated with hormones, including hormone implants. We keep deslorelin implants in house, and leuprolide is another option. For selected ferrets, surgery to remove an affected adrenal gland is done here at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital. Either way, long-term monitoring continues.

  • Insulinoma and hypoglycemia
  • Medical management of insulinoma, and surgical evaluation for selected ferrets
  • Adrenal-associated endocrinopathy
  • Adrenal alopecia, itch, and vulvar enlargement
  • Prostatic enlargement and urinary obstruction
  • Hormone implants and other hormonal treatment for adrenal disease
  • Surgery for selected adrenal disease
  • Long-term monitoring of endocrine disease

Ferret GI disease and foreign bodies

Rubber toys, foam, earplugs, and anything soft and chewy are the usual foreign bodies, especially in ferrets under a couple of years old. Eating less, grinding teeth, vomiting, or a black, tarry stool can mean a blockage, and rubber often hides on radiographs. Surgery is the usual answer when something is stuck. Chronic diarrhea, weight loss, and ulcers have a longer list of causes, including Helicobacter and inflammatory disease. Those ferrets need a workup, and it may take more than one visit. Ferret-proofing and swallowed objects covers what to look for at home.

  • Foreign bodies and gastrointestinal obstruction
  • Gastritis and ulceration
  • Chronic diarrhea and inflammatory gastrointestinal disease
  • Helicobacter
  • Gastrointestinal neoplasia
  • Anorexia and weight loss
  • Gastrointestinal surgery

Ferret cancer care

We see lymphoma, mast cell tumors, skin tumors, splenic masses, and tumors of the gut, mammary tissue, or reproductive tract in ferrets, and Dr. Kopp is trained to treat them. (Insulinoma and adrenal tumors are endocrine cancers, covered above.) The first step is finding out what the mass is and how far it has spread. If it's cancer, we'll go over all the options, including surgery, chemotherapy, and comfort care, and build a plan around your pet and your family's goals. When radiation therapy is indicated, we arrange it through a referral facility.

  • Lymphoma, mast cell tumors, and cutaneous tumors
  • Insulinoma and adrenal tumors
  • Splenic masses, gastrointestinal neoplasia, and mammary or reproductive neoplasia
  • Cytology, biopsy, and staging
  • Surgical removal of selected tumors
  • Comfort care and cancer pain control
  • Palliative care and cancer pain control
  • Radiation therapy through a referral facility when indicated

Ferret infectious disease and prevention

Distemper and rabies vaccines are part of prevention when they're appropriate for your ferret, and if a ferret has a vaccine reaction, we'll assess it. We'll also talk about heartworm prevention for ferrets that need it. Call if your ferret has respiratory signs, especially if someone at home has the flu. Diarrhea that lingers, or a ferret that's losing weight with vague illness, can be coronavirus disease and needs an exam.

  • Canine distemper vaccination and rabies vaccination
  • Vaccine reaction assessment
  • Heartworm prevention
  • Influenza
  • Ferret systemic coronavirus disease and enteric coronavirus
  • Bacterial and respiratory infections
  • Dermatophytosis and parasites

Ferret dental disease

Broken teeth, tartar, and sore gums turn up on wellness exams, or when a ferret starts eating on one side. We take skull and dental radiographs here in the hospital, and dental care is planned around ferret anesthesia and pain control. Call if the face is swollen or your ferret stops eating.

  • Dental and periodontal disease
  • Tooth fractures
  • Oral exams under sedation when an awake look is not enough

Ferret urinary and kidney disease

The emergency in male ferrets is urinary blockage from an enlarged prostate, usually tied to adrenal disease. Call before you drive if he strains and produces little or no urine. Always call first at (615) 361-4646. Kidney disease is a separate problem and is worked up with bloodwork and urine testing when the signs fit.

  • Urinary obstruction from prostatic enlargement
  • Kidney disease
  • Urine testing and imaging when indicated

Ferret heart and respiratory disease

Coughing, tiring during play, a fast breathing rate at rest, or a swollen belly can point to heart or lung disease, and Dr. Kopp is trained to treat both. We can arrange abdominal ultrasound read by board-certified veterinary radiologists, and an echocardiogram when a ferret needs one.

  • Cardiomyopathy and congestive heart failure
  • Respiratory infections
  • Echocardiography by appropriate arrangement
  • Radiographs, and abdominal ultrasound arranged with interpretation by board-certified veterinary radiologists
  • Echocardiography arranged when needed

Ferret anesthesia and pain management

Every ferret under anesthesia has a dedicated anesthesia technician watching ECG, oxygen levels, carbon dioxide, blood pressure, and temperature. Afterward, we focus on warmth, pain control, and getting food back in as soon as it's safe. Pain isn't only a surgery issue, either. An older ferret that has slowed down may be hurting from arthritis or cancer, and Dr. Kopp is also a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). If a procedure is already on the calendar, the note on preparing an exotic pet for surgery is a useful read, and chronic pain in older small pets covers ferrets too.

  • Anesthetic planning for young, old, and medically complicated ferrets
  • Sedation for diagnostics and imaging
  • Full monitoring: ECG, SpO2, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature, with dedicated anesthesia technicians
  • Multimodal pain control, local blocks when they fit the procedure, and constant-rate infusions when indicated
  • Osteoarthritis, cancer pain, and postoperative pain
  • Palliative pain care and long-term monitoring

Other ferret conditions

Hair loss has several causes. Adrenal disease is common, and skin infection, parasites, and other skin disease are evaluated when the pattern fits. An enlarged spleen is common in ferrets and still needs a reason if the ferret is sick. Anemia, neurologic signs, and geriatric decline are worked up with an exam, selected tests, and a plan you can carry out at home.

  • Anemia
  • Skin disease, alopecia, and pruritus

  • Nutrition counseling and geriatric care
