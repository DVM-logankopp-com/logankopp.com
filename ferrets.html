Care for pet ferrets

Ferrets get a short list of diseases that show up in middle age and later. A thinning tail, an itchy back, a swollen vulva, or a few seconds of staring into space can be the start of adrenal disease or insulinoma. A young ferret that vomits, or that suddenly sleeps through playtime, may have swallowed a piece of rubber or foam.

Dr. Kopp is an exotic companion mammal specialist. He is experienced in treating ferret endocrine disease, and he sees juvenile, adult, and geriatric ferrets at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch, including referral cases. The plan is chosen for the individual ferret. Evidence for many treatments in this species is limited, so the options are discussed with you first.

Endocrine disease: insulinoma and adrenal disease

These two diseases are the reason many ferrets in Nashville and Middle Tennessee are referred. Some ferrets develop both. A yearly exam is a practical time to catch hair loss, a change in weight, or a spell the family almost dismissed. The family guide to adrenal disease and insulinoma is worth reading before that visit.

Insulinoma

Insulinoma is a pancreatic tumor that releases too much insulin and drops the blood sugar. At home it looks like staring, a spaced-out moment, wobbly hind legs, drooling, or pawing at the mouth. Severe episodes become collapse or a seizure. The spells often follow exercise or a long gap without food. Call, and if your ferret is collapsed or having a seizure, call before you drive.

Medical management and frequent feeding are the plan for many ferrets. Surgery is evaluated for selected ferrets after the low blood sugar is confirmed and the rest of the health picture is clear. A portable glucometer is tempting because it is fast. A study Dr. Kopp published in the American Journal of Veterinary Research found that those meters do not reliably place a ferret on the correct side of the low blood sugar cutoff. A low or borderline reading is confirmed on a laboratory analyzer before a diagnosis or a change in plan. The write-up of that study is written for veterinarians.

Adrenal disease

Hair loss often starts at the tail and moves over the hips and back. Itch is common. Females may show a swollen vulva, and some ferrets get a stronger odor or a return of mating behavior. Males need a closer look: an enlarged prostate can block the urethra. A ferret that strains and passes little or no urine should be seen the same day, while the hospital is open. That is an emergency.

A deslorelin implant can quiet the signs and has to be repeated over time. Leuprolide is another option when it is appropriate. For selected ferrets, surgery to remove an affected adrenal gland is performed at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital. Long-term monitoring continues either way. Drug choice and any procedure are discussed for that ferret. Doses are not listed here.

  • Insulinoma and hypoglycemia
  • Medical management of insulinoma, and surgical evaluation for selected ferrets
  • Adrenal-associated endocrinopathy
  • Adrenal alopecia, itch, and vulvar enlargement
  • Prostatic enlargement and urinary obstruction
  • Deslorelin implant, and leuprolide when appropriate
  • Surgery for selected adrenal disease
  • Long-term monitoring of endocrine disease

Emergency and critical care

Collapse, a seizure, inability to urinate, or a ferret that has stopped eating and is vomiting needs a call before you drive. Emergency care depends on hospital hours and staffing. The hospital is open Monday through Friday, 7:30 AM to 7:00 PM. Stabilization can include glucose support, fluids, warmth, oxygen, and pain control, and surgery when a blockage or another emergency needs an operation and the team can do it that day.

  • Triage and stabilization of weak, collapsed, or seizuring ferrets
  • Hypoglycemia support
  • Urinary obstruction
  • Fluid support, temperature support, and oxygen when indicated
  • Severe pain and trauma
  • Emergency surgery when hours and staffing allow it
  • Postoperative monitoring and assisted feeding

Gastrointestinal disease

Rubber toys, foam, earplugs, and anything soft and chewy are the usual foreign bodies, especially in ferrets under a couple of years old. Eating less, grinding teeth, vomiting, or a black, tarry stool can mean a blockage, and rubber often hides on radiographs. Surgery is the usual answer when something is stuck. Chronic diarrhea, weight loss, and ulcers have a longer list of causes, including Helicobacter and inflammatory disease. Those ferrets need a workup, and it may take more than one visit. Ferret-proofing and swallowed objects covers what to look for at home.

  • Foreign bodies and gastrointestinal obstruction
  • Gastritis and ulceration
  • Chronic diarrhea and inflammatory gastrointestinal disease
  • Helicobacter
  • Gastrointestinal neoplasia
  • Anorexia and weight loss
  • Gastrointestinal surgery

Cancer care

Lymphoma, mast cell tumors, skin tumors, splenic masses, and tumors of the gut, mammary tissue, or reproductive tract are all seen in ferrets. Insulinoma and adrenal tumors are endocrine cancers and are covered above. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating these diseases. The visit starts with what the mass is and how far it has spread. Surgery is used for selected tumors. Selected chemotherapy is planned when it is appropriate. Not every chemotherapy drug is used at the hospital. Radiation therapy is arranged through a referral facility when it is indicated.

  • Lymphoma, mast cell tumors, and cutaneous tumors
  • Insulinoma and adrenal tumors
  • Splenic masses, gastrointestinal neoplasia, and mammary or reproductive neoplasia
  • Cytology, biopsy, and staging
  • Surgical removal of selected tumors
  • Cancer consultation, chemotherapy planning, and selected systemic chemotherapy when appropriate
  • Palliative care and cancer pain control
  • Radiation therapy through a referral facility when indicated

Infectious disease and prevention

Distemper and rabies vaccines are part of ferret prevention when they are appropriate for that animal. Vaccine reactions are assessed if they occur. Heartworm prevention is discussed for ferrets that need it. Call if your ferret has respiratory signs, especially if people in the home have influenza. Diarrhea that lasts, or a ferret that is losing weight with vague illness, can be coronavirus disease and needs an exam.

  • Canine distemper vaccination and rabies vaccination
  • Vaccine reaction assessment
  • Heartworm prevention
  • Influenza
  • Ferret systemic coronavirus disease and enteric coronavirus
  • Bacterial and respiratory infections
  • Dermatophytosis and parasites

Dental disease

Broken teeth, tartar, and sore gums show up on wellness exams and when a ferret is eating on one side. Dental care is planned around ferret anesthesia and pain control. Call if the face is swollen or the ferret stops eating.

  • Dental and periodontal disease
  • Tooth fractures
  • Oral exams under sedation when an awake look is not enough

Urinary and kidney disease

The emergency in male ferrets is urinary blockage from an enlarged prostate, usually tied to adrenal disease. Call the same day if he strains and produces little or no urine. Kidney disease is a separate problem and is worked up with bloodwork and urine testing when the signs fit.

  • Urinary obstruction from prostatic enlargement
  • Kidney disease
  • Urine testing and imaging when indicated

Heart and respiratory disease

Coughing, tiring during play, a fast breathing rate at rest, or a swollen belly can be heart disease or lung disease. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating these problems. Echocardiography is arranged when a ferret needs a heart ultrasound.

  • Cardiomyopathy and congestive heart failure
  • Respiratory infections
  • Echocardiography by appropriate arrangement
  • Radiographs, and ultrasound when it answers the question

Soft-tissue surgery

Adrenal surgery and foreign-body surgery are the operations families ask about most. Mass removal, biopsy, spay and neuter, and exploratory surgery are also performed when they fit the ferret. Selected abdominal organ procedures are considered when indicated. Complex bone surgery is arranged with a referral facility when the ferret needs it.

  • Surgery for selected adrenal disease
  • Gastrointestinal foreign body surgery
  • Spay, neuter, and cryptorchid surgery
  • Mass removal, biopsy, and abscess surgery
  • Exploratory laparotomy and selected abdominal organ procedures when indicated
  • Wound closure and selected reconstructive closure
  • Amputation when indicated

Anesthesia and pain management

Anesthesia is planned for a ferret, with monitoring, warmth, and pain control, and with food again as soon as it is safe. Older ferrets that have slowed down may also be painful from arthritis or cancer. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. If a procedure is already on the calendar, the note on preparing an exotic pet for surgery is a useful read. Chronic pain in older small pets includes ferrets.

  • Anesthetic planning for young, old, and medically complicated ferrets
  • Sedation for diagnostics and imaging
  • Monitoring during anesthesia, including ECG, oxygen saturation, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature
  • Multimodal pain control, local blocks when they fit the procedure, and constant-rate infusions when indicated
  • Osteoarthritis, cancer pain, and postoperative pain
  • Palliative pain care and long-term monitoring

Other conditions

Hair loss has several causes. Adrenal disease is common, and skin infection, parasites, and other skin disease are evaluated when the pattern fits. An enlarged spleen is common in ferrets and still needs a reason if the ferret is sick. Anemia, neurologic signs, and geriatric decline are worked up with an exam, selected tests, and a plan you can carry out at home.

  • Splenomegaly
  • Anemia
  • Skin disease, alopecia, and pruritus
  • Neurologic disease
  • Nutrition counseling and geriatric care
  • End of life care

What does ferret adrenal disease look like?

Hair loss often starts on the tail and spreads over the back. Many ferrets itch. A spayed female may develop a swollen vulva. A male who strains and passes little or no urine needs to be seen right away.

What if my ferret stares into space or seems weak?

Those spells can be insulinoma, a pancreatic tumor that drops blood sugar. Signs often come and go, especially after play or a long stretch without food. Call, and if your ferret is collapsed or having a seizure, call before you drive.

What if my ferret swallows something?

Rubber, foam, and soft toys are common. A ferret who stops eating, vomits, grinds their teeth, or seems suddenly quiet may have a blockage. Call the same day. Young ferrets do this most often.
