Chinchilla veterinary care in Nashville

A chinchilla's teeth grow throughout life, and its coat is built for cold, dry air, so it's no surprise that dental disease and heat stress are two of the problems that bring chinchillas to a veterinarian in Nashville. Dr. Kopp is an exotic companion mammal specialist and sees chinchillas for primary care and referral cases at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch.

Not every chinchilla treatment is well studied, so we make decisions together. Please don't give a leftover antibiotic from another pet, because some of those drugs are unsafe in chinchillas. For home care, see chinchilla dental care and heat stress.

Chinchilla dental disease and oral surgery

A wet chin, drooling, dropped food, smaller droppings, or weight loss can all be dental disease, and by the time it shows at home, it's often far along. Watery eyes and a firm swelling along the jaw are reasons to call too. Cheek teeth and tooth roots usually need an exam under anesthesia, and we take skull and dental radiographs here in the hospital. When the roots or the bone need more detail, we arrange CT with Nashville Veterinary Specialists, and MRI is available through a referral facility. Abdominal ultrasound, read by board-certified veterinary radiologists, can also be arranged.

  • Acquired dental disease and elongated crowns
  • Malocclusion and tooth root disease
  • Odontogenic abscesses
  • Chronic anorexia related to the teeth
  • Skull and dental radiographs, and dental surgery, including extraction when a tooth is diseased
  • Long-term dental monitoring, nutrition, and dental pain control

Chinchilla GI stasis and gut disease

Call while the hospital is open if your chinchilla stops eating, has fewer or smaller droppings, or has a tight, painful belly. We see hypomotility, stasis, bloat, constipation, diarrhea, and enteritis, and assisted feeding and pain control are often part of the first visit. If your chinchilla is collapsed, or bloated and painful, call (615) 361-4646 before you drive.

  • Hypomotility and gastrointestinal stasis
  • Bloat and constipation
  • Diarrhea and enteritis
  • Chronic anorexia
  • Nutrition plans and assisted feeding

Chinchilla heat stress and emergency care

Chinchillas don't shed heat well, and a warm, humid room in a Middle Tennessee summer can bring on heat stress fast. If your chinchilla is sprawled out, weak, or breathing with its mouth open, move the carrier to a cool room and call us for instructions before you drive. Don't wet the coat. During hospital hours, we can usually accept urgent transfers from primary care veterinarians and may be able to see emergency cases, so always call first at (615) 361-4646.

  • Heat stress and hyperthermia
  • Triage and stabilization
  • Fluid support, cooling when it is safe, and temperature monitoring
  • Oxygen and pain control when indicated
  • Emergency surgery if we can take the case

Chinchilla fur, skin, and feet

A patch of fur that comes out in the hand is fur slip, often from a hard grab or a scare. Fur chewing and bald spots need an exam, because the cause can be husbandry, dental pain, or skin disease. Ringworm can pass to people. Sore feet need a look at flooring, weight, and infection.

  • Fur slip and fur chewing
  • Dermatophytosis
  • Pododermatitis
  • Skin scrapings and fungal testing when indicated

Chinchilla urinary and reproductive disease

Straining, blood in the urine, or a chinchilla that stops eating can be urinary disease, including stones. If a female is in labor and not progressing, call (615) 361-4646 while the hospital is open. Reproductive surgery is considered for selected cases.

  • Urinary disease and urolithiasis
  • Reproductive disease and dystocia
  • Imaging and urine testing when indicated
  • Surgery for selected urinary or reproductive disease

Chinchilla surgery, anesthesia, and pain

We do dental surgery, mass removal, and other soft-tissue surgery when it's right for the chinchilla. During anesthesia, a dedicated anesthesia technician monitors ECG, oxygen levels, carbon dioxide, blood pressure, and temperature, and we keep the focus on warmth and pain control. Dr. Kopp is also a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM), and he has published on an anesthesia approach for chinchilla surgery (veterinarians can read the note on chinchilla anesthesia). Some fractures can be repaired in partnership with board-certified veterinary surgeons (DACVS), and others are referred.

  • Dental surgery and mass removal
  • Soft-tissue surgery and wound care
  • Anesthetic planning, monitoring, and pain control
  • Geriatric care and nutrition counseling

Other chinchilla conditions

We evaluate eye and respiratory disease at the visit, and Dr. Kopp is trained to treat the eye and respiratory problems that can be managed medically. If a chinchilla needs advanced eye surgery, we refer it. An older chinchilla that's losing weight needs a dental exam and a look beyond the teeth.

  • Eye disease
  • Respiratory disease
  • Geriatric care

What a visit is like

Bring the chinchilla in a secure carrier, out of direct sun, and travel during the cooler part of the day in summer. A little of the hay they already eat is useful. Tell the hospital if the house was hot, if droppings changed, or if weight has dropped.

When to call

Call while the hospital is open if your chinchilla:

  • Has stopped eating, or the droppings are smaller or fewer
  • Is drooling, has a wet chin, or has a swelling along the jaw
  • Is weak, sprawled out, or breathing with the mouth open
  • Is straining to urinate or to give birth
  • Has fur loss, sores on the feet, or a patch of fur that pulled out
