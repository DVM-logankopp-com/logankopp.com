Chinchilla veterinary care in Nashville

Chinchilla teeth grow throughout life, and the coat is built for cold, dry air. Dental disease and heat stress are two of the problems that bring chinchillas to a veterinarian in Nashville. Dr. Kopp is an exotic companion mammal specialist. He sees chinchillas for primary care and for referral cases at Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital in Antioch.

Chinchilla treatments are not all well studied, so we decide together. Do not give a leftover antibiotic from another pet. Some of those drugs are unsafe in chinchillas. Home care is covered in chinchilla dental care and heat stress.

Chinchilla dental disease and oral surgery

A wet chin, drooling, dropped food, smaller droppings, or weight loss can be dental disease, and it is often far along by the time it shows at home. Watery eyes and a firm swelling along the jaw are reasons to call. Cheek teeth and tooth roots usually need an exam under anesthesia. Skull and dental radiographs are taken in the hospital for dental evaluation. Abdominal ultrasound can be arranged and is interpreted by board-certified veterinary radiologists. CT is arranged with Nashville Veterinary Specialists when the roots or the bone need more detail, and MRI is arranged through a referral facility.

  • Acquired dental disease and elongated crowns
  • Malocclusion and tooth root disease
  • Odontogenic abscesses
  • Chronic anorexia related to the teeth
  • Skull and dental radiographs, and dental surgery, including extraction when a tooth is diseased
  • Long-term dental monitoring, nutrition, and dental pain control

Chinchilla GI stasis and gut disease

Call while the hospital is open if your chinchilla stops eating, produces fewer or smaller droppings, or has a tight, painful belly. Hypomotility, stasis, bloat, constipation, diarrhea, and enteritis are all seen. Assisted feeding and pain control are often part of the first visit. A chinchilla that is collapsed or bloated and painful needs a call before you drive. Always call first at (615) 361-4646.

  • Hypomotility and gastrointestinal stasis
  • Bloat and constipation
  • Diarrhea and enteritis
  • Chronic anorexia
  • Nutrition plans and assisted feeding

Chinchilla heat stress and emergency care

Chinchillas do not shed heat well. A warm, humid room in a Middle Tennessee summer can cause heat stress quickly. A chinchilla that is sprawled out, weak, or breathing with the mouth open needs a call before you drive. Move the carrier to a cool room and call for instructions. Do not wet the coat. During hospital hours, Priest Lake Veterinary Hospital can usually accept urgent transfers from primary care veterinarians and may be able to see emergency cases. Always call first at (615) 361-4646.

  • Heat stress and hyperthermia
  • Triage and stabilization
  • Fluid support, cooling when it is safe, and temperature monitoring
  • Oxygen and pain control when indicated
  • Emergency surgery if we can take the case

Chinchilla fur, skin, and feet

A patch of fur that comes out in the hand is fur slip, often from a hard grab or a scare. Fur chewing and bald spots need an exam, because the cause can be husbandry, dental pain, or skin disease. Ringworm can pass to people. Sore feet need a look at flooring, weight, and infection.

  • Fur slip and fur chewing
  • Dermatophytosis
  • Pododermatitis
  • Skin scrapings and fungal testing when indicated

Chinchilla urinary and reproductive disease

Straining, blood in the urine, or a chinchilla that stops eating can be urinary disease, including stones. A female that is in labor and not progressing needs a call while the hospital is open. Always call first at (615) 361-4646. Reproductive surgery is considered for selected cases.

  • Urinary disease and urolithiasis
  • Reproductive disease and dystocia
  • Imaging and urine testing when indicated
  • Surgery for selected urinary or reproductive disease

Chinchilla surgery, anesthesia, and pain

Dental surgery, mass removal, and other soft-tissue surgery are performed when they fit the chinchilla. The hospital provides high-quality anesthesia, with dedicated anesthesia technicians and full monitoring (ECG, SpO2, capnography, blood pressure, and temperature), plus warmth and pain control. Dr. Kopp is a Fellow in Companion Animal Pain Management (FABVP-CAPM). That fellowship is separate from his board certification in Exotic Companion Mammal Practice. He has published on an anesthesia approach for chinchilla surgery. That note is for veterinarians: chinchilla anesthesia. Some fractures can be repaired in partnership with board-certified veterinary surgeons (DACVS). Others are referred.

  • Dental surgery and mass removal
  • Soft-tissue surgery and wound care
  • Anesthetic planning, monitoring, and pain control
  • Geriatric care and nutrition counseling

Other chinchilla conditions

Eye disease and respiratory disease are evaluated at the visit. An older chinchilla that is losing weight needs a dental exam and a look beyond the teeth. Dr. Kopp is trained in treating eye and respiratory problems that are managed medically. Advanced eye surgery is referred when a chinchilla needs it.

  • Eye disease
  • Respiratory disease
  • Geriatric care

What a visit is like

Bring the chinchilla in a secure carrier, out of direct sun, and travel during the cooler part of the day in summer. A little of the hay they already eat is useful. Tell the hospital if the house was hot, if droppings changed, or if weight has dropped.

When to call

Call while the hospital is open if your chinchilla:

  • Has stopped eating, or the droppings are smaller or fewer
  • Is drooling, has a wet chin, or has a swelling along the jaw
  • Is weak, sprawled out, or breathing with the mouth open
  • Is straining to urinate or to give birth
  • Has fur loss, sores on the feet, or a patch of fur that pulled out
